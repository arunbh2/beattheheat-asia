    Who is protected? A separate, illustrative panel

    Illustrative Three editorial pathways for one protection programme, such as cooling spaces with check-ins or paid heat breaks, reaching one group you have in mind, such as outdoor workers in a city. They are not predictions of any government’s performance, and changing them does not change the climate panel.

    Pathway
    When funding and a decision are in place.
    Hiring, building, procurement.
    Percentage points of the group newly reached each year.
    Design limits: places it covers, who is eligible.
    Share of hot periods when it actually works: power, staff, supplies.
    Share of those reached who can use it: cost, distance, pay lost, access.
    Usable protection by 203515%
    Usable protection by 205034%
    Years without usable protection, 2026–205020.1 of 25

    The formula: nominal reach in year y = the smaller of the ceiling and pace × (y − start − lead + 1), and zero before start + lead. Usable protection = nominal reach × reliability × ability to use. Years without usable protection = the sum, over 2026 to 2050, of (1 − usable share): the average, per person in the group, of years without it. One programme and one group, so nobody is counted twice. Coverage only: not illness, deaths or costs.

    What changes if implementation starts later?

    Starting in 2035 instead of 2028, with the same lead time, pace, ceiling, reliability and usability, the defined group would spend about 2.4 more years each, on average, without usable protection by 2050 (a coverage gap, not a measure of exposure, illness or deaths). Nominal reach by 2050: 70% with the earlier start, 70% with the later one. To reach 70% by 2050 anyway, the later programme would need to add about 5.0 points a year instead of 5: more delivery capacity, not a catch-up that happens by itself.

    What could these conditions mean for daily life?

    Illustrative Situations, not interviews. Gender, age, disability, migration status, tenure and informal work interact; no group shares one level of risk.

    A street vendor balancing heat exposure and daily earnings

    What the climate indicator tells us
    More hot days mean more days when midday trade means midday heat.
    What it misses
    Sun on the pitch, heat from the road, hours without shade.
    What protection would need
    Shaded pitches, water, and income support so a day off is possible.
    Who can enable it
    City governments (vending rules, markets), social protection agencies.
    Who pays and maintains it
    Usually the city, for shade and water; income support from national schemes.
    Who could still be left out
    Vendors without a licence or a fixed pitch.

    A garment worker facing indoor heat and production demands

    What the climate indicator tells us
    Outdoor heat raises the starting point for indoor heat; it does not measure the factory.
    What it misses
    Roof and machine heat, ventilation, targets, shift length.
    What protection would need
    Cooling and ventilation, paid rest, targets that change on hot days.
    Who can enable it
    Employers, buyers, labour inspectors, worker organisations.
    Who pays and maintains it
    Employers, sometimes with buyers; inspections by the state.
    Who could still be left out
    Piece-rate, temporary and home-based workers.

    A caregiver in a rented home that holds heat overnight

    What the climate indicator tells us
    More hot nights mean fewer nights of recovery.
    What it misses
    Roof material, ventilation, crowding, the cost of running a fan or cooler.
    What protection would need
    Cooler roofs and homes, affordable power, care services that stay open.
    Who can enable it
    Housing authorities and landlords, utilities, care services.
    Who pays and maintains it
    Landlords or tenants, with housing programmes; utilities for supply.
    Who could still be left out
    Tenants who cannot change the building, and households behind on bills.

    An older person dependent on services and reliable electricity

    What the climate indicator tells us
    Hotter days and nights raise strain, which this indicator cannot measure.
    What it misses
    Health conditions, medicines, mobility, being alone.
    What protection would need
    Check-ins, cooling spaces within reach, power that stays on, clinics ready for heat.
    Who can enable it
    Health and social services, utilities, neighbourhood groups.
    Who pays and maintains it
    Public health budgets and local services.
    Who could still be left out
    People living alone, with limited mobility, or without a phone.

    An agricultural worker whose hours are limited by heat

    What the climate indicator tells us
    More hot days shrink the cooler hours in which field work is possible.
    What it misses
    Sun, humidity, effort, distance to shade and water.
    What protection would need
    Changed hours, shade and water in fields, pay that does not punish rest.
    Who can enable it
    Employers and landowners, agricultural and labour departments.
    Who pays and maintains it
    Employers, with public extension services.
    Who could still be left out
    Seasonal and migrant workers, and unpaid family labour.

    Connected consequences

    Explanatory pathways from the systems map, each with its evidence. They are not calculated by this dashboard.

    • The sleepless-nights loop established evidenceHot nights wreck sleep. Poor sleep frays mental health, and stress makes sleep harder still.
    • The poverty–heat trap established evidencePeople who can’t afford to stop working fall ill. Illness costs income, and lost income makes stopping even less affordable.
    • The energy-poverty trap emerging evidencePeople who can’t afford to cool fall ill. Illness brings debt, and debt puts cooling even further out of reach.
    • The overwhelmed-clinic loop emerging evidenceHeat illness floods clinics. Overwhelmed clinics treat people later, and more cases become severe.

    What can change this trajectory?

    Adaptation can reduce consequences but does not remove every risk, and local protection does not replace cutting emissions. Each action links to its examples, sources and side effects.

    Emissions reduction

    Changes how much heat comes; local action does not replace it.

    Action
    Deep emission cuts
    Intended benefit
    Every fraction of a degree avoided weakens every loop that runs through land, ice and forests at once. It also keeps more places within the limits to adaptation, where protecting people still works.
    Who acts
    national government, international partners, funders
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions

    Reduced local exposure

    Shade and cooler surfaces lower the heat people meet.

    Action
    Green, cool cities
    Intended benefit
    Trees, water, cool roofs and cool pavements lower city temperatures and flatten peak demand.
    Who acts
    city government, communities
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions
    Action
    Cool by design
    Intended benefit
    Shading, ventilation, reflective roofs and efficient cooling cut the need for air conditioning, and the waste heat it dumps outside.
    Who acts
    city government, national government
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions

    Better buildings and public spaces

    Cooler homes help nights give recovery.

    Action
    Cool, affordable housing
    Intended benefit
    Upgrading informal settlements with ventilation, shade and cool roofs, and planning homes for people who arrive.
    Who acts
    city government, funders, communities
    Watch for
    In 16 Ahmedabad homes compared over one week, most roof treatments lowered daytime indoor heat, but one coating left rooms hotter than untreated roofs. Designs differ, and night-time effects were not measured.
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions

    Work and income protection

    Makes stopping or slowing work possible without losing pay.

    Action
    Heat-safe work
    Intended benefit
    Rest, shade and water during dangerous heat, with paid breaks so that stopping work doesn’t mean losing pay.
    Who acts
    national government, employers, workers
    Watch for
    Protections work only when workers can use them. In Hong Kong, some outdoor cleaners given cooling vests were stopped from wearing them because the vests covered a company logo; in Cambodia, researchers found workers whose unions had bargained over heat exposure better protected, as Oxfam America reports.
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions
    Action
    Income protection in heatwaves
    Intended benefit
    Cash that pays out automatically when temperatures cross a danger threshold, so people can afford to stop. Triggers need careful design to limit basis risk: heat that hurts people without triggering a payout.
    Who acts
    insurers, national government, workers
    Watch for
    A payout trigger set too high can pay nothing: the first year of a heat insurance scheme for informal women workers in India paid nothing for that reason, until the threshold was redesigned.
    Watch for
    Payouts that take weeks to arrive weaken the link between the heat and the help.
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions

    Care and health-service continuity

    Keeps help open and reachable during heat.

    Action
    Heat-ready health services
    Intended benefit
    Cooling, backup power, surge plans and trained frontline workers keep clinics working through heatwaves.
    Who acts
    health services, funders
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions
    Action
    Cooling centres and check-ins
    Intended benefit
    Public cooling spaces, water points and check-ins on older people living alone can offer relief in a heatwave. How much they protect health depends on who can reach them, how long people stay, and how hot it is once they leave.
    Who acts
    city government, communities, health services
    Watch for
    A 2025 review found no study that measured health outcomes for people using cooling centres; in simulated heatwaves, the benefits were short-lived. That is not evidence of no benefit.
    Watch for
    Running costs can decide whether a cooling space keeps working. Bangkok reported that money to build is generally available but running costs are not always covered; consultation participants said some childcare centres held back from air conditioning over electricity costs.
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions

    Reliable utilities

    Cooling and water only help when power holds.

    Action
    Affordable cooling for the poorest
    Intended benefit
    Lifeline electricity tariffs, cool roofs and cooling suited to the local heat bring safer cooling within reach of households that cannot afford it. Which kind helps depends on the climate and the person: a fan that helps in humid heat may not help in very hot, dry air.
    Who acts
    utilities, city government, national government
    Watch for
    In laboratory trials with older adults, fans slightly cooled the body in hot, humid air but raised body temperature in very hot, dry air. These were short trials, not homes.
    Evidence
    Examples and sources on Actions
    Action
    Backup power for water
    Intended benefit
    Solar pumps and backup supply keep water flowing when the grid fails.
    Who acts
    water utilities, utilities

    What remains uncertain

    Climate uncertainty

    Models differ, natural variability can make a decade hotter or cooler than its trend, and the emissions path matters more the further ahead you look. The whiskers show the spread across models; a 20-year average is not a forecast for any single year.

    Impact uncertainty

    Outdoor air over a 28 km cell is not the heat inside a home, a factory or a market, and it is not a person’s exposure, health or income. This page estimates none of those.

    Implementation uncertainty

    Whether a programme is funded, built, staffed, maintained and usable is decided by people and institutions. The protection panel makes those assumptions visible; its band is an assumption range, not a probability.

    Sources and method

    Register of the projected series
    IndicatorsHot days (days a year): Days a year with a daily maximum temperature of 35 °C or more (the dataset's file says “35 degC and higher”). A fixed meteorological threshold, not a health limit.
    Hot nights (nights a year): Nights a year with a daily minimum temperature above 26 °C. A fixed meteorological threshold, not a health limit.
    Hottest day of the year (°C): The highest daily maximum temperature in a year, averaged over the 20-year period. A statistic of the hottest day, not a forecast for any year.
    GeographyOne 0.25° grid cell per city (about 28 km across), the cell nearest the city centre point the site uses; not the city boundary, the metropolitan area or a neighbourhood. Mumbai: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 19°N, 73°E, 15.4 km from the city centre point; Delhi: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 28.5°N, 77.25°E, 12.8 km from the city centre point; Dhaka: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 23.75°N, 90.5°E, 11.3 km from the city centre point; Jakarta: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 6.25°S, 106.75°E, 11.9 km from the city centre point; Bangkok: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 13.75°N, 100.5°E, 0 km from the city centre point; Manila: the 0.25° grid cell centred at 14.5°N, 121°E, 11.3 km from the city centre point.
    Reference period1995–2014, from the models’ historical runs (modelled, not observed).
    Future periods20-year averages: 2020–2039 and 2040–2059. The dataset has no 2035 window; the mid-2030s sit inside 2020–2039. No values are interpolated between periods.
    ScenariosSSP1-2.6 (lower emissions), SSP2-4.5 (intermediate), SSP3-7.0 (higher), as named in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (Summary for Policymakers (opens in a new tab)). No probability is attached to any of them, and none is called “business as usual”.
    Models and processingThe multi-model ensemble of the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal’s CMIP6 collection, downscaled and bias-adjusted at 0.25° (collection documentation (opens in a new tab)). Values are the files’ own annual climatologies, read at one grid cell by extract.py.
    Uncertainty shownThe 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles across the models; not a probability range, and not natural year-to-year variability.
    Source and retrievalWorld Bank CCKP public bulk files, wbg-cckp (opens in a new tab), retrieved 2026-11-27.
    LimitationsOutdoor air at about 2 m over a 28 km cell; city heat islands, indoor heat and humidity are not in these indicators. Hot-day counts use a fixed 35 °C, which means little in a city where it is rarely reached (Jakarta, Mumbai) and is not a health threshold anywhere.
    Why there is no humid-heat projection here

    A humid-heat measure needs temperature and humidity from the same day. Combining separately averaged or separately maximised values would invent extremes, so this dashboard uses only measures computed by the data provider from daily values. For how heat and humidity combine, see The wet-bulb wall.

    Maintenance

    To refresh the climate values, run research/2026-11/heat-futures/extract.py (needs the netCDF4 Python package, a one-off step), rebuild, and run node tools/check-heat-futures.js. The protection pathways are editorial and live in heat-futures/futures-core.js.
