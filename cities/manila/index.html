    Heat type: Mostly humid heat. Dry heat and humid heat, explained.

    Heat action plan: Parts in place, no full plan found

    How the estimate is made

    Wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is the heat-stress index used in international and national standards for heat at work.

    The figure here is our estimate for the city as a whole, calculated from forecast air temperature and humidity with the Australian Bureau of Meteorology's simplified formula. That formula assumes fairly strong sun and light wind, and leaves out changes in sun and wind. In cloudy or windy weather, and at night or early in the morning, it is likely to read higher than a measured value.

    It is not a measurement and not an official warning. No heat-stress level is shown beside the estimate: published WBGT levels are for measured values.

    Researchers comparing methods recommend a fuller calculation for outdoor work, which also uses sun and wind; this page does not use it yet.

    Sources: Australian Bureau of Meteorology: Thermal comfort observations, about the approximation to the WBGT · Lemke and Kjellstrom, 2012: Calculating workplace WBGT from meteorological data, Industrial Health · Weather data by Open-Meteo.com

    A single station shows how one site has changed against its own past. Each count is measured against this station's own record, so it is not a comparison between cities, and the station may sit at an airport or in open ground, so streets nearby can be hotter. Warm nights at a station can also rise as the city grows around it, which is part of the city's own heat. Humidity is not included, so these figures show temperature alone; in humid cities the heat felt in the body can rise even where temperature changes little. Strong El Niño years, such as 2016 and 2023 to 2024, bring hotter years in much of the region, and warm years near the end of a record tilt a trend upward.

    Trend: Sen's slope with its 95% range, and a Mann-Kendall test: "a clear rise" where the test is under 5%, "leaning upward, not clear" between 5% and 20%, otherwise "no clear change". Index definitions follow the ETCCDI (TN90p, TX90p, TXx), counted against this city's whole record. A change at the station is checked by comparing its yearly mean maximum and minimum with the regional model: for daily maxima, a step of at least 0.5 °C (Pettitt's test under 1%) means hot days and the hottest day show no trend; for minima, the same step (test under 5%) withdraws warm nights only where it fits clearly better than a steady drift, because nights warm gradually as a city grows.     Vicious cycles the evidence ties to Manila

    The lost-schooling loop

    In April 2024, 5,288 schools in the Philippines moved to remote learning, affecting more than 3.6 million students. Source: AFP via Malay Mail, April 2024

    Children pulled out of school earn less as adults, and poorer families pull more children out of school. Follow it on the map.

    The cooling trap

    One of the dense, high-rise city cores where air-conditioner waste heat adds to the night. Source: Salamanca et al., Journal of Geophysical Research, 2014 (summary by Arizona State University)

    Air conditioning heats the street, and a hotter city needs even more air conditioning. Follow it on the map.

    The farm-debt loop

    Rice yields in the Philippines fell about 10% for each 1 °C rise in growing-season night temperature. Source: Peng et al., PNAS, 2004

    A failed harvest pushes farmers into debt. Debt leaves less for seed, water and fertiliser, so the next harvest fails more easily. Follow it on the map.

    Already at work here

    • PAGASA, Philippines: Issues heat index warnings with danger levels that local governments use to decide when to suspend classes.
    • Department of Education, Philippines: Lets school heads suspend in-person classes when the heat index is dangerous, and moved the school year back to a June start to keep classes out of the hottest months.
