Worked heat action plans from Asia

How places in Asia have turned heat risk into a plan: what happened, what they decided, what changed, and who leads each action.

Ahmedabad’s heat action plan

Ahmedabad, India · 2013 onwards

What happened: In May 2010 a heatwave peaking at 46.8 °C caused an estimated 1,344 excess deaths in the city, 43% more than in the same weeks of 2009 and 2011.

What they decided: In 2013 the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation, with the Indian Institute of Public Health Gandhinagar and NRDC, launched South Asia’s first heat action plan: forecasts from the India Meteorological Department feed colour-coded alerts, departments act together, the public is warned, and health staff are trained. From 2017 the plan added cool roofs.

What changed: An evaluation estimated about 1,190 deaths a year were avoided after the plan began, compared with before it. The model has since spread to many other Indian cities and states.

  • Heat action plans: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Colour-coded alerts issued from IMD forecasts, with inter-agency response
  • Heat-ready health services: led by Health authorities; paid by Not documented; Health staff trained to recognise and treat heat illness
  • Cool, affordable housing: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Cool roofs on homes in informal settlements and on public buildings

Sources: Azhar et al., PLoS ONE, 2014: the 2010 heatwave · NRDC: the plan’s components and cool roofs · Hess et al., 2018: deaths avoided

Thane’s ward-by-ward heat action plan

Thane, India · 2024 onwards

What happened: In Thane, a fast-growing coastal city next to Mumbai, humidity and warm nights make heat more dangerous than the thermometer suggests, and the risk differs sharply from one ward to the next.

What they decided: In March 2024 the Thane Municipal Corporation and CEEW published a heat action plan built on a ward-level risk index of felt heat, warm nights and social vulnerability. A Heatwave Task Force across departments, chaired by an Additional Municipal Commissioner, runs it with emergency procedures. Measures include early warnings, cool roofs and pavements, water stations, shade, and heat preparedness in schools and hospitals.

What changed: Its effect on illness and deaths has not yet been publicly evaluated. What it shows is a plan that names who acts and where the risk is highest, before the season starts.

  • Heat action plans: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Ward-level risk index updated each season, with task-force actions logged against it
  • Cooling centres and check-ins: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Water stations and shaded areas in the highest-risk wards
  • Cool, affordable housing: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Cool roofs and cool pavements installed in high-risk wards
  • Heat-ready health services: led by Health authorities; paid by Not documented; Hospitals and schools with heat preparedness procedures

Sources: CEEW: How can Thane city tackle heatwave risks with a heat action plan · Global Heat Health Information Network: Heat Action Plan for Thane City 2024

Tamil Nadu’s state-wide heat network

Tamil Nadu, India · 2024 onwards

What happened: About 68% of Tamil Nadu’s people face 8–9 hours a day of strong heat stress from March to June, and 56% live where summer nights are warmer than the 1981–2011 average. The state’s 2019 heatwave action plan dealt mainly with short-term emergency response.

What they decided: In 2024 the State Planning Commission, with WRI India as knowledge partner and support from the British High Commission, convened a Heat Action Network of line departments, including disaster management, health, education, agriculture, environment and forests, and water. Together they wrote India’s first state-wide heat strategy, covering people, animals and ecosystems along three pathways: health and well-being, sustained resources and productivity, and cooling. It builds heat into schemes that already exist rather than starting new ones.

What changed: It is a strategy, not yet an evaluated programme. Departments are to take its recommendations to detailed project reports, with progress reviewed every 6–8 months. What it shows is a whole state, not one city, giving heat an owner across departments.

  • Heat action plans: led by National governments; paid by Not documented; Departments in the Heat Action Network reporting progress every 6–8 months
  • Heat-ready health services: led by Health authorities; paid by Not documented; Pregnancy-scheme check-ups that screen for heat risk (proposed)
  • Cool, affordable housing: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Slum redevelopment tenders that set a limit on heat gain through walls and roofs (proposed)
  • Green, cool cities: led by National governments; paid by Public budget; Forest and tree cover rising from 23.8% towards 33% by 2030–31

Sources: Tamil Nadu State Planning Commission, 2024: Beating the Heat · WRI India: mapping the path to heat resilience in Tamil Nadu · Tamil Nadu Pollution Control Board, G.O. 215, 2023: the tree cover target

Singapore’s heat rules for outdoor work

Singapore · 2023 onwards

What happened: In one of the world’s densest, most humid cities, the commercial centre has measured up to about 7 °C warmer than the outskirts, and builders, shipyard and landscape workers labour outdoors through it. A study of 355 workers found each hot day cost a median S$21 in lost income.

What they decided: In 2023 the National Environment Agency launched a national heat stress advisory based on wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which counts humidity, sun and wind, with low, moderate and high levels. The Ministry of Manpower made employers check WBGT hourly at outdoor work, give at least 10 minutes’ shaded rest each hour for heavy work at 32 °C or above, help new workers acclimatise, and enforced it.

What changed: In March and June 2024 the ministry inspected 250 workplaces and acted against 78, mainly for missing shaded rest breaks and acclimatisation. The rules have since been revised and strengthened. Their effect on heat illness has not yet been publicly evaluated.

  • Heat-safe work: led by National governments; paid by Employers; Outdoor worksites checking WBGT hourly and giving shaded rest breaks; workplaces inspected
  • Heat action plans: led by Weather and climate services; paid by Not documented; Heat stress advisory with three WBGT risk levels, shown by location
  • Green, cool cities: led by City and local governments; paid by Not documented; Districts modelled for cooling through Cooling Singapore

Sources: Ministry of Sustainability and the Environment: Heat Stress Advisory, 2023 · Ministry of Manpower: enhanced heat stress measures, 2023 · HRD Asia: 78 of 250 worksites non-compliant, 2024 · NUS Project HeatSafe, 2024 · Cooling Singapore

Dhaka acts before the heat peaks

Dhaka, Bangladesh · 2023 onwards

What happened: Bangladesh’s ‘feels-like’ temperature has risen by about 4.5 °C since 1980, and parts of Dhaka run more than 10 °C hotter than the countryside. In April 2024 a long heatwave closed schools for about 33 million children.

What they decided: In 2023 Dhaka North City Corporation appointed Asia’s first Chief Heat Officer, a post paid for by the Arsht-Rock foundation rather than the city, to bring heat planning together. The same year the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society’s heatwave early action protocol was approved: when forecasts pass 38 °C, warnings, drinking water, cooling stations and cash grants go out before the peak.

What changed: On 21 April 2024 the protocol was activated for Dhaka, with about CHF 500,000 from the IFRC’s emergency fund to reach some 124,000 people. Its effect on illness and deaths has not been publicly evaluated. What it shows is money and a plan agreed before the heat, released by a forecast.

  • Anticipatory action: led by Community and civil society groups; paid by Not documented; Days from a 38 °C forecast to cash, water and warnings reaching people
  • Cooling centres and check-ins: led by Community and civil society groups; paid by Not documented; Cooling stations open at three locations during the heatwave
  • Heat action plans: led by City and local governments; paid by Philanthropy; A Chief Heat Officer bringing the city’s heat planning together

Sources: Red Cross Red Crescent Climate Centre, 2024 · Arsht-Rock: Dhaka North’s Chief Heat Officer, 2023 · World Bank, 2025 · NBC News: school closures, 2024
