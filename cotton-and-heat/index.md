# Heat from field to factory

Cotton, informal work and the conditions that make protection possible.

A worker's exposure depends on the weather and the task. Their ability to protect themselves also depends on pay, water, rest, transport, care responsibilities and who controls the working day.

The question this page keeps asking

What would have to change for the person doing this work to stay safe without losing the income, security or support they depend on?

## Follow the cotton

Seven stages, from field to garment. The first four, where informal work is most common and evidence is thinnest, get the most depth; spinning, fabric and garments link to Heat inside factories for indoor controls.

How the chain works, in five lines

- Ginning separates cotton lint from seed; the seed is a coproduct with its own markets.
- Spinning turns fibre into yarn. Yarn becomes fabric through weaving or knitting.
- Dyeing and finishing can happen at several points: to fibre, yarn, fabric or finished garments.
- Production can cross borders: fibre, yarn and fabric are traded, so no single country need host every stage.
- Actual chains branch and vary. This map shows typical stages, not a route any particular product took.

### Field preparation, planting and crop care

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Preparing land, sowing, thinning, weeding, irrigating, applying fertiliser and pesticides, and carrying water, inputs and equipment.

**Who may do it**: Smallholder families including unpaid family members, tenant farmers and sharecroppers, and daily-wage workers hired for weeding and spraying. In a 2025 assessment of women cotton farmers in Sindh, nearly all sowed by hand and most weeded, while few operated irrigation or sprayed.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Hours in open fields with little shade, bending and lifting, and protective clothing for spraying that holds in heat. In Indian Punjab, sowing falls in April and May; in Sindh, planting can begin in March, so early field work can meet hot weather.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: No shade or water point at the field; long walks home; household water collection on top of farm work; no labour-law or social-protection coverage for many small farmers.

**What could change**: A shared water point and shade near the field; work moved to cooler hours by agreement; spraying planned for cooler, calm periods with suitable protection; help with water collection at home in the hottest weeks.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: The farmer or landholder sets the day for family and hired workers; landlords shape tenants' choices; extension services, farmer groups and programmes can supply water points and training; local government shapes water supply.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: No checked study measures heat strain or illness among cotton field workers in Asia on this page, or the effect of shade or water points in cotton fields. ### Harvesting and collection

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Hand-picking open bolls, often over several rounds; carrying picked cotton in cloth bundles; collecting and weighing it at the edge of the field.

**Who may do it**: Mostly women in hand-picked cotton in Pakistan, according to reports; family members; daily-wage and piece-rate pickers; seasonal and migrant pickers recruited through contractors.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Long days bent among plants in sun; carrying growing bundles; pesticide residues on plants. When picking falls depends on the place and the year: where sowing starts early, as in Sindh from March, picking can start while the weather is still hot.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Pay by weight means a break costs money; weighing points set the rhythm of the day; clinics may be far away; few pickers wear protective gear, and many report health effects during the picking season.

**What could change**: A paid or protected cooling break that does not cut earnings; water and shade at weighing points; shorter carrying distances to collection points; first aid and transport to a clinic.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: The grower or contractor who sets the rate and the weighing schedule; buyers and programmes whose prices and standards shape what growers can afford; health services for referral.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: Checked studies here measure pesticide-related health costs, not heat strain; the share of pickers paid by weight and its effect on rest are not measured on this page. Sources: [The Express Tribune, from Reuters, 2017](https://tribune.com.pk/story/1502534/women-pickers-toil-unprotected-pakistans-cotton-fields); [International Labour Organization, 2026](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/cotton-farming-communities-trained-safe-work-practices-amid-extreme-heat); [USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (GAIN), 2018](https://gain.fas.usda.gov/Recent%20GAIN%20Publications/Cotton%20and%20Products%20Annual_Islamabad_Pakistan_3-20-2018.pdf); [Bakhsh et al., BMC Public Health, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27618928/); [Yasin et al., Environmental Science and Pollution Research, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32865685/).

### Aggregation, weighing, storage and transport

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Bringing seed cotton to traders, market yards, procurement centres and gins; loading and unloading trucks and trolleys; waiting to be weighed and paid; storing heaps and modules.

**Who may do it**: Farmers waiting with their crop; casual loaders and unloaders; truck and tractor drivers; traders' and yard workers.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Waiting in exposed yards and queues, sometimes for long periods, and heavy loading in the sun. In 2018 Pakistan was reported to have 1,200 ginneries, each fed with seed cotton brought from farms by road.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Losing a place in the queue to seek shade; payment that depends on being present; loaders paid per load; no toilets or water in the yard.

**What could change**: Shaded waiting and loading areas; appointment or token systems that shorten exposed queues; water and toilets in yards; handling aids for loading.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: Yard and market operators, procurement agencies and gin owners; transport contractors; local authorities that run market yards.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: No checked source on this page describes heat in cotton market yards or tests appointment systems; this stage is described from how the work is organised and needs local assessment. Sources: [USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (GAIN), 2018](https://gain.fas.usda.gov/Recent%20GAIN%20Publications/Cotton%20and%20Products%20Annual_Islamabad_Pakistan_3-20-2018.pdf).

### Ginning and baling

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Separating cotton fibre (lint) from seed, cleaning out foreign material, and pressing lint into bales. Cottonseed is a coproduct, sold for crushing into oil and other uses, not simply waste.

**Who may do it**: Gin operators, feeders, press and bale workers, maintenance staff, and seasonal workers hired for the ginning season, many on short or informal terms.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Heat from machinery, motors and the building, cotton dust, noise and moving machinery, and heavy bale handling. Loose cotton fibre can smoulder if ignited, which shapes what cooling is safe.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Seasonal workers may fear losing the next shift if they report symptoms; dust and fire risks limit simple fixes such as fans or water sprays around lint.

**What could change**: Engineered ventilation and dust control designed together; heat-source controls on motors and dryers; roof and building measures; water, toilets and a cool recovery area; machine guarding for handling aids; non-punitive symptom reporting.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: Gin owners and managers; equipment suppliers and engineers; fire and factory inspectorates where they apply; buyers who set delivery schedules.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: No checked study here measures heat in Asian cotton gins; the respiratory study and the fire testing describe other hazards that cooling choices have to respect. Sources: [USDA Economic Research Service, 2024](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/cotton-and-wool/cotton-sector-at-a-glance); [USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (GAIN), 2018](https://gain.fas.usda.gov/Recent%20GAIN%20Publications/Cotton%20and%20Products%20Annual_Islamabad_Pakistan_3-20-2018.pdf); [Dube et al., International Journal of Occupational Safety and Ergonomics, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24321634/); [Hughs and Wakelyn, Journal of Agricultural Safety and Health, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29140634/).

### Spinning

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Turning cotton fibre into yarn in large machine halls.

**Who may do it**: Machine operators, often working continuous shifts.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Heat from running machines and the building; air conditions in some mills are controlled for the yarn as well as for people.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Shift patterns and production targets; see the indoor controls on the factory page.

**What could change**: Indoor heat controls: ventilation, heat-source reduction, water, rest and fair targets.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: Mill owners and managers, with worker representatives.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: Covered in more depth on Heat inside factories.

[Spinning and weaving on Heat inside factories](https://beattheheat.asia/factory-heat/#other-factories)

### Weaving or knitting, and wet processing

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Making fabric from yarn by weaving or knitting; dyeing, printing and finishing, which can happen to fibre, yarn, fabric or finished garments.

**Who may do it**: Loom and knitting operators; dye-house and finishing workers.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Hot water, steam and wet floors in dyeing and finishing; heat from looms and knitting machines.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Chemical hazards that need protective equipment, which adds heat.

**What could change**: Steam and moisture control at source, protective equipment chosen for heat as well as chemicals.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: Mill and dye-house owners and managers, with worker representatives.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: Covered in more depth on Heat inside factories.

[Dyeing and wet processing on Heat inside factories](https://beattheheat.asia/factory-heat/#other-factories)

### Garment manufacturing

#### Work and exposure

**What work happens**: Cutting, sewing, pressing, finishing and packing garments.

**Who may do it**: Sewing operators, most of them women in many producing countries, pressers, finishers and packers.

**How heat exposure occurs**: Roof heat, pressing steam, crowded lines and heavy packing.

#### Pay and protection

**What can make protection difficult**: Line targets, piece rates and attendance bonuses that make breaks costly.

**What could change**: See the garment-factory walkthrough and intervention library.

#### Who can act

**Who controls that change**: Factory managers, worker representatives and buyers.

**Evidence and uncertainties**: Covered in depth on Heat inside factories.

Three pathways

## People, the crop and livelihoods are affected in different ways

### Heat affecting people

The same seven parts set a cotton worker's heat load as a factory worker's: air temperature, sun and radiant heat, humidity, air movement, the heat bodies make at work, clothing and protective equipment, and how long exposure lasts and whether people recover overnight.

Documented Women cotton pickers in Pakistani Punjab report health effects during the picking season that researchers relate mainly to pesticide exposure, alongside dust and sun.

Plausible pathway, to investigate Long picking days in hot weather, paid by weight, with little shade or water, add up to heat strain; this page found no checked study measuring it in cotton fields in Asia.

[The seven parts, explained on Heat inside factories](https://beattheheat.asia/factory-heat/#understand)

### Heat affecting cotton

Heat acts on the plant through its own routes: establishment, flowering, boll development, water demand, yield and fibre quality. Drought, rain and pests act alongside it, and the effects differ by cultivar, growth stage, irrigation and place.

Documented In a controlled-environment study, heat during flowering cut pollen germination by 71% and lint yield by 26% on average, and cultivars differed. Indian agronomists describe 27 to 32 °C as favourable for reproductive development, and rain at harvest damages fibre.

Plausible pathway, to investigate Field effects depend on how heat combines with water stress and timing; a growth-chamber result does not give a field yield loss for any district.

### Disruption affecting livelihoods and working conditions

Lost working time, medical costs, interrupted production, payment delays and contractual pressure can pass through households and firms, and the routes are not the same for everyone.

Documented Health costs of cotton picking fall on women pickers' households, and distance from hospital adds to them; in Sindh, women's days are split between cotton work and unpaid care.

Plausible pathway, to investigate A bad season can mean less paid picking work, more debt for tenant farmers and pressure to work through symptoms; the size and direction of these effects need local evidence.

What this page does not claim

- That every crop loss causes the same effect on wages or prices.
- That every hotter season brings more pests.
- That higher cotton prices necessarily reach workers.
- That protecting crop yields protects workers.
- That a farm weather forecast measures any individual's exposure at work.

Informal work

## Nine working arrangements, not one

Informal does not mean unskilled, unimportant or outside formal supply chains. Pickers, loaders and gin workers carry skills and knowledge of the work and the weather, and informal work happens inside chains that end in registered firms and export contracts. The farmers trained in Madhya Pradesh were described as lacking coverage under labour legislation and social protection.

- Smallholder owner-operators

Farm their own land with family labour; carry crop risk and their own heat exposure; often outside labour law as self-employed.

- Tenant farmers and sharecroppers

Farm land they do not own, paying rent or sharing the crop; carry crop risk and often debt; decisions depend partly on the landholder.

- Unpaid contributing family workers

Work on the family farm without a wage of their own; often women and young people; their hours rarely appear in records.

- Daily-wage agricultural workers

Hired by the day for weeding, spraying or picking; paid only for days worked.

- Piece-rate harvest workers

Paid by the weight or amount picked; a break or a slower pace costs money directly.

- Seasonal and migrant workers

Move for the season; may lack local documents, housing, health access and benefits where they work.

- Contractor-recruited workers

Recruited and often paid through a labour contractor; responsibility is split between the contractor and the farm or gin.

- Casual loaders, drivers and warehouse workers

Paid per load, trip or day in yards, transport and storage.

- Informally employed workers inside registered enterprises

Work in a registered gin, mill or factory without a written contract, benefits or job security.

### Who can actually stop?

Illustrative situations, not cases For each: the exposure, what stops the person resting, the arrangement that would make protection usable, and who is responsible for it.

#### A picker paid by weight takes a cooling break

- **Exposure**Hours bent in the sun, carrying a growing bundle.
- **Constraint**Every minute resting is cotton not picked and money not earned.
- **Necessary arrangement**A paid break or a rate that does not penalise necessary rest; water and shade at the weighing point.
- **Responsible actor**The grower or contractor who sets the rate and the schedule; buyers whose prices make it affordable.

#### A casual loader waits in an exposed procurement yard

- **Exposure**Waiting and loading in an open yard in the afternoon.
- **Constraint**Leaving the queue for shade risks losing the next load.
- **Necessary arrangement**Shaded waiting areas and a token or appointment system that keeps people's place.
- **Responsible actor**The yard operator or procurement agency.

#### A tenant farmer faces both crop risk and repayment obligations

- **Exposure**Field work in the heat at sowing and picking.
- **Constraint**A poor crop still leaves rent or loan payments due, so cutting work hours feels impossible.
- **Necessary arrangement**Repayment terms that flex in a bad season, crop insurance that pays reliably, and work-hour changes that do not risk the crop.
- **Responsible actor**Landholders, lenders and insurers; agricultural agencies.

#### A seasonal gin worker fears losing the next shift after reporting symptoms

- **Exposure**Heat, dust and heavy lifting inside a gin.
- **Constraint**Reporting dizziness may mean not being called back.
- **Necessary arrangement**Non-punitive reporting, paid recovery time and a written commitment that reporting does not affect rehiring.
- **Responsible actor**The gin owner and manager; buyers and inspectors who check whether reporting is safe.

Women and intersecting inequalities

## Who carries the extra load

Gender runs through every stage above, along with age, disability, migration, language, caste and ethnicity. These shape tasks, pay, access to toilets and clinics, and whose voice counts. They are not reasons to treat any group as uniformly vulnerable.

A question to ask of every protection

Does this protection expand someone's choices, or transfer more unpaid work, cost or risk to someone else?

- Tasks and control over earnings

In the Sindh assessment, women did most of the hand sowing, weeding and picking, and few operated irrigation, sprayed or sold cotton, which shapes who controls the income.

- Unpaid care and water collection

Women's waking hours were split almost evenly between cotton work and unpaid domestic and care work, and in Madhya Pradesh a participant described fetching water becoming harder in summer as supplies fell.

- Water, toilets and menstrual hygiene

Drinking water helps only if a safe, private toilet is within reach; the Madhya Pradesh workshops reported inadequate drinking water, sanitation and washing areas. This page found no checked study of menstrual hygiene in cotton fields.

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Precautionary occupational guidance and accommodation, not exclusion from work, apply in fields as in factories; India's health ministry has published guidance on heat and pregnancy for health workers.

[Pregnancy at work, on Heat inside factories](https://beattheheat.asia/factory-heat/#women-pregnancy)

- Transport and shift timing

Moving work to cooler hours can mean travel in the dark; safe transport is part of the arrangement, not an afterthought.

- Documents, benefits and grievance routes

Seasonal and migrant workers may lack local identity documents, benefits and health access; a grievance route is only useful if it is reachable and safe.

[Who could help a household through a hot season](https://beattheheat.asia/social-protection/)

- Expertise and organisation

Workers and farmers know where heat collects and how the season runs. In Madhya Pradesh, the absence of farmers' associations limited collective dialogue on safety and welfare.

Asian context

## Not one cotton belt

Examples start in India and Pakistan, where the evidence on this page is strongest. Other places appear only where a checked source supports them.

- Seasonal calendars differ

In Indian Punjab, sowing falls in April to mid-May; in Sindh, planting can begin in March. Picking times vary by place and year and are not given here from a checked source. Exposure at planting, crop care and harvest has to be read against the local calendar, not assumed.

- Rainfed and irrigated

Most of India's cotton is rainfed, which ties yields closely to the monsoon; irrigated systems face other limits, including groundwater.

- Manual and mechanised harvest

Cotton is picked by hand in the settings described here; in the United States it is machine-harvested. Where machines replace hand-picking, heat exposure shifts to fewer workers and to machine operation and maintenance, and work is lost.

- Smallholders and larger operations

Small and marginal farmers often lack the shade, water and money that larger operations can provide, and are often outside labour-law coverage themselves.

- Recruitment, payment and protection

Payment by weight, contractor recruitment and limited social protection change who can stop work; they differ by place and need local checking.

Countries important in garment manufacturing are not treated here as cotton-growing settings. Where this page has no checked source for a place, it says nothing about it.

Find protections

## An intervention library, by who acts and who pays

33 measures in six families, organised around prevention, protection people can actually use, and continuity of income and services. Costs are described, not priced; no cooling effect, health benefit or return is claimed without a checked source.

What the evidence labels mean

**Occupational guidance**: What agencies recommend. Where it sets duties, they apply only in the jurisdiction named.

**Research finding**: A study with a stated design, population and place. The finding holds for those conditions.

**Programme description**: What a programme says it did. Training delivered is not proof that illness fell.

**Worker testimony, as reported**: What a worker said, as reported. It shows an experience, not how common it is.

**News report**: A journalist's report. Useful for timing and context; not a measurement.

**Proposal requiring validation**: A candidate arrangement with no checked source on this page. Described so it can be appraised.

### Field and task controls

Shade, water, lighter loads, rest and adjustment, where the work happens.

#### Shade and cooler recovery spaces near the work

Occupational guidance Research finding

Portable or fixed shade close to where people work lets them cool during breaks without a long walk.

Who acts, who pays, and what to watch

**Stage**: Field work, Picking

**Intended beneficiaries**: Pickers, weeders and family workers.

**Implementing actor**: Grower or landholder; farmer groups; programmes.

**Funding responsibility**: Grower, often with programme or buyer support.

**Recurring needs**: Moving, repairing and replacing shade through the season.

**Prerequisites**: Shade within a short walk; time to use it.

**Possible harms or exclusions**: A shade far away goes unused; shade without paid time protects only those who can afford to stop.

**Evidence**:
- **Occupational guidance.** [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS), 2026](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/heat/heat_control.html)
- **Research finding.** A cooling station lowered heat strain in a pilot field trial in an indoor sawmill, not a cotton field. [Journal of Agromedicine, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42897938/)

#### Safe drinking water, replenishment and sanitation

Programme description Occupational guidance

Water close by, kept cool and refilled, with a safe private toilet nearby, replaces sweat losses; people drink less when toilets are far or unsafe.

Who acts, who pays, and what to watch

**Stage**: Field work, Picking, Yards and transport, Ginning

**Intended beneficiaries**: Everyone working in heat, especially women.

**Implementing actor**: Grower, yard operator or gin owner; local government for supply.

**Funding responsibility**: The employer or site operator; buyers and programmes can share costs.

**Recurring needs**: Refilling, cleaning, testing and supplies, every day of the season.

**Prerequisites**: A water source; someone responsible for refilling.

**Possible harms or exclusions**: Water without toilets shifts the burden to women; one intake schedule does not fit everyone.

**Evidence**:
- **Programme description.** [International Labour Organization, 2026](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/cotton-farming-communities-trained-safe-work-practices-amid-extreme-heat)
- **Occupational guidance.** [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS), 2026](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/heat/heat_control.html)

#### Shorter carrying distances, handling aids and task redesign

Programme description Occupational guidance

Collection points closer to pickers, carts and changed loads reduce the heat the body makes from carrying.

Who acts, who pays, and what to watch

**Stage**: Field work, Picking, Yards and transport

**Intended beneficiaries**: Pickers, carriers and loaders.

**Implementing actor**: Grower, contractor, yard operator.

**Funding responsibility**: Grower or operator.

**Recurring needs**: Maintenance of carts; staffing of collection points.

**Prerequisites**: Paths and equipment suited to the field.

**Possible harms or exclusions**: Mechanisation can remove jobs or bring machine hazards.

**Evidence**:
- **Programme description.** The programme trained safe postures for carrying loads. [International Labour Organization, 2026](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/cotton-farming-communities-trained-safe-work-practices-amid-extreme-heat)
- **Occupational guidance.** [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS), 2026](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/heat/heat_control.html)

#### Staffing and work-rest arrangements

Occupational guidance Programme description

Enough people to share the work and rest planned around the heat and the workload, with strenuous work moved to cooler times.

Who acts, who pays, and what to watch

**Stage**: Field work, Picking, Ginning

**Intended beneficiaries**: All workers, especially in the heaviest tasks.

About temperature and heat indices

Temperature or a heat index alone does not describe a worker's heat stress. Occupational guidance uses a heat stress index such as WBGT, which combines humidity, radiant heat and air movement, and interprets it with workload, clothing and acclimatisation. Thresholds come from qualified assessment and the rules that apply where the work happens.

How risk can travel

## How risk can be passed along the chain

Three mechanisms to investigate. Each is plausible from how work is organised; the full causal path is not established by the evidence on this page.

- Lost earnings → pressure to keep working → more exposure

When pay depends on output and a hot spell cuts what people can pick, the pressure to work longer or through symptoms rises.

**Interruption point:** Pay that does not penalise rest, and income support in heatwaves.

**Actor with authority:** Growers and contractors; buyers through prices; social-protection agencies.

Mechanism to investigate

- Production disruption → compressed schedules → pressure downstream

A late or heat-damaged harvest can squeeze the time for ginning, spinning and garment production when delivery dates stay fixed.

**Interruption point:** Delivery schedules with room for climate disruption.

**Actor with authority:** Brands, retailers and traders who set delivery dates.

Mechanism to investigate

- Household water and care demands → less recovery → harder to work safely

In hot weeks, collecting water and caring for others take more time, leaving less rest before the next working day.

**Interruption point:** Water supply closer to homes, and hours that allow recovery.

**Actor with authority:** Local government for water; employers for hours.

Mechanism to investigate

People across a chain are connected through production, places and institutions, a community of fate in the sense used on Who shares the heat. But exposure, bargaining power and responsibility are unequal along it: a picker and a brand share a chain, not a burden. [Who shares the heat](https://beattheheat.asia/shared-heat/).

## The evidence, its limits, and what was left out

### Gaps

- No checked study on this page measures heat strain or heat illness among cotton field workers, pickers, yard workers or gin workers in Asia. Studies of rice, sugarcane and salt work are summarised below as nearby evidence.
- No checked evaluation tests shade, water points, paid breaks or appointment systems in cotton production.
- The pickers' studies measure pesticide-related health effects and costs, not heat.
- The crop study is a controlled-environment experiment; field yield losses from heat for any district are not given here.
- No checked source describes heat in cotton market yards or gins in Asia.
- No checked study on menstrual hygiene or pregnancy in cotton field work was found.
- No checked source supports contrasts with other Asian cotton regions, such as mechanised harvesting in China, so none is made.

### Nearby evidence: heat strain measured in other Asian field work

No checked study measures heat strain among cotton workers in Asia. Researchers have measured it in neighbouring work: rice and sugarcane fields, farming villages and salt pans in India, Thailand, Viet Nam, Malaysia and Sri Lanka. These studies are not about cotton, and crops differ in season, posture, pace and pay; they show what has been found where people looked, and what a cotton study could measure.

#### Strain in the body

- In rice fields in West Bengal, India, hourly heat (WBGT) was 26-32°C. Among 124 harvesters, most reported exhaustion and pain on hot days; in 48 whose heart rate was recorded, it recovered more slowly at high heat, and bundles collected fell by about 5% for each degree of WBGT above 26°C. [Sahu et al., Industrial Health, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23685851/)
- Among 200 men harvesting paddy in West Bengal from April to June 2024, heat (WBGT) predicted physiological strain hour by hour; older age added to the strain and work experience was protective. [Journal of Occupational and Environmental Medicine, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40963212/)
- In a small study of 14 people transplanting paddy by hand in Northeast India, oral temperature rose by 0.5°C after 65 minutes of work at the lower heat levels studied. The authors propose work-rest cycles that shorten as heat rises, down to 35 minutes of work and 22 of rest at a WBGT of 31-32°C. [Work (IOS Press), 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40808610/)
- In the hottest month of a Thai sugarcane harvest, cane cutters faced high heat stress and strain, with dehydration and rises in heart rate and body temperature across the shift; workers in the sugar factory were at low risk. [International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32882881/)
- In Nakhon Sawan, Thailand, sugarcane harvesters' heat stress exceeded the occupational limit (ACGIH) for 72.7% of working time in the cooler months and 90.9% in the hotter month; harvesting burnt cane added to the heat. [Annals of Work Exposures and Health, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39899707/)

#### Symptoms people report

- Among 379 rice farmers in Ha Tinh province, Viet Nam, 83.4% reported at least one heat-related symptom during the 2021 summer-autumn harvest. More than 4 hours outdoors in the heat and heavy tasks were associated with more illness, and drinking more water with less. [Journal of Agromedicine, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453470/)
- Among 295 Thai sugarcane workers (165 men, 130 women), 98% reported at least one heat-related symptom and 74% at least one severe symptom. Symptoms rose with each degree of full-shift heat, and the study found no significant difference in risk between women and men. [International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42303799/)
- Among 200 sugarcane farmers in Kamphaeng Phet, Thailand, 48% reported heat-related illness symptoms in summer. Being a woman and wearing two-layer shirts were associated with higher risk, and drinking 3.1-5.0 litres a day with lower risk. [Industrial Health, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34819408/)

#### Water, salt and kidneys

- In seven Indian salt pans, where there is no pesticide exposure, 87% of 352 workers exceeded the heat-stress limit and median water intake over the workday was one litre. Workers above the limit had 2.9 times the odds of reduced kidney function. [Kidney International Reports, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441492/)
- Among 50 sea salt workers in Thailand, more than half were dehydrated after work. [Roczniki Panstwowego Zakladu Higieny, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34928120/)
- Among agricultural and brick-field workers in eastern India, sweat gland activity was higher in summer. Blood electrolytes stayed in the normal range at the monitoring sessions, but full-shift heat above a WBGT of about 23-25°C was associated with a higher probability of low blood sodium. [American Journal of Human Biology, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42214031/)
- Thai sugarcane cutters and farmers, most of them women, both had higher urinary markers of kidney injury after the shift than before, more so among cutters. Cutters exceeded recommended heat exposure; farmers did not. [Human and Ecological Risk Assessment, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34290492/)
- In chronic kidney disease hotspot villages of Bargarh district, Odisha, screening of 1,136 people identified 157 potential cases of heat stress nephropathy, 63.3% of them farmers. The study is cross-sectional and does not show cause. [Kidney International Reports, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41141547/)
- Among 261 agricultural workers in four villages of Sri Lanka's North Central Province, a heat stress and dehydration symptom index was not associated with urine albumin or urine concentration; the authors note that urine albumin is not a reliable marker of early disease. [Preventive Medicine Reports, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31304082/)

Kidney findings are cross-sectional: they show patterns at one time, not cause. The Sri Lanka study found no association, and is listed for that reason.

#### Heat with pesticides

- Over six months in Malaysia, conventional farmworkers adapted less well to heat than agroecological farmers; the authors link this to earlier metabolic effects of pesticide exposure. [International Journal of Environmental Health Research, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35157533/)

Cotton work often combines both exposures; no checked study measures the two together in cotton.

#### Output and pay

- Among 287 Thai sugarcane workers, each degree of full-shift heat was associated with about 9 fewer stalks harvested per hour. [Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment and Health, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42853598/)
- Across 11 districts of Tamil Nadu, WBGT measured at outdoor workplaces exceeded 30°C, and among 1,560 informal outdoor workers higher heat was associated with 1.4 times the risk of reported productivity loss. [Scientific Reports, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41857093/)

Where pay follows output, fewer stalks or bundles an hour can mean less income, which is one reason rest can feel unaffordable.

#### What the research itself leaves out

- A 2026 mapping review of 210 field reports from low- and middle-income countries found 40% from the South-East Asia Region and 30% from India; agriculture made up 33%. 39% of reports included no women, and only 9% included any intervention to protect workers. [Current Environmental Health Reports, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42905195/)

On women and men, the studies differ: one Thai sugarcane study found no significant difference in symptom risk, another found women at higher risk, and many reports include no women at all. This is one reason the page does not assume a single pattern.

For a cotton study

What a cotton study could borrow: hourly heat measured where people work (WBGT), heart rate and body temperature across a shift, urine concentration before and after work, output per hour alongside pay, and women counted and reported, all agreed with workers and their organisations before any measurement starts.

Searched in Europe PMC (November 2026): heat strain, body temperature, heart rate, WBGT, urine specific gravity, heat-related illness and kidney markers among farmers and agricultural, rice, sugarcane, tea, salt and brick workers in South and Southeast Asia, 2008 to 2026, with intervention terms. No measured field study from Pakistan or Bangladesh was found, and no field trial of shade, water or rest in Asian agriculture; one Indian study proposes work-rest cycles from measurements but did not test them over a season.
