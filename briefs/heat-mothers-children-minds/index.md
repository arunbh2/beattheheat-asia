Evidence brief · November 2026

# Heat and the health of mothers, children and minds

What the evidence shows about extreme heat and pregnancy, newborns and young children, mental health, and violence against women in South and Southeast Asia: what is known, what is inferred, what is still uncertain, and five questions that research could answer.

Common Shade · beattheheat.asia · Free to use under CC BY 4.0 ·

## In brief

- Heat in pregnancy is linked to more preterm births and stillbirths[1](#ref-1), and the largest risks fall on poorer women. Women doing heavy work in the heat may carry the most risk[2](#ref-2).

- Young children face more heatwave days than in the past[3](#ref-3), and warmer weather is linked to more diarrhoea[4](#ref-4), a leading cause of death in young children.

- Heat is linked to distress, worse outcomes for people living with mental illness[5](#ref-5), and, in India, to more suicides in the growing season[6](#ref-6).

- Across India, Nepal and Pakistan, hotter years are linked to more violence against women by their partners[7](#ref-7).

- Most of the strongest evidence comes from outside the region, or from one place within it. What protects people is the least studied part.

## Pregnancy and birth

Known

- Across 70 studies in 27 countries, the odds of a preterm birth rose about 5% for each 1 °C rise in temperature, and about 16% during heatwaves; stillbirths rose about 5% for each 1 °C[1](#ref-1).
- The associations were largest among women in lower socioeconomic groups and at the youngest and oldest ages[1](#ref-1).
- In Tamil Nadu, among 800 pregnant women doing moderate to heavy physical work, those with high workplace heat had about twice the risk of miscarriage[2](#ref-2).
- A call to action by three UN agencies names heat among the hazards linked to gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, preterm birth, low birth weight and stillbirth, and to stress and anxiety in pregnancy[8](#ref-8).

Inferred

- Rest, lighter work and cooler places during pregnancy could reduce these risks; this follows from the evidence, but has not been tested in the region.
- Women who keep working outdoors or fetching water while pregnant are likely to carry the most risk.

Uncertain

- Only seven of the 27 countries in the main review were low- or middle-income[1](#ref-1); effect sizes in South and Southeast Asia may differ.
- The Tamil Nadu finding comes from one cohort, and its range is wide[2](#ref-2).
- A preprint, not yet peer reviewed, estimates that the extreme-heat days a pregnancy passes through in Bangladesh rose from about 4 for births in 2018 to about 30 for births in 2024[9](#ref-9).

## Newborns and young children

Known

- In 2014–2023, each infant in India faced an average of 7.7 heatwave days a year, 47% more than in the 1990s[3](#ref-3).
- Across 26 studies, each 1 °C rise in temperature was linked to about 7% more diarrhoea, a leading cause of death in young children[4](#ref-4).

Inferred

- Repeated diarrhoea in hot months is likely to add to undernutrition, and to the load on clinics.
- Safe water, handwashing and oral rehydration in place before the hot months could offset part of the risk.

Uncertain

- The diarrhoea review found signs of publication bias and an uneven spread of studies across regions[4](#ref-4).
- We found no measurements of how hot labour wards and newborn units get during heatwaves in the region.

## Mental health and suicide

Known

- Heat is linked to more anxiety, distress and suicide, and to worse outcomes for people living with mental illness[5](#ref-5).
- Heat can worsen underlying illness, including mental health conditions[10](#ref-10).
- In India, a 1 °C rise in a day’s temperature above 20 °C in the growing season was linked to about 70 more suicides, as the same heat cut crop yields; warming over 30 years is estimated to account for 59,300 suicides[6](#ref-6).
- In many countries, mental health services are far from meeting the need[11](#ref-11).

Inferred

- Lost farm income is the likely route between heat and suicide in rural India; support after crop losses could weaken the link.

Uncertain

- The India suicide findings have been debated.
- Little is known about heat and self-harm in cities, or in other countries of the region.

## Violence against women

Known

- Across 194,871 women in India, Nepal and Pakistan, each 1 °C rise in yearly temperature was linked to about 4.5% more intimate partner violence, with physical and sexual violence rising most[7](#ref-7).
- Under very high emissions, it could rise about 21% by the 2090s, against about 6% if warming is held lowest[7](#ref-7).

Inferred

- Lost income, stress and crowded, hot homes may all play a part.
- Longer trips to fetch water in the heat may expose women and girls to harassment.

Uncertain

- The study is cross-sectional and uses yearly temperatures: it shows an association, not its causes[7](#ref-7).
- Whether income support, cooler homes or women’s groups weaken the link has not been studied.

## Women at work

Known

- Among 312 women workers in India, heat exceeded safe limits for 71%; of those with poor or no toilets, 87% reported genitourinary problems, and about 1 in 10 showed heat strain and moderate dehydration[12](#ref-12).

Inferred

- Safe toilets, water and rest at hot workplaces are likely to protect women’s health, including in pregnancy.

Uncertain

- The study covers three sectors in one country.

## What could help

Possibilities the evidence points to. Each is described on the map, with who could act.

- [Heat-safe pregnancy and newborn care](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-maternalheat) Heat advice and checks in antenatal care, lighter work and rest for pregnant workers, cool labour wards and newborn units, and visits to new mothers in a heatwave.
- [Safe water and rehydration for young children](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-childwater) Safe drinking water, handwashing and oral rehydration salts in place before the hot months, carried to families by clinics and community health workers.
- [Mental health in heat plans](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-mindheat) Heat plans that include mental health: support that reaches farm households after crop losses, services that stay open through heatwaves, and warnings that reach people in mental health care.
- [Violence prevention in heat planning](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-safeathome) Helplines, shelters and women’s groups included in heat plans and kept open in heatwaves, income support in hot seasons, and health workers trained to recognise violence on their visits.
- [Heat-ready health services](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-readyclinics) Cooling, backup power, surge plans and trained frontline workers keep clinics working through heatwaves.
- [Cooling centres and check-ins](https://beattheheat.asia/#break-coolingcentres) Public cooling spaces, water points and check-ins on older people living alone can offer relief in a heatwave. How much they protect health depends on who can reach them, how long people stay, and how hot it is once they leave.

## Five questions research could answer

From the learning agenda on the [Evidence map](https://beattheheat.asia/evidence-map/#questions). What could answer each one, and at what scale, are our suggestions.

- Which protections lower heat risks in pregnancy in South and Southeast Asia: rest and lighter work, cooler homes, antenatal advice, or cash that lets women stop work?

**What we have**: A review of 70 studies in 27 countries, only seven of them low- or middle-income, and one cohort of 800 working women in Tamil Nadu; none tests what reduces the risk.

**What could answer it**: Add heat exposure and simple protections to antenatal records in a few districts, and compare outcomes across hot seasons.

**Scale**: Two or three districts, two to three years

- How hot do labour wards and newborn units get in a heatwave, and what keeps them safe when the power fails?

**What we have**: A WHO, UNICEF and UNFPA call to action that names heat among the risks to mothers and newborns, and no measurements from facilities in the region that we have found.

**What could answer it**: Log indoor temperatures and power cuts in a sample of facilities through one hot season, alongside any cooling and back-up power they have.

**Scale**: Twenty to thirty facilities, one season

- How much does heat add to diarrhoea and undernutrition in young children in the region, and how much do safe water and oral rehydration offset it?

**What we have**: A review of 26 studies finding about 7% more diarrhoea per 1 °C, uneven across regions and with signs of publication bias.

**What could answer it**: Combine clinic records of child diarrhoea with temperature and water-supply data in a few towns over several seasons.

**Scale**: Three to five towns, three seasons

- Beyond farm households in India, how does heat affect self-harm and suicide across South and Southeast Asia, and does income support in bad seasons reduce it?

**What we have**: One national study of India over 47 years linking heat in the growing season to suicides through crop losses; little from cities or other countries.

**What could answer it**: Link daily temperatures with suicide and self-harm records where they exist, and study districts where crop insurance or cash reached households in bad years.

**Scale**: Two or three countries, existing records

- Why is heat linked to more intimate partner violence in South Asia, and do income support, cooler homes or women’s groups weaken the link?

**What we have**: A cross-sectional study of 194,871 women in India, Nepal and Pakistan, using yearly temperatures; it shows an association, not the reasons for it.

**What could answer it**: Add heat and household strain questions to existing violence surveys, and compare places with and without seasonal income support.

**Scale**: Existing surveys, with a qualitative study in a few districts

## How this brief was made

Every figure is one the Common Shade map already cites. Each source was opened and archived, and the terms we rely on were found in its text; for journal articles, in the abstract. Known means measured, official or peer-reviewed; inferred means a reasonable step from the evidence, or a projection; uncertain means a gap, a preprint, or a finding that has been debated. If something here is wrong, or a better source exists, we would be grateful to [hear it](https://beattheheat.asia/corrections/).
