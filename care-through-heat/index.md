# Care that continues through heat

Who takes on the work when someone becomes ill or a service closes?

Heat adds care: a child home from a closed school, an older relative who needs checking, a clinic visit that takes half a day. Who absorbs it depends on the household and on the services around it. Where services close or cannot be reached, the work does not disappear; it moves, often onto women and girls, but not always and not only.

### What the evidence says

- Guidance WHO warns that heatwaves can reduce health service delivery when power shortages disrupt health facilities, transport and water. [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health), 2026
- Qualitative study A 2026 study built on eight semistructured interviews describes disabled people's experience of heatwaves, including communication supported by caregivers. [Kibria et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13753-026-00778-y), 2026
- Qualitative study Interviews with 15 older people and 15 family members describe how households adapt, season by season, to extreme heat. [Junlapeeya et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37372754/), 2023
- Study A laboratory study of 58 older adults (320 short exposures) examined fan use in extreme heat; short laboratory exposures do not show what happens over days at home. [Chaseling et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12308431/), 2025

Each line says what kind of evidence it is. Guidance recommends; a study observes; a report describes what an organisation did. None of them alone shows that a measure improved health.

## When a service closes

Illustrative disruptions. No times or costs are estimated.

### A childcare centre closes for a heat wave

**Who needs care**: Young children, at home all day in a hot room.

**Who provides it now**: Usually a parent or another relative, often a woman or an older girl, sometimes a neighbour.

**What extra work arises**: A full day of care on top of paid work; meals; keeping children cool and hydrated; perhaps missed shifts or school for an older sibling.

**Public or employer arrangements that could reduce the burden**:
- Keeping the centre open with cooling, shade and water, or opening an alternative cooler space
- Paid leave or flexible hours from employers when centres close
- Notice in advance, so families can plan
- Meals or cash where centres also provide food

**What trade-offs remain**: A centre that stays open needs cooling, staff and water that work in heat; one that closes keeps children out of a hot building but moves the work home. Neither is right everywhere.

### Home support for an older or disabled person stops

**Who needs care**: An older or disabled person who relies on visits for meals, medicines, washing or checks.

**Who provides it now**: Paid care workers, community health workers or family members.

**What extra work arises**: Family members take time off paid work or travel in the heat; the person may go without checks when risk is highest.

**Public or employer arrangements that could reduce the burden**:
- Treating home visits and check-ins as an essential service with a heat plan
- Cool transport, water and rest for care workers, who face heat themselves
- Phone or video check-ins where people can use them, in accessible formats
- Paid care rather than relying on unpaid relatives

**What trade-offs remain**: Fewer visits protect care workers from heat but leave people unchecked; more visits need staff, transport and pay.

### A clinic is hard to reach in the heat

**Who needs care**: A pregnant woman due for an antenatal visit; a child with a fever; an older person who needs medicine.

**Who provides it now**: The person themselves, or a relative who goes with them.

**What extra work arises**: A long walk or wait in the sun, a lost day's earnings, children to bring along or leave with someone.

**Public or employer arrangements that could reduce the burden**:
- Shaded, ventilated waiting areas with water and toilets
- Appointments at cooler times, and fewer repeat visits
- Outreach and home visits during heat waves
- Transport support, and backup power so the clinic stays open

**What trade-offs remain**: Moving appointments earlier may clash with work or care; outreach reaches people at home but needs staff and transport.

## Pregnancy, postpartum, newborns and access to care

Home, work, the journey and the clinic all matter together. This page gives no personal medical advice and asks nothing about anyone’s pregnancy; people who are pregnant follow their health worker’s advice.

### Guidance and evidence

- Guidance WHO, WMO, UNICEF and UNFPA set priority indicators, in four domains, to monitor how heat affects maternal, newborn and child health (2026). [WHO, WMO, UNICEF and UNFPA](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240125063), 2026
- Systematic review A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 103 studies examined heat thresholds for maternal and postnatal health; one person did the quality assessment, a limitation the authors note. [Heil et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76822-8), 2026
- Guidance India's guidance on heat and pregnancy for health workers includes a heat-risk checklist and calls for functional referral systems. [Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and UNFPA India](https://india.unfpa.org/en/publications/heat-and-pregnancy-guidance-healthcare-providers-and-community-health-workers), 2026
- Guidance Bangladesh launched national guidelines in 2024 to protect children and pregnant women from heat stress, with UNICEF. [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/press-releases/new-guidelines-launched-protect-children-and-pregnant-women-heat-stress-bangladesh), 2024
- Guidance UNICEF's 2023 technical note on protecting children from heat stress covers infant feeding (breastfeeding infants under six months) and monitoring and assessment. [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/media/139926/file/Protecting-children-from-heat-stress-A-technical-note-2023.pdf), 2023
- Guidance Occupational guidance on pregnancy at work names a rise in core body temperature above 38 °C as a concern and points to work-rest cycles. [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS)](https://ccohs.gc.ca/oshanswers/psychosocial/wh/reproductive-health-pregnancy-in-the-workplace.html), 2024

Each line says what kind of evidence it is. Guidance recommends; a study observes; a report describes what an organisation did. None of them alone shows that a measure improved health.

- **Appointments:** whether antenatal and postnatal visits can be reached in heat, at cooler times, with fewer repeat trips.
- **Transport and waiting:** shade, seating, water and toilets on the way and at the clinic.
- **Facility readiness:** cooling, backup power and staff who know heat illness in pregnancy and in newborns.
- **At work:** confidential adjustments, such as lighter duties or extra rest, without dismissal or loss of income.
- **What to monitor:** the shared indicators above, rather than new measures for each project.

- [Heat-safe pregnancy and newborn care](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#maternalheat) Heat advice and checks in antenatal care, lighter work and rest for pregnant workers, cool labour wards and newborn units, and visits to new mothers in a heatwave.
- [Heat-ready health services](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#readyclinics) Cooling, backup power, surge plans and trained frontline workers keep clinics working through heatwaves.

## Schools and childcare

Classrooms under hot roofs, water and toilets, shade in the yard, the journey, school meals and the staff who work there all shape heat at school. Closing protects children from a hot building but moves care home and can cost meals and learning; staying open needs cooling, water and adjusted hours that work. Building improvements, adjusted schedules and continuity arrangements can be compared; none is right everywhere.

- [Keep children in school](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#keepinschool) Cash transfers, school meals and heat-ready classrooms and timetables keep children learning through hot seasons.
- [Safe water and rehydration for young children](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#childwater) Safe drinking water, handwashing and oral rehydration salts in place before the hot months, carried to families by clinics and community health workers.

## Ageing, disability and depending on services

Accessible cooling, transport and assisted mobility, reliable power for devices, information in formats people can use, and supported care decide whether protection reaches older and disabled people. They are also the people best placed to say what works: plans that involve them from the start are more likely to fit.

- [Cooling centres and check-ins](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#coolingcentres) Public cooling spaces, water points and check-ins on older people living alone can offer relief in a heatwave. How much they protect health depends on who can reach them, how long people stay, and how hot it is once they leave.**Watch for:** A 2025 review found no study that measured health outcomes for people using cooling centres; in simulated heatwaves, the benefits were short-lived. That is not evidence of no benefit.
- [Free care for heat illness](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#freecare) Covering emergency treatment for heat illness stops one bad day from becoming years of debt.

### What we know, and what we do not

**What we know**:
- Heatwaves can disrupt health services through power, transport and water failures.
- Guidance exists on heat and pregnancy for health workers, and on protecting children from heat.

**What has been tested**:
- The sources checked here include guidance and small qualitative studies, not evaluations of service continuity in heat.

**What remains uncertain**:
- Who takes on care when services close, and at what cost to paid work.
- Whether continuity plans reach disabled and older people living alone.

**What a useful next study would ask**: Do service-continuity arrangements reduce care burdens without moving unpaid work onto other women and girls?

**What needs measuring**: Service days kept open in heat waves, time spent caring, paid work lost, check-ins completed.

Not the same thing: a cooler room and better health; a service being open and people being able to use it; a warning sent and protective action taken; a committee seat and a say in decisions; reported symptoms and a diagnosis; an association and a cause.
