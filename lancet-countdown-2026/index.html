    New evidence · October 2026

    Ten years of health evidence on heat,
    and what to do with it.

    The Lancet Countdown on health and climate change is a yearly report by more than 300 researchers in over 100 institutions, working with the World Health Organization. Its tenth report, published in October 2026, tracks 54 measures of how climate change affects health. Here is what it says about extreme heat, read for South and Southeast Asia and organised by what you can do about it.

    About this summary. Every number here is the report’s own, with the measure it comes from (“indicator 1.1.5”), so you can check it in the full report (opens in a new tab) or its technical appendix (opens in a new tab). The words and the choice of what matters for heat are ours. Most figures are global; we say when they are about Asia. The report also covers energy, food, finance and politics in depth; we cover those only where they bear on heat. We do not take positions on the policies of particular countries.

    What it means for South and Southeast Asia

    • Asia has one of the largest rises in heatwave days caused by climate change. Over 2016–25, the rise was about 12.9 days a year in Asia, behind small island developing states (16.9) and ahead of Europe (10.7). indicator 1.1.1, appendix
    • Medium human-development countries carry the heaviest share of heat deaths. In the report’s groupings, Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Lao PDR, Myanmar and Nepal are “medium”; Indonesia, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Viet Nam are “high”. In medium countries, 1.26% of all deaths over 2016–23 were related to heat, the highest share of any group. indicator 1.1.5
    • The region has the world’s largest outdoor workforce. About 0.4 billion people work outdoors in each of WHO’s South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions; in South-East Asia that is 26.6% of working-age people. More men than women work outdoors everywhere, and the biggest gaps in numbers are in these two regions. indicator 1.1.3, appendix
    • Heat and dirty air hit together. A study of about 3.6 million deaths in ten Indian cities found the effect of fine-particle air pollution on deaths was roughly five times higher on the hottest days than on ordinary warm ones. [152]
    • Being active outdoors is getting riskier. Medium human-development countries, where India accounts for most people, had the most hours a year in which brisk walking outdoors carried a heat-stress risk: about 2,430 hours a person. indicator 1.1.2
    • Humidity matters more here than the headline figures show. The heatwave measure used across the report counts temperature only, not humidity. In our reading, in humid Asian cities, where sweat cools the body less well, the real danger on many days is likely greater than the heatwave count suggests. The report’s work-hours and outdoor-activity measures do include humidity. indicator 1.1.1, appendix

    If you work for a city or local government

    What the report found

    • 63.8% of cities that report to the CDP have now assessed their climate risks, up from 44.5% in 2016. Of 6,318 adaptation actions cities reported in 2025, about 9% targeted health; among the health benefits they named, 17.5% were protection from extreme heat or cold. indicator 2.1.3
    • 62.7% of large cities have low or very low greenery. In cities of low human-development countries, green cover fell by 4.1% from the 2015–17 average. Trees, parks and water cool neighbourhoods and support mental health. indicator 2.2.3
    • Air conditioning saves lives, but its waste heat warms the street for people without it, and its electricity can add to air pollution. The report calls for low-energy, fair cooling: active cooling where it is essential, with shade, ventilation and passive design first. indicator 2.2.2

    If you work in health

    What the report found

    • Heat deaths reached a record 710,000 in 2023. Risk is highest for babies, people over 65, people with long-term illness, and during pregnancy. indicator 1.1.5
    • Only 24.2% of WHO member states reported completing a health vulnerability and adaptation assessment since 2020, and 28.9% have a health national adaptation plan. Of those with a plan, only 13 report high or very high implementation; money and staff are the main barriers. indicator 2.1.1, 2.1.2
    • Only 39% of nursing schools surveyed teach anything on climate and health; in low human-development countries the figure is 20%. Nurses are the largest part of the health workforce. indicator 2.2.5
    • In low human-development countries, only 34.6% report strong capacity to manage health emergencies, against 80.6% in the richest. indicator 2.2.4

    If you employ people, or represent workers

    What the report found

    • Heat cost 573 billion potential work hours in 2025. Farm work loses the most hours; its share is falling as farming mechanises, while the share lost in construction and services is rising. indicator 1.1.3
    • The lost hours meant US$1.07 trillion in potential income in 2025, the third year in a row above a trillion. In medium human-development countries, two thirds of the loss falls on farm workers. indicator 4.1.3
    • The report’s numbers mostly cover formal jobs, because data on informal work is thin. In much of Asia many outdoor workers are informal, so in our reading the real loss is likely larger. indicator 1.1.3
    • The report sets a clear target: rules that reduce at-risk workers’ exposure to heat. indicator Panel 7

    If you lead a community group, or look after a household

    What the report found

    • People lost an average of 48 hours of sleep a year to hot nights over 2016–25, and almost every country saw the loss grow. Poor sleep harms health, learning and work the next day. indicator 1.1.4
    • Heatwave exposure is rising fastest for older people and babies: older adults averaged 11.5 heatwave days a year over 2016–25, up from 7.2 the decade before; infants 8.8, up from 5.1. indicator 1.1.1
    • Warnings save lives only if they reach people and they can act on them. The report names the barriers: poor housing, work that cannot stop, living alone, and language. indicator Panel 4

    If you fund, invest or run a business

    What the report found

    • Countries that put a price on protecting health from climate change asked for about US$3.3 billion a year; international funding committed in 2024 covered about a third. The report thinks the real need is higher, because measuring it takes money and staff that many countries lack. indicator 4.3.3
    • International development aid is falling: the report projects a drop of more than US$50 billion between 2023 and 2026, about a quarter of the total. It warns this could slow early-warning systems and climate-resilient clinics. indicator Panel 5
    • Only 1% of climate-and-health research grants went to researchers in low human-development countries; 88% went to the richest. indicator 4.3.4
    • Clean energy investment reached US$2.1 trillion in 2025 and clean energy jobs reached 16.6 million in 2024. The report asks for grant-based and low-cost finance for health, so protection does not add to debt. indicator 4.3.1, 4.2.1

    If you plan or set policy nationally

    What the report found

    • 77.2% of national weather services provide data to their health sector, but only 51.3% offer products tailored to health, such as heat warnings set at health-based thresholds. In low human-development countries the figure is 46.2%. indicator 2.2.1
    • Where countries have health early-warning systems, the number of people affected by floods and storms grew 8.6% less than elsewhere. indicator 2.3.2
    • Warnings work best when they are tied to agreed actions: protected work hours, open cooling spaces, extra hospital staff, checks on people living alone. Where health data is scarce, the report suggests setting warnings using what is known about how bodies cope with heat, for each at-risk group. indicator Panel 4
    • Climate laws that mention health have grown 50-fold since 2000. Of countries’ updated national climate plans (NDCs), 93% mention health, and mentions of heat-related health are rising. indicator 5.5, 5.4.1

    The report’s targets for the next decade

    For the first time, the report sets targets it will track each year. These are the ones that matter most for heat.

    1. By the end of 2027Early warning and response for all major climate health risks in every country, reaching all vulnerable groups and health systems.
    2. By 2030Health adaptation plans, based on evidence of risk, with money and clear responsibilities, in every country and city.
    3. By 2030Climate and health taught in depth in the training of all health professionals.
    4. By 2030Every country able to respond to climate-related health emergencies under the International Health Regulations.
    5. By 2030Cut energy demand by 7%, favouring buildings with healthy indoor temperatures and air.
    6. By 2030Restore 350 million hectares of land and forest, and grow green and blue space in cities.
    7. By 2035Sustainable cooling for all vulnerable people, including every nursing home and health facility: low-carbon, used only where essential, and backed by passive cooling.
    8. No date setRules that reduce at-risk workers’ exposure to heat.
    The report’s six recommendations to governments and global institutions
    1. Recognise climate change as a health emergency, and respond through WHO and UN emergency systems, including with a new WHO instrument.
    2. Make climate and health a top priority in how global institutions are organised and funded.
    3. Treat a fair and rapid cut in greenhouse gases as a health priority, with health professionals helping design the plans.
    4. Make fewer deaths and better health a core goal and measure of success for all climate action.
    5. Build a shared global climate and health data commons, as public infrastructure.
    6. Increase grant-based and low-cost finance for climate and health, in line with international commitments, without adding to debt.

    What the numbers cannot show yet

    The report is open about its limits. These matter most for heat in Asia. They are not reasons to wait; they are places where better data would make protection fairer.

    • Humid heat is undercounted. The heatwave measure uses temperature, not humidity. In humid places the danger to the body can be greater than the count shows.
    • Many heat deaths in South Asia are estimated, not counted. The mortality model learns from death records in 129 countries. In the part of the appendix we could read, the Philippines, Sri Lanka and Thailand supplied records; we did not find Bangladesh, India, Indonesia or Nepal. Their figures rest on models built from other places. Better death registration is itself a way to break the invisible-deaths cycle.
    • Informal work is mostly missing. Lost work hours are counted mainly for formal jobs, so the losses for street vendors, day labourers and home-based workers are likely larger than reported.
    • Who is hit hardest is hard to see. Data is rarely broken down by gender, income, ethnicity or age, so the report cannot show these differences well.
    • Mental health and care costs are not yet tracked. The report says both are gaps and has opened funding calls to build indicators.
    • Each risk is counted alone. Heat, smoke, drought and floods often come together and make each other worse; the report says the combined harm is probably much larger than any single number.
    • The research comes mostly from elsewhere. Only about 10% of climate-and-health papers have co-authors based in low or medium human-development countries.

    The cycles on the map these findings speak to

    The map shows how heat’s harms feed each other. Each finding below adds evidence to one of its vicious cycles, and each cycle links to solutions that can break it.

    • The invisible-deaths loopHeat deaths that are never recorded, so the problem looks smaller than it is (1.1.5).
    • The sleepless-nights loop48 hours of sleep lost a year to hot nights (1.1.4).
    • The poverty–heat trapUS$1.07 trillion in lost income, most of it in poorer countries (4.1.3).
    • The cooling trapAir conditioning saves lives and adds heat and emissions (2.2.2).
    • The overwhelmed-clinic loopHealth emergency capacity is weakest where risk is highest (2.2.4).
    • The dying-green loopMost large cities have little greenery, and it is falling in the poorest (2.2.3).
    • The energy-poverty trap2.1 billion people still rely on polluting fuels at home (3.1.2).
    • The farm-debt loopHeat and drought added 127.9 million people to food insecurity in 2024 (1.4).
    • The protection-gap loopHealth adaptation money covers about a third of declared needs (4.3.3).
    • The reactive-spending loopEarly warning reduces how many people are affected (2.3.2).
    • The wildfire loopMost countries saw more days of very high wildfire risk (1.2.1).
    • The global cooling loopHigher demand for cooling drove about 80% of the rise in energy emissions in 2024 (3.1.1).
