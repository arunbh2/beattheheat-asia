    Organisations working on extreme heat

    330 organisations across Asia and the world already working on parts of the web of extreme heat. Inclusion describes relevant published work. It does not imply partnership, affiliation or endorsement.

    Open the interactive network

    Each organisation is marked with its main side in a heat alliance. Demand: Holds the need or the duty to protect people: governments and public agencies, employers, and organisations of the workers and communities affected. Supply: Provides what protection needs: technology, services and delivery on the ground, insurance, lending and grants, research and data. Platform: Connects demand and supply: networks, alliances and conveners, and the bodies that set shared rules or standards for a market. Many play more than one part; this is the main one.

    Global networks and organisations

    • Global Heat Health Information Network (WHO, WMO and NOAA) (Platform): A member-driven forum of scientists, practitioners and policymakers. Its resource library gathers guidance on heat warnings, heat action plans and case studies, heat at work and heat in cities.
    • International Labour Organization (Platform): Sets out the evidence and standards for protecting workers from heat stress.
    • UNICEF (Platform): Tracks how heatwaves and other climate hazards disrupt children’s schooling.
    • Zurich Climate Resilience Alliance (Platform): An alliance of humanitarian, development and research partners with Zurich Insurance Group. In 2024 it expanded from floods to extreme heat, with work in countries including Bangladesh and Nepal. Its 2026 study of heat early warning systems covered eight countries, among them Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, the Philippines and Vietnam.
    • Heat Resilience Action (HERA), formerly Climate Resilience for All (Platform): Works on extreme heat with a focus on women and low-income communities. With SEWA and Swiss Re, it combined insurance payouts and cash for lost income after India’s 2024 heatwaves.
    • UNEP Cool Coalition (Platform): A global network for clean, efficient cooling and cooler cities. With the COP30 Presidency, it runs Beat the Heat, an initiative supporting cities in the Global South with heat assessments and cooling action.
    • Red Cross Red Crescent Climate Centre (Supply): Wrote the Heatwave Guide for Cities with more than 25 partners, covering how cities prepare for, respond to and learn from heatwaves.
    • Sustainable Energy for All (Platform): Measures who lacks access to cooling through its Chilling Prospects series.
    • International Energy Agency (Platform): Analyses cooling demand and how efficiency standards can cut it.
    • Practical Action (Supply): Connects heat risk intelligence, local action, practical technology, institutions and finance, following a Sense → Decide → Act → Sustain logic. In Nepal, Dhangadhi’s heat action plan links temperature, humidity and health data, hotspot mapping and community views to thresholds and set municipal responses. In Bangladesh, heat is being built into early warning and livelihood work. A partner in the Zurich Climate Resilience Alliance.
    • People’s Courage International (Supply): Works on safe migration and labour protection for migrant workers in South and Southeast Asia. Its Migrants Resilience Collaborative supports more than seven million households.
    • World Bank (Supply): Its 2026 report A Livable Future finds extreme heat already costs South Asia the equivalent of about 31 million full-time jobs a year, and sets out how cities can protect jobs and growth.
    • World Meteorological Organization (Platform): With WHO, runs the South Asia Climate–Health Desk with India’s meteorological agencies, turning weather and climate information into health protection.
    • C40 Cities (Platform): Its Cool Cities Accelerator brings together cities including Ahmedabad, Bengaluru, Mumbai and Singapore on early warnings, building standards and tree cover.
    • Vital Strategies (Supply): Through the Partnership for Healthy Cities, supports Bangkok to pilot heat-health action in high-risk districts: outdoor worker protection, cooling spaces and drinking water.
    • Global Buildings Performance Network (Supply): Works on residential building codes in India and Southeast Asia, where demand for cooling homes is rising fastest.
    • Start Network (Platform): Its members run heatwave anticipatory action in Pakistan, releasing funds automatically when forecasts cross agreed triggers.
    • Mercy Corps (Supply): A Zurich Climate Resilience Alliance partner leading work on extreme heat in Nepal, where the southern plains passed 40 °C on 50 days in 2024.
    • United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) (Platform): Helps governments connect heat resilience with health systems, urban planning and finance. Its work includes a financing strategy for tackling urban heat in Bangkok.
    • UN-Habitat (Platform): Develops guidance on urban heat management, helping cities combine planning, building design, green infrastructure and cooling to protect vulnerable residents.
    • UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) (Platform): Helps governments strengthen heat risk governance through practical assessment tools, coordinated planning and investment across sectors and levels of government.
    • Resilient Cities Network (Platform): Works with cities and communities on heat resilience, including indoor heat monitoring and locally designed cooling in Southeast Asian cities.
    • ICLEI South Asia (Platform): Supports cities with heat risk assessments, heat action plans, urban greening and preparedness tools in India, Nepal and Bangladesh.
    • United Cities and Local Governments Asia-Pacific (UCLG ASPAC) (Platform): Provides technical assistance for urban heat resilience, including work with Iloilo City in the Philippines and Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh.
    • Women in Informal Employment: Globalizing and Organizing (WIEGO) (Demand): Documents how heat affects informal workers’ health and earnings, and develops worker education, policy recommendations and protection guidance with local organisations.
    • International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC) (Platform): Mobilises national societies and communities to prepare for heatwaves and reach people at risk, including through its global Heat Action Day campaign.
    • Climate and Development Knowledge Network (CDKN) (Platform): Supports locally relevant climate knowledge and partnerships, including heat early warning, urban health resilience and preparedness planning in India.
    • United for Efficiency (UNEP-led) (Platform): Helps countries strengthen appliance efficiency policies and standards, including work across Southeast Asia on efficient, climate-friendly air conditioning.
    • Humanity & Inclusion (HI) (Supply): Sets out disability-inclusive approaches to climate action, including accessible early warnings, which people with disabilities often lack. A heat-specific programme in Asia is not confirmed.
    • Human Rights Watch (Supply): Documents how extreme heat endangers older people and people with disabilities, and how heat and climate plans leave them out.
    • World Health Organization (Platform): Supports Indonesian heat-health coordination and publishes occupational heat guidance with WMO.
    • The Conduit Foundation (Platform): Develops an urban-heat workstream connecting city leaders and finance.
    • International Union of Food, Agricultural, Hotel, Restaurant, Catering, Tobacco and Allied Workers’ Associations (IUF) (Demand): Calls for negotiated heat protection covering migrant, seasonal and informal workers.
    • Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (Platform): Co-produced the extreme heat and agriculture report.
    • ICLEI: Local Governments for Sustainability (Platform): Supports urban heat governance and heat-relevant planning tools.
    • UN Environment Programme (Platform): Convenes heat-resilience and sustainable-cooling initiatives.
    • Freetown City Council (Demand): Participates in the C40 Cool Cities Accelerator for heat protection and urban adaptation.
    • City of Athens (Demand): Participates in the C40 Cool Cities Accelerator for heat protection and urban adaptation.
    • International Organization for Standardization (Platform): Publishes the WBGT-based occupational heat-stress screening standard.

    • National Disaster Management Authority, India (Demand): Issues national guidance for state and city heat action plans.
    • National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority, Nepal (Demand): Issued Nepal’s first federal heat plan in 2024.
    • Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation, with IIPH Gandhinagar and NRDC (Demand): Launched South Asia’s first heat action plan in 2013: early warnings, public outreach and training for medical staff.
    • Dhaka North City Corporation (Demand): Appointed Asia’s first Chief Heat Officer in 2023, focused on cooling the poorest neighbourhoods.
    • Nepal Red Cross Society (Supply): Published the Dhangadhi Heat Action Plan in 2025 with the Climate Centre, setting out when and where to act before and during heatwave days in a city that passes 40 °C every year.
    • Department of Education, Philippines (Demand): Lets school heads suspend in-person classes when the heat index is dangerous, and moved the school year back to a June start to keep classes out of the hottest months.
    • Bangladesh Red Crescent Society (Supply): Runs an early action protocol for heat, first activated in April 2024 during Dhaka’s longest recorded heatwave.
    • ICIMOD (Platform): Monitors the Himalaya’s snow and ice, and helps brick kilns switch to cleaner technology that cuts the soot darkening the glaciers.
    • Asian Development Bank (Supply): Supports heat resilience in Asian cities, including technical assistance on heat stress among women and girls in Bangladesh, Cambodia, Pakistan, Sri Lanka and Tajikistan. Its 2026 report on the heat crisis in Thailand and Southeast Asia calls for mobilising public and private finance, and proposes a heat action plan for Thailand.
    • UN ESCAP (Platform): Helps fast-growing cities in South and Southeast Asia plan for extreme urban heat, working with Iloilo City and Cox’s Bazar.
    • India Meteorological Department and IITM Pune (Demand): Issue India’s heatwave forecasts and work with WHO and WMO on the South Asia Climate–Health Desk.
    • PAGASA, Philippines (Demand): Issues heat index warnings with danger levels that local governments use to decide when to suspend classes.
    • Ministry of Manpower, Singapore (Demand): Requires hourly wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) checks on outdoor worksites, shaded rest breaks when heat stress is high, and time for new workers to acclimatise, and enforces the rules.

    • National Environment Agency, Singapore (Demand): Issues public heat stress advisories through myENV and weather services, so people can adjust outdoor activity as heat rises.

    Research and evidence

    • Council on Energy, Environment and Water (CEEW) (Supply): Assessed heat risk in all 734 districts of India in 2025, finding 57% of districts, home to three in four Indians, at high or very high risk. Its 2026 heat-health reports for the GHHIN South Asia hub cover India, Nepal, Bangladesh and Bhutan, and it helped Thane build its heat action plan.
    • Lancet Countdown (Supply): Tracks heat deaths, lost working hours and other health impacts of climate change every year.
    • NUS Heat Resilience & Performance Centre (Platform): Hosts the Southeast Asia Hub of the Global Heat Health Information Network, bringing governments and partners together on heat and health.
    • Cornell Global Labor Institute (Supply): With Schroders, mapped heat and flood risk in garment hubs including Dhaka, Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Phnom Penh, and found few fashion brands require heat protection in their supplier codes.
    • International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) (Supply): Showed from its Philippine farm that warmer nights cut rice yields, and breeds rice that copes better with heat.
    • icddr,b (Supply): Works with Bangladesh’s Department of Agricultural Extension and others on HeatShield, which helps farmers cope with extreme heat.
    • BRAC James P Grant School of Public Health (Supply): Part of a South Asian partnership with CEEW, NRDC, UN ESCAP and the Global Heat Health Information Network assessing heat-health action across countries.
    • IISER Pune (Supply): Leads the South Asia Scientific Research Consortium on how heat affects different populations, with Rockefeller Foundation support.
    • International Institute for Environment and Development (IIED) (Supply): Has researched the health and economic toll of heat stress on informal workers in urban Bangladesh.
    • Heat and Health Research Centre, University of Sydney (Supply): Tested low-cost cooling for Bangladeshi garment workers: fans, drinking water and reflective roofs recovered much of the 12–15% output lost to heat.
    • Salata Institute, Harvard University (Supply): Published Critical Perspectives on Extreme Heat in India in 2026, covering heat action plans, insurance and informal work.
    • WRI India (Supply): Works on heat mitigation planning with Indian cities, including greening and cooling pilots in Kochi and Mumbai and the Bengaluru Heat Collective.
    • Good Business Lab (Supply): Researching heat, health and gender in India’s and Bangladesh’s garment sector, 2025–2027.
    • Integrated Research and Action for Development (IRADe) (Supply): Develops heat hotspot assessments and gender-sensitive heat action plans in India, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.
    • Indian Institute for Human Settlements (IIHS) (Supply): Researches urban heat and tests passive and low-energy cooling shelters that give outdoor workers safe places to rest and recover.
    • Center for Study of Science, Technology and Policy (CSTEP) (Supply): Builds heat intelligence and decision-support tools for Indian cities, including the BHRIGU National Heat Insights Explorer.
    • The Energy and Resources Institute (TERI) (Supply): Researches thermal comfort, efficient buildings and sustainable cooling, combining passive design with better cooling equipment.
    • Sustainable Futures Collaborative (Supply): Studies heat action plans and their implementation gaps, and how policy, finance and social protection can make heat resilience last.
    • Karachi Urban Lab (Supply): Researches how low-income residents and informal workers in Karachi live with heat, including access to shade, cooling, water and reliable infrastructure.
    • Singapore Management University, Urban Institute (Supply): Studies urban heat vulnerability and cooling through the Cooling Singapore collaboration, linking neighbourhood conditions to climate-sensitive planning.
    • Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST) (Supply): Researches urban heat, ventilation, tree canopy and building cooling in dense cities.
    • London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (Supply): Researches heat-related health risks and adaptation, including ways to protect pregnant women and newborns, with partners in Africa and Pakistan.
    • Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (Supply): Studies occupational heat exposure among workers in India, including physiological strain, dehydration, kidney health and productivity.
    • CEPT University, Centre for Advanced Research in Building Science and Energy (Supply): Tests building and cooling performance, including work with RMI on air conditioners that cool efficiently in real heat and humidity.
    • Heat Island Group, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Supply): Researches reflective roofs and pavements, urban trees and other measures that cool buildings and cities while cutting energy use.
    • Heat Policy Innovation Hub, Duke University (Platform): Brings researchers, governments and communities together on heat policy, including adaptable heat action planning tools.
    • CIMMYT (International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center) (Supply): Breeds heat-tolerant maize for rainfed smallholder farms with national research institutes and seed companies. Hybrids are now grown in Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal and Pakistan.
    • ICRISAT (Supply): Improves the heat and drought tolerance of dryland crops such as pearl millet, sorghum and chickpea, which feed rainfed farming households across South Asia.
    • Nature Conservation Foundation (Supply): Has monitored Lakshadweep’s reefs since 1998 and links their decline to repeated marine heatwaves, with consequences for island fishing communities.
    • BRAC University: James P Grant School of Public Health (Supply): Contributes heat-health country evidence to the South Asia hub.
    • Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (Platform): Hosts the South Asia Climate and Health Desk linked to the heat-health hub.
    • Universitas Indonesia (Supply): Serves as a local implementing partner in KATALIS-HEAT.
    • Hasanuddin University (Supply): Serves as a local implementing partner in KATALIS-HEAT.

    Working on the ground

    • Self Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) (Demand): Protects women informal workers’ income in heatwaves. After the May 2024 heatwaves, more than 46,000 members received insurance payouts.
    • Mahila Housing Trust (Supply): Cools homes in informal settlements with reflective and modular roofs, and runs heat income insurance for women workers.
    • ADRA India (Supply): Ran an anticipatory heatwave response in 2025 with national and district authorities.
    • HomeNet Thailand (Demand): With WIEGO, surveyed more than 1,000 informal workers in Bangkok on heat: nearly 80% had cut their hours. A trusted link between workers and authorities.
    • All India Disaster Mitigation Institute (AIDMI) (Supply): An Ahmedabad-based institute that helps Indian states, districts and cities turn heat warnings into local action. It supports local heat action planning, surveyed 121 street vendors, auto drivers and construction and domestic workers in Lucknow on how heat hits their work, offers Afat Vimo disaster microinsurance to low-income families, and runs a regular southasiadisasters.net series on extreme heat.
    • SEEDS (Supply): Combines building-level heat risk mapping, community preparedness and affordable cool roofs for vulnerable urban settlements in India.
    • SELCO Foundation (Supply): Develops sustainable energy and cooling for underserved communities, including thermally comfortable buildings, cool roofs and decentralised cold storage.
    • HeatWatch India (Supply): Focuses on heat and worker protection through research, heat exposure mapping, practical education and a public directory of cooling centres.
    • Chintan Environmental Research and Action Group (Supply): Works with waste picker communities on heat awareness, hotspot mapping and practical ways to cut heat exposure at home and at work.
    • Hasiru Dala (Demand): Works with waste workers in Bengaluru to document heat-related health and income losses and press for water, shade, safer housing and workplace protection.
    • Janpahal (Demand): Works with informal worker organisations and WIEGO on heat protection, worker education and policy dialogue for street vendors.
    • Jan Sahas (Supply): Works with migrant and informal workers on community-led heat resilience, and funds premiums for a temperature-triggered heat cover with Go Digit and K.M. Dastur.
    • Save the Children International (Supply): Addresses heat risks to children through awareness, drinking water and advocacy on education and health, including heat-related school closures in Bangladesh.
    • Watershed Organisation Trust (WOTR) (Supply): Sends heat-health advisories by SMS to farmers, farm labourers and rural job-scheme workers in drought-prone Marathwada, built with the India Meteorological Department, alongside its watershed work.
    • BRAC (Supply): In Bangladesh’s April 2024 heat emergency, mobilised 50,000 community health workers to raise awareness, with a focus on families with pregnant women, and is piloting heat-reduction ideas in urban informal settlements.
    • HelpAge International (Supply): Works with older people’s associations in Bangladesh and Pakistan on disaster risk, including heatwaves and early warning, and speaks up for older farm labourers and street vendors exposed to heat.
    • Centre for Disability in Development (CDD), with CBM (Supply): Runs disability-inclusive disaster risk and climate adaptation in Bangladesh, including coastal sub-districts, where heatwaves are among the hazards. A heat-specific programme is not confirmed.
    • Rujak Center for Urban Studies (Supply): Leads Be-Housing research on urban-poor housing and heat adaptation.
    • Kota Kita Foundation (Supply): Partners in Be-Housing research on urban-poor settlements and heat adaptation.
    • Shri Kashi Vishwanath Temple Trust (Demand): Participated in the Varanasi heat action plan launch.
    • HomeNet South Asia (Demand): Brings home-based-worker perspectives to heat and climate policy dialogue.
    • British Red Cross (Supply): Supported research and preparation for Dhangadhi’s heat action plan.

    Banks, regulators and microfinance

    • Monetary Authority of Singapore (Platform): Its 2020 environmental risk guidelines expect banks to assess physical climate risk, including through scenario analysis and stress tests. Its Singapore-Asia Taxonomy (2023) covers emission cuts only, so there are no criteria yet for finance that helps people adapt to heat.
    • Bank of Thailand (Platform): A 2023 policy statement asks banks to build environmental and climate risk into governance, strategy, risk management and disclosure. It has worked with the World Bank on assessing physical climate risk in Thai banks’ loan books.
    • Otoritas Jasa Keuangan (Financial Services Authority), Indonesia (Platform): Its climate risk guideline for banks (2024, refined in 2026) includes data on Indonesia’s physical climate hazards. A 2022 rule lets banks restructure loans for borrowers in areas it designates as disaster-hit: after the 2025 Sumatra floods, Rp12.58 trillion for 237,083 borrowers. Heat has not yet been designated.
    • Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (Platform): Since 2021, banks must check whether a borrower’s collateral or income is exposed to climate hazards. In areas under a state of calamity, banks can give borrowers up to six months’ grace with no interest on interest or penalties, longer for farm loans. It also eased rules for lenders in drought-hit areas during the 2015–16 El Niño.
    • State Bank of Vietnam (Platform): Requires banks to assess environmental risk before lending to projects (since 2023). After Typhoon Yagi in 2024, it let lenders reschedule repayments for affected borrowers across 26 provinces, with final repayment allowed until the end of 2027.
    • Bangladesh Bank (Platform): Steers credit to farmers after climate shocks through its refinancing schemes. In June 2026 it told banks to prioritise farmers hit by heavy rain. Heat is not yet among the shocks that trigger this support.
    • Nepal Rastra Bank (Platform): Sets environmental and social risk rules for banks (2018), and since October 2024 a green finance taxonomy that sorts lending into green, amber and red.
    • Reserve Bank of India (Platform): Its 2018 directions let banks reschedule loans and pause repayments in areas hit by notified calamities, which in India now include heatwaves. A 2024 draft framework would require banks to disclose their climate-related financial risks.
    • Sustainable Banking and Finance Network (Platform): A network of financial regulators and banking associations from emerging markets, supported by IFC. Its policy library holds the climate and environmental risk rules of central banks across South and Southeast Asia.
    • KOMIDA, with Build Change (Indonesia) (Supply): One of Indonesia’s largest microfinance institutions, piloting small home-improvement loans for climate adaptation with Build Change, aimed at women earning under US$5 a day and paired with digital advice.
    • Center for Financial Inclusion (Supply): Researches how small loans and parametric insurance can help low-income people get through heatwaves.
    • KOMIDA (Supply): Offers incremental housing adaptation loans with heat retrofit support.
