# Who shares the heat: one market through one hot season

A worked example of how a hot day ties lives together through shared places, work and services, why people sharing the heat do not share the same power over it, and what makes protection last beyond one season.

## Connected by the heat

On a hot afternoon at a market, whether a porter can rest depends on whether leaving the queue costs a paid load. Whether a vendor has water depends on who orders the refill. Whether a child can learn depends on the classroom, and whether a parent keeps a shift depends on the school's hours. And whether any of them recovers overnight depends on water, electricity and the home they go back to.

Heat ties these lives together through the places, work, institutions and services they share. Common Shade calls such groups communities of fate: people whose wellbeing becomes connected through shared conditions, even when their exposure, money, rights and say in decisions are far from equal. The connection can run beyond one street, along a commuting route, a labour contract, a supply chain or a public service.

Building resilience, in this sense, means giving people a greater say in those shared conditions and making protection dependable: something a household can count on, not a project that ends with its funding. [What resilience means here](https://beattheheat.asia/approach/#resilience).

A lens, not a findingA way of looking, not a measured finding. Common Shade uses the phrase as a lens and does not trace it to a single thinker or theory.

## Shared heat, unequal ground

People in the same street, market or workplace share the same hot afternoon, but not the same exposure, money, rights or say over what changes. Shared consequences do not make responsibility equal, and they do not create agreement on their own. Protection tends to hold better when the people most exposed take a real part in decisions: organising, negotiating, and bringing what they know about their own day.

## One market through one hot season

FictionalThe town, the people and every event here are made up, and continue the market town of The Heat Assembly. The relationships they illustrate are drawn from the sources behind that game and the pages linked below; no figure here is a measurement.

- Shared exposure

A hot season is forecast for the market town. The public hall beside the market is shaded and open, and almost empty. The loading lane, the stalls, the tea stall at the bay and the homes around the square will all take the same heat.

- Different constraints

The same heat, five different reasons the hall does not help. The municipal officer controls the hall and a small budget; the market association controls the loading order; the porters have no seat at first.

- **Tara, a porter paid per load** loses about two loads by leaving the queue to rest.
- **Jeevan, a porter on a day rate** loses nothing by resting, so is the person others ask to cover the loads.
- **Noor, a stitcher paid per piece, caring for a child of three** can work only while the childcare centre is open; nothing on offer cools Noor's home.
- **Kiran, a stall holder** can leave the stall in the afternoon lull, not at the midday peak.
- **Hira, an older tea seller** cannot walk to the hall in the heat; a rickshaw costs a day's money.

- A collective decision

Before the season, the market association calls a meeting with the municipal officer, the neighbourhood services coordinator and a workers' representative, invited after porters raised the queue. Traders object to paying for cover; the association agrees to keep places in the queue only with a day's notice. After two meetings they agree four things: places are kept for anyone who rests, with a paid relief porter on alert days; the hall opens at midday and in the afternoon; a water point and shade go to the loading bay; and childcare keeps its hours on alert days.

Where they disagreeThey do not agree on everything. The traders accept the cost of relief cover for one season only, and the workers' representative wanted it every hot day, not only on alert days.

- Who gains access

- **Tara** can rest at midday on alert days without losing a place.
- **Kiran** rests in the hall in the afternoon.
- **Hira** gets water and shade at the bay, where the work is, rather than at the hall.
- **Noor** keeps the childcare hours, and the paid work that depends on them.
- **Jeevan** is no longer the unpaid cover, because the relief porter is paid.

- What remains

- Noor's home is still hot at night. Housing sits with another department, which was not at the meeting.
- Relief cover is funded for one season. Who pays next year is not agreed.
- On hot days without an official alert, Tara still pays for a break in lost loads.
- Nobody yet records who used the hall, at what hour, so the next meeting cannot see who it missed.

- Learning for the next season

- Record use of the hall and the water point by hour, and ask who could not come and why.
- Move relief cover from a one-season allocation into the municipal budget, or into the market's own fees.
- Invite the housing department, and ask what would cool the homes around the square at night.
- Agree in advance what changes on a hot day without an alert, so protection does not wait for the warning.

## What keeps protection working

A shade, a water point or a rest break protects people only while it keeps working. Five things usually decide whether it does:

- **Ownership and authority**Someone holds the site and can say yes, and everyone knows who.
- **Operating money and maintenance**Money for water, cleaning and repairs through the season, not only to build.
- **Staffing and access**Someone opens it at the hours people need it, and people can reach it: step-free, close to their work, without losing pay or a place.
- **A say for workers and residents**The people who use it help decide where it goes and when it opens, and can raise a problem without risk to their work.
- **Monitoring, and a way to fix failures**A way to report a failure, someone who fixes it, and a record of how long it took.

## The same lens, three other places

Each is an illustration. The pages linked hold the evidence and the actions.

- **A school****Who shares it:** Pupils, teachers, support staff and families.Classroom heat affects all of them at once. If a school shortens its hours on hot days, care moves home and a parent can lose a shift. Opening a school to the neighbourhood in the holidays needs its own permission, staffing and safeguarding.**Who can shape it:** Children, meal staff and teachers know where the heat is worst; the School Management Committee can help choose what changes.[A school at lunchtime](https://beattheheat.asia/el-nino-and-cities/#school) · [School actions](https://beattheheat.asia/practical-actions/#schools)
- **A rented neighbourhood****Who shares it:** Tenants, landlords, the power utility and the municipality.Whether a room cools at night depends on a roof the tenant does not own, power the utility supplies and trees the municipality plants. A landlord can pass the cost of a cooler roof into the rent.**Who can shape it:** Tenants' groups can say which rooms stay hottest and when the power goes; that knowledge can set priorities for repairs.[The sleepless-nights loop](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#sleep) · [Home actions](https://beattheheat.asia/practical-actions/#homes)
- **A workplace and its supply chain****Who shares it:** Workers, contractors, employers and the buyers they supply.A buyer's deadline sets the pace of work in the heat; a contractor decides whether a break costs pay. When protection is not paid for up the chain, the worker pays for it.**Who can shape it:** Workers' organisations can negotiate rest, shade and timing, and report what actually happens on site.[The poverty and heat trap](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#poverty) · [Live through a worker's week](https://beattheheat.asia/a-week-in-the-heat/)
