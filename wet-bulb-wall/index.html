    Heat and the body

    The wet-bulb wall

    Move the controls to explore how hot air and moisture combine. As humidity rises, evaporation becomes less effective at cooling the body.

    What this isThis explores environmental conditions. It does not predict an individual’s survival or establish safe working conditions.

    Three guided explorations

    A. What changes when the air becomes more humid?

    Hold the air at 35 °C and raise the humidity. At 30% relative humidity the calculated wet-bulb temperature is 21.5 °C; at 50% it is 26.1 °C; at 70%, 30.1 °C; at 90%, 33.5 °C. The air is no hotter, but sweat evaporates less readily, so the body loses its main way of shedding heat.

    B. Different combinations, similar wet-bulb temperature

    Hypothetical: 45 °C at 20% gives 25.2 °C wet-bulb; 32 °C at 58% gives 25.2 °C. Similar numbers, different strain: in the hot, dry air the body also gains heat directly from the air and skin is hotter, which is why chamber studies found lower limits in hot, dry conditions than in humid ones.

    C. Explore from a city

    Choose a city above, then “Explore from these conditions”. Its values are copied into a separate hypothetical scenario; the city’s own marker stays where the data put it.

    Same weather. Different ability to escape it.

    Illustrative situations, not real people. Temperature and humidity describe the air; whether someone can rest, cool down or stop work depends on much more. No group shares one biological limit, and nothing here assigns one.

    A street vendor whose earnings depend on staying outside

    What temperature and humidity capture
    The air around the stall.
    What they miss
    Sun on the street, heat off the road, the effort of moving stock, and how many hours there are no customers in shade.
    What decides whether protection is usable
    Whether a day off or a shaded pitch costs a day’s income, and whether water and a toilet are nearby.
    Who can change it
    Municipal markets and street-vending rules, social protection and heat-linked income support, local heat plans.

    A garment worker facing indoor heat and production targets

    What temperature and humidity capture
    Neither, directly: a weather reading describes outdoor air, not the factory floor.
    What they miss
    Machine and roof heat, airflow, the pace set by targets, clothing and the length of the shift.

    The weather on the way, not the load carried.

    Who could help a household through a hot season →

    Explanatory pathways from the systems map

    How heat can carry into income and recovery. These come from the map’s cycles and their evidence, not from this simulator.

    • The poverty–heat trap established evidencePeople who can’t afford to stop working fall ill. Illness costs income, and lost income makes stopping even less affordable.
    • The sleepless-nights loop established evidenceHot nights wreck sleep. Poor sleep frays mental health, and stress makes sleep harder still.
    • The protection-gap loop emerging evidenceAs insurers retreat, public budgets absorb the losses, protection is cut, and the risk drives insurers further away.

    What changes exposure and protection?

    Some changes alter the conditions; others decide whether people can use them. None is a switch that subtracts wet-bulb degrees, drinking water does not cancel dangerous heat, and fans do not help in every condition. Each action below links to its evidence and what to watch for.

    Environment

    Shade, less radiant heat and cooler indoor space change the conditions themselves.

    • Cool by designShading, ventilation, reflective roofs and efficient cooling cut the need for air conditioning, and the waste heat it dumps outside.
    • Green, cool citiesTrees, water, cool roofs and cool pavements lower city temperatures and flatten peak demand.
    • Cool, affordable housingUpgrading informal settlements with ventilation, shade and cool roofs, and planning homes for people who arrive.Watch for: In 16 Ahmedabad homes compared over one week, most roof treatments lowered daytime indoor heat, but one coating left rooms hotter than untreated roofs. Designs differ, and night-time effects were not measured.

    Work arrangements

    Paid rest, changed hours and lighter loads change how much heat the body makes and for how long.

    • Heat-safe workRest, shade and water during dangerous heat, with paid breaks so that stopping work doesn’t mean losing pay.Watch for: Protections work only when workers can use them. In Hong Kong, some outdoor cleaners given cooling vests were stopped from wearing them because the vests covered a company logo; in Cambodia, researchers found workers whose unions had bargained over heat exposure better protected, as Oxfam America reports.

    Services

    Cooling spaces, water and reliable power only help when people can reach and use them.

    • Cooling centres and check-insPublic cooling spaces, water points and check-ins on older people living alone can offer relief in a heatwave. How much they protect health depends on who can reach them, how long people stay, and how hot it is once they leave.Watch for: A 2025 review found no study that measured health outcomes for people using cooling centres; in simulated heatwaves, the benefits were short-lived. That is not evidence of no benefit.
    • Affordable cooling for the poorestLifeline electricity tariffs, cool roofs and cooling suited to the local heat bring safer cooling within reach of households that cannot afford it. Which kind helps depends on the climate and the person: a fan that helps in humid heat may not help in very hot, dry air.Watch for: In laboratory trials with older adults, fans slightly cooled the body in hot, humid air but raised body temperature in very hot, dry air. These were short trials, not homes.
    • Heat-ready health servicesCooling, backup power, surge plans and trained frontline workers keep clinics working through heatwaves.

    Institutions

    Income protection, employer duties and local heat plans decide whether stopping is possible.

    • Income protection in heatwavesCash that pays out automatically when temperatures cross a danger threshold, so people can afford to stop. Triggers need careful design to limit basis risk: heat that hurts people without triggering a payout.Watch for: A payout trigger set too high can pay nothing: the first year of a heat insurance scheme for informal women workers in India paid nothing for that reason, until the threshold was redesigned.
    • Social protection floorsBasic income security that keeps households above the line between one hot season and the next.
    • Heat action plansEarly warnings, public cooling spaces and outreach to the people most at risk prevent illness before it reaches hospital.Watch for: Plans are often sized to the worst heatwave in memory. A 2026 modelling study found that even with low emissions, 6 to 11% of future heatwaves in India and Pakistan, Europe and North Africa could outlast each region’s recent record heatwave; with high emissions, close to 1 in 5 in India and Pakistan.

    Evidence and method

    Words that are not interchangeable
    Air temperature
    What a shaded thermometer reads (the “dry-bulb” temperature).
    Relative humidity
    How much water vapour the air holds, as a share of the most it could hold at that temperature.
    Wet-bulb temperature
    The temperature air would cool to by evaporating water into it. It combines temperature and humidity, and equals air temperature when the air is saturated. This page calculates it; it ignores sun and wind.
    Wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT)
    A workplace heat index that adds sun (radiant heat) and wind. It cannot be calculated from temperature and humidity alone, so this page does not show it. Glossary: WBGT.
    Heat index
    An estimate of how hot it feels from temperature and humidity, used by some weather services; a different scale again.
    Loss of heat balance
    When the body gains heat faster than it can lose it and core temperature keeps rising. What the chamber studies measured.
    Heat illness and survival
    Outcomes that depend on the person, their health, effort, clothing, exposure time and care. Not calculated here.

    What the studies measured

    Sherwood and Huber, 2010: a theoretical limit

    What it is
    A modelling and theory paper. It argues that wet-bulb temperatures above 35 °C sustained for extended periods would make it impossible for the body to shed metabolic heat.
    Measured or modelled
    Modelled and reasoned from physics; not tested on people.
    What it supports here
    Why 35 °C wet-bulb became a widely cited upper reference. Not a threshold that conditions below it are safe.

    An adaptability limit to climate change due to heat stress (opens in a new tab) · Sherwood SC, Huber M; PNAS, 2010

    Vecellio and colleagues, 2022: testing the 35 °C idea

    Who and what
    24 young, healthy adults (11 men, 13 women) in an environmental chamber, light clothing, very low effort (about 10 watts of pedalling), with core temperature tracked as humidity or temperature was raised step by step.
    Endpoint
    The point at which core temperature began to rise steadily: heat balance could no longer be maintained. Not illness, and not survival.
    Finding
    In humid conditions the critical wet-bulb temperature averaged 30.55 ± 0.98 °C; in hotter, drier conditions it was lower still. No one reached 35 °C.
    What it supports here
    That a single wet-bulb number cannot describe the limit across climates. Its participants were young and healthy, so the values do not describe older people or people working hard.

    Evaluating the 35°C wet-bulb temperature adaptability threshold for young, healthy subjects (PSU HEAT Project) (opens in a new tab) · Vecellio DJ, Wolf ST, Cottle RM, Kenney WL; Journal of Applied Physiology, 2022

    Wolf and colleagues, 2023: older adults

    Who and what
    51 young (23 ± 4 years) and 49 older (71 ± 6 years) adults in an environmental chamber, at seated rest or minimal activity, with the same rising-heat protocol.
    Endpoint
    The combination of temperature and humidity above which heat balance could not be maintained.
    Finding
    Limits were lower for older than for young adults during minimal activity, and lower during minimal activity than at rest.
    Conditions
    An indoor chamber with no source of radiant heat and little air movement; participants from central Pennsylvania. Outdoors, sun and wind change the picture.
    What it supports here
    The optional overlays on the chart: the measured mean limits, drawn only across the conditions tested.

    Heat stress vulnerability and critical environmental limits for older adults (opens in a new tab) · Wolf ST, Cottle RM, Fisher KG, Vecellio DJ, Kenney WL; Communications Earth & Environment, 2023

    Wang and colleagues, 2026: longer exposure

    Who and what
    36 unacclimatised healthy young adults, seated light office work, unrestricted drinking water, held at wet-bulb temperatures of 32, 33, 34 and 35 °C for up to 8 hours.
    Finding
    32 to 33 °C was compensable for these participants; 34 to 35 °C was not. At the same wet-bulb temperature, different temperature and humidity mixes gave similar core temperature responses in shade.
    Why protocols matter
    Stepwise rising heat (Vecellio, Wolf) and long steady exposure (Wang) answer different questions. The newer study does not cancel the earlier ones; together they show how much the limit depends on who, how long and how hard.
    What it does not support here
    Its projected tolerance times are for its participants and conditions; this page does not turn temperature and humidity into survival times.

    Human heat tolerance limits under prolonged exposure to extreme heat (opens in a new tab) · Wang F, Xu Y, Wang H and others; Environment International, 2026

    How the wet-bulb temperature is calculated

    Psychrometric (thermodynamic) wet-bulb temperature, from the equations in the ASHRAE Handbook, Fundamentals, chapter 1, the same equations PsychroLib implements: saturation vapour pressure over water from Hyland and Wexler, then the wet-bulb temperature that balances the psychrometric equation, solved to a thousandth of a degree. The code is wetbulb-core.js.

    Inputs: air temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), pressure. Pressure: one value, the standard sea-level 1013.25 hPa, for the chart, the contours, your scenario and the cities alike, so every point is on the same chart. At Delhi’s height (about 200 m) the real surface pressure is about 2% lower, which lowers the calculated wet-bulb temperature by less than a tenth of a degree. Range: 10 to 50 °C and 1 to 100% on the chart; the method holds well beyond it. Accuracy: within 0.03 °C of an independent reference (CoolProp’s humid-air model) at 264 test points from 20 to 50 °C and 5 to 100%. Shown to one decimal place, which is more than any weather input supports.

    Fahrenheit is a display choice; every calculation stays in °C.

    City weather: source, timing and limits

    Source: Open-Meteo’s forecast API (Open-Meteo, 2026 (opens in a new tab)), current conditions for the coordinates the site uses for each city: air temperature at 2 m, relative humidity at 2 m and surface pressure, from one response, so temperature and humidity always share a place and a time. Open-Meteo builds these from weather models and updates them every 15 minutes: they are modelled, not a measurement at a station.

    When: one request for all six cities when you open the page, kept in your browser for 15 minutes; nothing is sent when you move the controls. Fresh means the model time is within the last hour. If a request fails, the last good values stay with their original time and a “stale” label; a city with no values stays in the list without a marker. Nothing missing becomes a zero.

    Privacy: your browser asks Open-Meteo directly, as the city pages do; see Privacy and terms.

    Limits of this page
    • Wet-bulb temperature leaves out sun, wind, clothing, effort, acclimatisation, health, medicines and time.
    • The reference overlays come from small groups of healthy volunteers in a chamber at rest or very light effort. They are not limits for people working, for people with health conditions, or for children, and they are not safety lines.
    • Indoor conditions can differ widely from the weather outside.
    • This page gives no survival times, risk percentages or personal predictions.
