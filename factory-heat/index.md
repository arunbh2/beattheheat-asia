> Why two people in the same factory can face very different heat, what each source of heat does to the body, and which measures address it: an interactive floor plan, an intervention library with its evidence and limits, a finder, four worked examples, a measurement guide and a worksheet for workers and managers to fill in together. Garment manufacturing is the main example.

Workplaces

# Heat inside factories

Understand the sources. Protect the people. Improve the conditions.

## Why can two people in the same factory experience very different heat?

Because heat is not one number. It depends on where a person stands, what the work asks of their body, what is near them, what they wear, how long they stay and whether they can step away to recover. A thermometer on the wall, or the weather forecast for the city, describes none of that on its own.

An illustrative walk, not a measurement

- A sewing line, mid-morning

An operator sits at a machine under the second-floor roof. The work is light for the body but steady, and the line target leaves little room to stand up. Motors and lamps add a little heat; the roof above is warming as the sun climbs. By early afternoon the same seat may be much hotter than it was at nine.

- A pressing station, early afternoon

A presser stands at a steam iron and vacuum table. Heat comes from the iron and the steam, moisture hangs in the air, and the arms work constantly. The presser feels hot before the sewing floor does, and sweat evaporates less well in the damp air.

- The packing and dispatch bay

A packer lifts and carries cartons near the loading door. The air may be cooler than at the presses, but the work is heavier, so the body makes more heat of its own; sun on the loading apron and the trucks waiting there add more.

- The rest space

If there is a cool, shaded place close by, water within reach, a toilet nearby and permission to use them without losing pay, the body can recover. If the rest space is a hot corridor, far away, or effectively off limits during a rush order, the heat of the morning carries into the afternoon.

Exposure changes by task, by location, by time of day and by how long it lasts. Protection works when it reaches each of those, and when people can actually use it.

### Receiving and material handling

#### Heat sources

- Sun on the loading apron and through open doors
- Hot air from outside when doors stand open
- Truck engines and exhaust nearby

#### Work and duration

Moderate to heavy: lifting rolls of fabric and cartons, pushing trolleys. Work comes in bursts when deliveries arrive, often at the hottest time.

#### Who works here

Loaders and material handlers, often men, often on contract or daily terms; drivers waiting with vehicles.

#### Ask the workers

- When do deliveries arrive, and can any be moved to cooler hours?
- How heavy are the loads, and are there trolleys or lifts that work?
- Where do drivers and loaders wait?

#### Measurements that help

- Air temperature and humidity at the dock at peak delivery times
- Globe temperature in sun and in any shade
- Weights and distances carried

**Measures to consider:** [Shaded loading bays and waiting areas](#rec-shade-dock) · [Mechanical handling aids: trolleys, conveyors, lifts](#rec-handling-aids) · [Reschedule hot processes and heavy tasks](#rec-reschedule) · [Relief operators and extra staffing in hot periods](#rec-relief)

**Depends on:** Shade and doors depend on who owns the building; rescheduling depends on suppliers and buyers.

### Cutting and sewing

#### Heat sources

- Radiant heat from the roof and walls
- Many people working close together
- Machine motors and lighting
- Sun through windows and skylights

#### Work and duration

Light for the body, but long and steady: hours at a machine against a line target.

#### Who works here

Machine operators, most of them women in garment factories, with helpers and line supervisors; cutters, more often men, at cutting tables.

#### Ask the workers

- Which lines are hottest, and at what time of day?
- Can people leave the line to drink, use the toilet or rest without losing pay or being scolded?
- Where does air move, and where does it stagnate?

#### Measurements that help

- Air temperature, humidity and air speed at seated head height on several lines
- Globe temperature under the roof versus near the floor
- Readings across the shift, not only at noon

**Measures to consider:** [Reflective (cool) roof surfaces](#rec-roof-cool) · [Roof and wall insulation](#rec-roof-insul) · [Ceiling and large-diameter fans](#rec-ceiling-fans) · [Workstation fans](#rec-workstation-fans) · [Replace hot lighting and switch off idle equipment](#rec-led-lighting) · [Paid recovery breaks](#rec-paid-breaks) · [Cool, safe drinking water close to every line](#rec-water-access) · [Unrestricted, clean, safe toilets](#rec-toilet-access)

**Depends on:** Roof work needs the building owner; break rules need production planning and, often, the buyer's delivery schedule.

### Pressing and finishing

#### Heat sources

- Steam irons and vacuum tables
- Steam leaks and uninsulated steam lines
- Moisture in the air
- Fusing and heat-press machines

#### Work and duration

Moderate: standing, repetitive arm work, often fast near the end of an order.

#### Who works here

Pressers and finishers; in many factories pressing is done mostly by men, with women in thread-cutting, checking and folding nearby.

#### Ask the workers

- Where does steam escape, and who reports leaks?
- Are steam lines and boilers insulated?
- Is pressing next to sewing lines, so its heat spreads?

#### Measurements that help

- Humidity and globe temperature at the pressing tables
- Surface temperature of steam lines and irons (for contact risk)
- Air speed at the presser's position

**Measures to consider:** [Repair steam leaks and maintain the steam system](#rec-steam-repair) · [Insulate hot pipes, boilers and vessels](#rec-insulate-hot) · [Local extraction of heat and moisture at the source](#rec-local-exhaust) · [Separate, enclose or relocate hot processes](#rec-separate-hot) · [Zoned or workstation cooling](#rec-spot-cooling) · [Task rotation, where it truly reduces exposure](#rec-rotation)

**Depends on:** Steam repair and insulation need maintenance budget and an owner; extraction needs replacement air and commissioning.

### Packing and dispatch

#### Heat sources

- Heavy physical work
- Sun at the loading door
- Little air movement among stacked cartons

#### Work and duration

Moderate to heavy: lifting, carrying and stacking cartons, sometimes at speed before a shipment.

#### Who works here

Packers and loaders, often men, plus women in folding and tagging; workers on short contracts during peak orders.

#### Ask the workers

- What happens to breaks in the final days before shipment?
- Do new or returning workers get time to adjust to the heat?
- Are there carts, conveyors or lifts?

#### Measurements that help

- Air and globe temperature at packing tables and the loading door
- Workload: weights, lifts per hour, distances
- Hours worked in the days before shipment

**Measures to consider:** [Mechanical handling aids: trolleys, conveyors, lifts](#rec-handling-aids) · [Deliver materials to the point of use](#rec-point-of-use) · [Relief operators and extra staffing in hot periods](#rec-relief) · [Acclimatisation for new and returning workers](#rec-acclimatise) · [Limits on excessive overtime](#rec-overtime-limits) · [Shaded loading bays and waiting areas](#rec-shade-dock)

**Depends on:** Peak workload depends on the order calendar, which buyers set as much as the factory.

### Roof-exposed upper floors

#### Heat sources

- Sun-heated roof radiating downward
- Hot air rising from lower floors
- Few openings for air to escape

#### Work and duration

Depends on the task on that floor; the heat is added to whatever the work already makes.

#### Who works here

Whoever is placed there: often sewing lines or finishing, sometimes storage with occasional work.

#### Ask the workers

- Is the top floor hotter than lower floors, and from what time?
- What is the roof made of, and who owns it?
- Is there space to vent hot air at the top?

#### Measurements that help

- Globe and air temperature just below the roof and at work height, through the afternoon
- Compare with a lower floor at the same time

**Measures to consider:** [Reflective (cool) roof surfaces](#rec-roof-cool) · [Roof and wall insulation](#rec-roof-insul) · [Shaded or ventilated roof layers](#rec-roof-ventilated) · [Stack ventilation: let hot air out at the top](#rec-stack-vent) · [Reschedule hot processes and heavy tasks](#rec-reschedule)

**Depends on:** Building owner's permission; roof strength for any added layer or panels.

### Process equipment and utility rooms

#### Heat sources

- Boilers and steam generators
- Compressors and generators
- Hot exhaust and pipes

#### Work and duration

Short visits for operation and maintenance, sometimes long repair jobs in a hot room.

#### Who works here

Boiler operators, maintenance technicians and electricians, often working alone.

#### Ask the workers

- Who goes in, how often, for how long?
- Is anyone working alone in heat?
- Is waste heat vented outside or into the building?

#### Measurements that help

- Air and globe temperature during a typical maintenance job
- Surface temperatures of pipes and casings

**Measures to consider:** [Insulate hot pipes, boilers and vessels](#rec-insulate-hot) · [Recover or safely remove waste heat](#rec-waste-heat) · [Remote operation and cooled control booths](#rec-enclose-hot) · [Buddy arrangements](#rec-buddy) · [Trained first responders and a tested emergency procedure](#rec-first-response)

**Depends on:** Engineering advice; the plant room may serve the whole site, so changes need planning.

### Water, toilets, canteen and rest

#### Heat sources

- Rest areas under hot roofs or in corridors
- Canteens with cooking heat
- Long distances from the line

#### Work and duration

This is where recovery happens, or does not.

#### Who works here

Everyone, but especially those whose breaks are shortest: operators on targets, pregnant and breastfeeding workers, older workers.

#### Ask the workers

- How long does it take to walk from the farthest line to water and a toilet?
- Is the rest space cool, seated and usable in the time allowed?
- Is drinking water safe, cool and refilled?

#### Measurements that help

- Air and globe temperature in the rest space
- Walking time from lines to water and toilets
- Whether water points run dry, and when

**Measures to consider:** [Cool, safe drinking water close to every line](#rec-water-access) · [Unrestricted, clean, safe toilets](#rec-toilet-access) · [Cooled rest rooms](#rec-cool-rest) · [Lactation breaks and facilities](#rec-lactation) · [Paid recovery breaks](#rec-paid-breaks)

**Depends on:** Space, plumbing and a budget for cleaning and refilling, every day of the hot season.

Understand the heat

## Seven things set a worker's heat load

Occupational guidance separates heat stress, the total heat load on a worker from the work, the clothing and the surroundings, from heat strain, the body's response to that load. Seven things set the load.

- Air temperature

What an ordinary thermometer reads. It matters, but it is one of seven parts. Two places with the same air temperature can feel and act very differently on the body.

**Ask:** Where was it read, at what height, at what time?

- Radiant heat

Heat that reaches the skin across a gap, without touching: from a sun-heated roof or wall, from sunlight through a window or skylight, and from hot equipment such as irons, boilers, ovens, furnaces and steam pipes. A worker can face strong radiant heat in air that does not feel very hot.

**Ask:** What hot surfaces can the worker see from where they stand?

- Humidity and evaporation

The body cools mainly by sweat evaporating from the skin. Damp air slows evaporation, so in humid places sweating does less to cool. Steam, wet processes and evaporative coolers all add moisture.

**Ask:** Is the air damp from the weather, the process, or the cooling system?

- Air movement

Moving air carries heat away from the skin and helps sweat evaporate, as long as the air is cooler than the skin. Very hot air moved across the body can add heat instead.

**Ask:** Does air actually move where people work, and how hot is that air?

- Heat made by work

Muscles make heat. Lifting, carrying, pushing carts and fast repetitive work make more than sitting at a machine. The same room is a different exposure for a packer and for a seated operator.

**Ask:** How hard does each task work the body, and for how long without a break?

- Clothing and protective equipment

Clothing that blocks air and sweat, aprons, gloves and protective suits keep heat in. Some protection is essential for the task; its weight, fit and fabric still change the heat load.

**Ask:** What do people wear for the task, and is all of it needed?

- Duration and recovery

Heat builds over time. Long spells without a break, overtime, and nights too hot to sleep all reduce the body's chance to recover before the next exposure.

**Ask:** How long is the longest stretch without a cool break, and how did people sleep?

A different hazard

### Touching hot surfaces is a separate hazard

Conduction is heat passing by direct contact. At work it matters most as a contact burn from an iron plate, a steam pipe, a mould or a hot product. That is a burn hazard, controlled by guards, insulation, handles and gloves. It is different from whole-body heat stress, which builds from all seven parts above even when nothing hot is touched.

### Four words that are easily confused

**Environmental heat stress**: The heat load the surroundings and the work place on a person. It can be estimated by measurement.

**Physiological heat strain**: The body's response: rising core temperature, faster heart rate, sweat loss. Two people under the same heat stress can show different strain.

**Thermal discomfort**: Feeling too hot. It matters for wellbeing and attention, but feeling cooler is not proof of being protected, and feeling fine is not proof of being safe.

**Heat-related illness**: Harm to health, from heat rash and cramps through heat exhaustion to heat stroke, which is a medical emergency.

### Why a shaded indoor workplace can still be hot

A roof keeps the sun off people but can itself become a large radiant heater, and machines, lamps, steam and bodies add heat inside. Without enough air exchange the heat stays.

### Why the outdoor weather reading may poorly describe a workstation

The city's weather station measures air in open shade away from buildings. A workstation under a hot roof, beside a press or near a loading door has its own conditions. In assessments of Cambodian apparel factories in the hottest months, about half were above the programme's indoor threshold while hotter than the air outside at the same time.

### Why a measure that feels better still needs checking

A fan, a cooling vest or a misting system can feel pleasant at once. Whether it lowers the heat load enough, for the people most exposed, through the whole shift, is a separate question that only measurement and worker feedback answer. Changes also need checking so that they do not add a new risk, such as moving hot air or blocking exhaust ventilation.

Where this page gives a number, it names its source and the conditions it applies to. A figure from a laboratory, another climate or another kind of work does not transfer to your factory without checking.

Health and equity

## What heat does to workers, and why it is unequal

Heat harms through pathways that are well described, through associations seen in surveys, and through outcomes measured in studies. This page keeps them apart.

Mechanisms

How heat acts on the body, as occupational guidance describes it: core temperature rises when heat gain exceeds loss; sweating removes water and salt; the heart works harder to send blood to the skin; fatigue builds and attention slips; severe strain becomes heat exhaustion and, at worst, heat stroke.

Associations

What surveys and economic studies observe alongside heat, without proving cause on their own. In a survey of women at three garment factories in Phnom Penh, the share reporting heat symptoms rose from 68% in the cooler months to 88% in the hot months, by their own reports. A research note cites an estimated average 1.5% fall in productivity for moderate manufacturing work for each 1 °C above 25 °C WBGT.

Measured outcomes

What was measured on people under defined conditions. In a laboratory simulation of garment work, core temperature, heart rate and sweat loss were measured for each cooling option. That shows physiological strain under those conditions; it does not measure illness, injury or long-term health in a factory.

- **Fatigue and attention.** Heat strain and dehydration make people tired and slower to react. Where needles, blades, presses and moving machinery are close at hand, that is a route from heat to injury. In a survey at Sri Lankan apparel firms, workers linked headaches, dehydration and poorer concentration to needle injuries, by their own accounts; no checked study on this page measures that route directly.
- **Dehydration.** Sweat losses build over a shift. Water helps only if it is close, safe, cool and people have time and permission to drink, and to use a toilet afterwards.
- **Recovery.** The body recovers in cool rest, and overnight. Hot homes and short nights carry strain into the next shift.

### Women's work, women's bodies, and the arrangements around them

Women make up most of the garment workforce in many producing countries. Some differences in heat risk come from the body; many more come from how work is arranged. Keeping the two apart matters, because the answer to an arrangement is to change the arrangement.

From a laboratory study

In the laboratory simulation, the cooling options lowered core temperature in men more often than in women: women's core temperature fell only with air conditioning. The tasks differed by design (men ironed, women sewed) and the participants were healthy volunteers, so the result points to a question for real factories, not a settled difference.

- What the body brings

Body size, fitness, acclimatisation, age and health all shape heat tolerance, and individuals differ widely. Occupational guidance notes that women tend to sweat less than men of the same fitness, size and acclimatisation, and that differences in heat tolerance narrow once those are taken into account. Physiology is one part; the arrangements below are often the larger one.

- Tasks and authority

Women are often concentrated in seated, target-paced sewing and finishing work, and less often in supervisory roles that decide when lines pause, who rests and where fans go. Ask who holds those decisions.

- Targets, piece rates and attendance bonuses

Where pay depends on pieces completed or on a perfect attendance record, a break or a sick day costs money. In a study at two garment factories in Dhaka, quota pressures restricted workers' use of drinking water. Protection that ignores this may exist on paper and go unused.

- Water and toilets

People drink less when toilets are far, dirty, unsafe or require permission. Water and toilets belong in the same plan, measured in walking minutes and in whether people actually use them.

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Occupational health guidance links a pregnant worker's core temperature rising above 38 °C to risks for the fetus, and suggests doing tasks earlier in the day or in cooler places, with work-rest cycles. Evidence in humans of harm to the developing baby is not conclusive, so this guidance is precautionary. Pregnant and breastfeeding workers need reliable access to drinking water. The response is an individual assessment with the worker and her health provider, adjusted tasks, timing or location, and protected earnings, not exclusion from work by default.

- Lactation

Where the ILO Maternity Protection Convention (No. 183) is ratified, it gives a right to daily breaks or reduced hours to breastfeed, counted as working time and paid. A clean, private, cool place to express milk and store it is part of that.

- Menstruation and menopause

Workers raise these concerns in heat, from access to private, clean facilities to hot flushes. This page has not yet found checked occupational studies on heat and menstruation or menopause in factory work, so it makes no health claim; facilities and confidential support are still matters of dignity and access.

- Protective equipment that fits

Equipment sized for an average man may fit women badly, trap more heat or be left off. This page found no checked study measuring this in garment or textile work; it belongs in the worksheet as a question to ask.

- Commuting, hot homes, sleep and unpaid care

Long commutes, hot homes and unpaid care leave some workers, often women, with less time to rest, rehydrate and recover outside working hours.

- Confidential reporting, representation and freedom from retaliation

Workers know where heat collects and airflow is weak. They can tell you only if reporting is safe: confidential, answered, and free of punishment. Representation needs to include women, contract and temporary workers, and those in the hottest jobs.

Risk also varies within any group: by age, disability, pregnancy, health conditions and medicines, how long someone has worked in heat, and how secure their job is. A temporary worker may not report symptoms for fear of losing work. Plans that count only the average worker miss the people most at risk.

Accommodation, not exclusion

Protection keeps people in work. Moving a pregnant worker to a cooler task with the same pay, adding a relief operator so a line can pause, or shifting a heavy job to the cooler morning are accommodations. Sending people home without pay, or quietly not hiring them, is not protection.

## The intervention library

70 measures in twelve families, each with what it addresses, how it works, when it fits, what it needs, who runs it, and the type of evidence behind it. Where this page has no checked source, the record says so and is marked a proposal requiring validation.

What the evidence types mean

**Authoritative occupational guidance**: Guidance describes what agencies recommend from accumulated evidence and experience. It rarely measures a single measure's effect, and legal duties apply only in the jurisdiction named.

**Field report from operating factories**: Reports from operating factories, mostly by management, programme staff or researchers visiting. They show what was done and what people observed; none here is an independent before-and-after evaluation.

**Controlled experimental study**: Measured under controlled conditions, often in a laboratory. The effect is clear for the conditions tested; transfer to a working factory needs checking.

**Modelling or engineering analysis**: Calculated, not measured on people at work. Useful to bound what is possible; the result depends on the assumptions.

**Manufacturer claim**: Claims by a supplier. None is used on this page.

**Proposal requiring validation**: A candidate measure with no checked source on this page yet. It is described so it can be appraised, not recommended.

### Process and heat-source reduction

Make less heat, or keep it where it is made.

#### Repair steam leaks and maintain the steam system

Authoritative occupational guidance

**Addresses:** Heat and moisture escaping from steam lines, valves, traps and irons.

How it works, when it fits, and its evidence

**How it works**: Every leak adds heat and humidity to the room and wastes fuel. Fixing leaks and faulty traps removes both at the source.

**Factory types and tasks**: Pressing and finishing in garment factories; laundries, food processing, dyeing and wet processing.

**Works if**: A named person checks for leaks on a schedule; Workers can report a leak and see it fixed

**Take care**: Steam systems are pressurised: repairs need trained staff and isolation of the line

**Upfront**: Low: parts and labour.

**Running**: Routine inspection and maintenance time.

**Who installs, runs and maintains it**: Maintenance or utilities lead; production reports leaks.

**Workers' part**: Pressers usually know where steam escapes; give them a quick way to report it.

**Equity and access**: Benefits pressers and anyone working beside the pressing section.

**Evidence**:
- **Authoritative occupational guidance.** [US Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), 2024](https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/controls); [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS), 2026](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/heat/heat_control.html)

**Can people actually use this?**: Not assessed yet for this measure. The questions are on [Heat, health and the ability to act](https://beattheheat.asia/heat-health-and-equity/#taxonomy).

**Measure before and after**: Humidity and globe temperature at pressing tables before and after; count of open leak reports and days to fix.

**Pairs with**: [Insulate hot pipes, boilers and vessels](#rec-insulate-hot) · [Local extraction of heat and moisture at the source](#rec-local-exhaust)

#### Insulate hot pipes, boilers and vessels

Authoritative occupational guidance Field report from operating factories

**Addresses:** Radiant and convective heat from bare hot surfaces; contact burns.

### Active cooling and humidity management

#### Direct evaporative cooling (water curtains, cooling pads)

Field report from operating factories

**Addresses:** Hot, dry or moderately humid air across a large floor.

How it works, when it fits, and its evidence

**How it works**: Fans pull outside air through water-soaked pads; the water evaporates and cools the air before it reaches people. It adds moisture as it cools.

**Factory types and tasks**: Large garment floors in hot climates; common in Southeast and South Asia, Central America and North Africa.

**Works if**: Outdoor humidity is low or moderate; Strong exhaust removes the moist air; Pads and water are kept clean

**Take care**: Cools less as humidity rises, and adds humidity that slows sweating; Humid air can affect fabric and product quality in monsoon conditions; Uneven airflow: people near the pads may be cold, those far away still hot; Uses water; stagnant water needs treatment against bacteria

**Upfront**: Moderate.

**Running**: Water, electricity, pad replacement and cleaning.

**Who installs, runs and maintains it**: Facilities team.

**Workers' part**: Ask people far from the pads, and near them.

**Equity and access**: Check the lines farthest from the pads.

#### Buddy arrangements

Proposal requiring validation

**Addresses:** People working alone in heat, and symptoms people miss in themselves.

How it works, when it fits, and its evidence

**How it works**: Two people agree to check on each other for signs of heat illness during hot shifts, and know what to do and whom to call when one of them shows symptoms. It adds to, and never replaces, heat controls, rest arrangements and a tested emergency procedure.

**Factory types and tasks**: Utility rooms, maintenance, night shifts, new and returning workers.

**Works if**: Paid time for both people to learn the signs, the response and the escalation route, and for the checks themselves; Pairs that both people accept, in a language both understand, with arrangements for workers with hearing, speech or literacy needs; A named supervisor or responder who answers every call, and a rule that raising the alarm never costs pay or work; Privacy: a buddy notices symptoms, not medical details, and nothing is recorded about a person's health without consent

**Take care**: Checking on others is work: if it falls repeatedly on the same people, often women, without time or pay, it becomes unpaid caring work; A buddy cannot cool a workplace or stop production; responsibility stays with supervisors and the employer; Pairs break down when shifts, overtime or staffing change; review them each hot season; Not a substitute for trained first responders or medical care

**Upfront**: Training time for both people and their supervisor, during paid hours; local costing needed.

**Running**: Paid time for checks and refresher training before each hot season; supervision of the arrangement.

**Who installs, runs and maintains it**: The employer, through supervisors, with the safety committee or worker representatives agreeing how pairs are formed.

**Workers' part**: Workers choose or agree their pairing and can change it; they help set the escalation route.

**Equity and access**: Protects lone, new, migrant and night-shift workers when pairs are fair, paid and in a language both understand; can add an unpaid load if the same people always do the checking.

**Evidence**:
- **Guidance.** Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety: [Hot environments: control measures](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/heat_control.html)
- **Proposal requiring validation.** Recommended practice in national occupational guidance; no evaluation of buddy systems in factories was found in the sources checked for this page.

**Can people actually use this?**:
**Who controls access**: Every worker on a hot shift, including contract, temporary and migrant workers.

**What using it costs in money, time or earnings**: Paid time for training and checks; no equipment.

**Who does the additional work**: The checks themselves, done by co-workers; supervisors respond.

**Is that work paid and supported**: Paid when done in working hours and counted in workload; unpaid if added on top.

**Who might be left out**: Workers without a shared language, workers with hearing or speech impairments if no adapted method exists, and people working alone with no one assigned.

**What personal information it needs**: None about health: a buddy reports symptoms seen, not medical history.

**Can the people it serves shape it**: Through the safety committee or worker representatives.

**Who is accountable if it fails**: The employer and the named responder on each shift.

**What to check locally**: Who is paired, whether every call was answered, and whether the same people always carry the load.

**Measure before and after**: Lone working in heat; calls raised and how fast someone responded; whether checking time is paid.

## Beyond garment assembly

Garment assembly is the worked example. Other manufacturing shares the same seven parts of heat, in different proportions. Each line below names what to check first; none is a finding.

- Textile spinning and weaving

Different from garment assembly: large machine halls running continuously, where the air's humidity may be controlled for the yarn. Check how process humidity interacts with workers' heat.

- Dyeing and wet processing

Hot water, steam, open vessels and wet floors, plus chemicals that may require protective equipment. Check moisture, radiant heat from vessels, and the heat added by protection.

- Food processing

Ovens, fryers, boilers and steam beside cold rooms; wet floors; hygiene clothing. Check moves between hot and cold areas, steam, and what hygiene clothing adds.

- Electronics assembly

Often climate-controlled for the product, so the main heat risk may lie in warehouses, loading and utility areas, or in protective gowning. Check areas outside the controlled rooms.

- Metalworking and foundries

Intense radiant heat from furnaces and molten metal, heavy protective clothing and contact-burn hazards. Check radiant heat at each position and time in heavy protection.

- Plastics, ceramics and glass

Moulding machines, kilns and furnaces radiate heat; hot products are handled. Check radiant heat, contact burns and ventilation around machines.

## Measure effectiveness responsibly

Measurement tells you whether a change reached the people it was meant for. Each instrument answers a different question.

**Air temperature**: The ordinary thermometer reading. Simple and useful for trends; it misses humidity, radiant heat and air movement.

**Humidity**: How much moisture is in the air. High humidity slows sweat evaporation; evaporative cooling and steam raise it.

**Air speed**: How fast air moves past the worker. Shows whether ventilation and fans actually reach people.

**Globe temperature**: A thermometer inside a black globe, which responds to radiant heat. The difference from air temperature shows how much radiant heat there is.

**Wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT)**: A heat stress index that combines a natural wet-bulb reading (humidity and air movement), the globe temperature (radiant heat) and, outdoors, the air temperature. Guidance and some national rules use it, adjusted for how hard the work is.

### Good practice

- Measure at representative workstations, at the height where people work, not on a wall or by a door.
- Measure across the shift and the season, not only at noon on one day.
- Record the task and how hard it works the body; the same reading is a different risk for a packer and an operator.
- Ask workers. Their reports find hot spots instruments miss, and tell you whether protection can be used.
- Repeat the same measurements, at the same places and comparable times, after each change.
- Make readings visible to workers and managers alike.

### Four things that can be true, separately

- **Installation completed**The fan, roof coating or water point exists. This says nothing yet about exposure.

- **Measured exposure changed**Readings at workstations differ, under comparable weather and work.

- **Workers could use the protection**People reached the water, took the breaks, sat in the cool room, without losing pay.

- **Health outcomes improved**Fewer symptoms, illnesses or injuries. This needs records over time and a fair comparison; claim it only with that evidence.

## Evidence gaps, stated plainly

The wider evidence has the same shape. A 2026 scoping review of 90 intervention studies found 68% were in laboratories and 73% in developed countries, with only four testing engineering controls. A 2026 mapping review of field research on workers' heat in low- and middle-income countries found only 9% of reports included any intervention, and 39% included no women.

- No checked independent before-and-after evaluation of a heat intervention in an operating garment factory was found for this page; garment field evidence is from reports by management, programme staff and researchers. The nearest field trial found is a pilot with 12 workers in an indoor sawmill in Thailand.
- The one controlled garment study was a laboratory simulation with healthy volunteers; how its results transfer to factory floors, other climates and other tasks needs testing.
- No checked occupational study was found on heat and menstruation or menopause in factory work, or on protective-equipment fit for women in heat.
- No checked study was found linking toilet access to drinking and heat illness in garment factories, beyond summaries.
- Many intervention records rest on guidance or principle; their effect sizes in factories in South and Southeast Asia are not established here.
- No manufacturer claims are used; products are described by type only.
- Injury pathways from heat in garment work were not found measured in a checked study; the one link found is workers' own accounts in a Sri Lankan survey.
