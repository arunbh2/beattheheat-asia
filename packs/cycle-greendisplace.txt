CONTENT

The green-displacement loop
Green space follows wealth. When greening reaches poorer districts without protection, rents rise, residents are pushed into hotter housing, and the gap grows.
Evidence for the cycle as a whole: emerging.

THE LINKS IN THE CYCLE
- Deepening inequality, which leads to green space follows wealth: Every impact lands hardest on those with the least protection, widening gaps in health, income and opportunity. [https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/fao-report--heatwaves-and-floods-affect-rural-women-and-men-differently--widen-income-gap] [https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2022.874751/full]
- Green space follows wealth, which leads to greening pushes people out: Trees and parks cluster in better-off neighbourhoods, which can pay for them and for the upkeep that keeps them alive, so poorer districts stay hotter. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landurbplan.2014.01.017] [https://doi.org/10.4337/9781800376762]
- Greening pushes people out, which leads to homes that trap heat: New parks and greening can raise rents and land prices until the poorer residents they were meant to cool are pushed out, often into hotter, more crowded housing. This is green gentrification. Across 28 North American and European cities, greening in the 1990s and 2000s was followed by gentrification in 17. [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31572-1] [https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369538102_Green_Gentrification_and_The_Value_of_Urban_Nature_Case_Study_of_Pathumwananurak_Park_Bangkok]
- Homes that trap heat, which leads to deepening inequality: Tin roofs, poor ventilation and crowded rooms can make homes hotter inside than out, leaving nowhere safe to rest. Thin roofs cool slowly, so the worst comes at night: in low-income homes measured across South Asia, tin-roofed houses stayed warmer indoors than the outdoor air. [https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2022.874751/full] [https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01314-5]

STUDIES BEHIND IT
- Anguelovski et al., Nature Communications, 2022: Green gentrification in European and North American cities. Across 28 cities in 9 countries, greening in the 1990s and 2000s was strongly linked to gentrification between 2000 and 2016 in 17 of them. Where: 28 cities in Europe and North America. Limit it states: No Asian cities in the sample. [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31572-1]
- Wolch, Byrne and Newell, Landscape and Urban Planning, 2014: Urban green space, public health and environmental justice: making cities “just green enough”. Green space tends to favour wealthier neighbourhoods, and new parks meant to fix that can raise housing costs and displace the residents they were meant to help. Small, local projects are more likely to reach those who need them. Where: Mostly the United States. Limit it states: Mostly US evidence. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landurbplan.2014.01.017]
- Endo, The Developing Economies, 2026: Gentrification, urban informality and displacement in Bangkok. Fieldwork on how recent gentrification in Bangkok, including green urban renewal, affects the city’s lower-income residents. Where: Bangkok, Thailand. Limit it states: Qualitative; it does not measure how many people moved. [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/deve.70013]
- Chapter 3 in Nature-Based Solutions for Cities, 2023: Towards just nature-based solutions for cities. Nature-based solutions can deepen injustice when benefits flow to some and costs, including displacement, fall on others; procedural, distributional and recognition justice all need attention. Where: Global. Limit it states: A framework for justice, not a measure of harm. [https://doi.org/10.4337/9781800376762]

ACTIONS THAT COULD INTERRUPT IT
- Green without pushing people out: Secure tenure, rent safeguards and designing with residents let poorer neighbourhoods gain shade and parks without losing their homes. Small, local greening that is “just green enough” protects people better than flagship projects.
