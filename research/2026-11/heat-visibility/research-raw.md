# A. THE STORY IN 9 BULLETS

- **Before 1988: heat was measurable, but institutionally scattered.** Urban temperature differences had a long scientific history. This review reaches an 1833 observation through a later EPA history, rather than through the original publication. [S58]
- **1988-2001: climate assessment gave heat a recurring policy route.** The IPCC's early impacts assessments connected heat with health. By TAR, the policy summary linked heatwaves with mortality, crops, electricity and unequal vulnerability. This was recognition, rather than proof of sustained public attention. [S1, S5, S62]
- **1995-2010: urban mortality made heat a public-health emergency.** Chicago, Europe and Russia became reference cases for surveillance, warnings and organised prevention. The term silent describes a recognition problem: deaths can be dispersed, delayed or attributed to other conditions. It does not mean communities were unaware of heat. [S4, S7, S8, S57]
- **2010-2017: South Asian experience entered both practice and science.** Ahmedabad connected a deadly event with a municipal plan. Karachi followed a major emergency with a management plan. India circulated national guidance. Humid-heat research placed the Indus and Ganges agricultural regions within debates about adaptation limits. [S9, S13, S17, S18]
- **2011-2019: heat became a connected disaster and development risk.** SREX connected extremes with vulnerability. GHHIN built a dedicated exchange network. The Lancet Countdown and ILO made heat exposure and lost work capacity recurring indicators. These metrics widened the conversation beyond emergency deaths. [S11, S15, S19, S22]
- **2018-2022: warming levels, attribution and physiology sharpened the stakes.** SR1.5 connected temperature goals with exposure differences. Observations and experiments complicated the 35°C wet-bulb shorthand. AR6 strengthened the assessment of observed hot extremes. A 2022 India-Pakistan attribution study reached international audiences through WMO. [S20, S23, S24, S27, S31]
- **2023-2024: institutions began naming protection functions.** Examples include a heat coordination role in Dhaka North, operational workplace measures in Singapore, insurance coverage for SEWA members, and Bangladesh's clinical guidance. COP28 and the 2024 UN call gave health and extreme heat greater international visibility. Announcements, coverage and rules are different forms of progress. [S33-S39, S60]
- **2025-2026: implementation, finance and regional exchange gained dedicated homes.** Regional hubs in Singapore and New Delhi, a Bangladesh development assessment, city cooling initiatives and a heat-financing framework connected evidence with delivery. Maternal and child health gained more specific monitoring attention. [S40-S48]
- **The transition remains uneven and incomplete.** Scientific publication growth is measurable; a comparable regional history of media attention was not verified here. Disaster databases and epidemiological mortality estimates count different things. Country coverage in this review is uneven, and absence from this timeline is not evidence of inaction. [S26, S49, S55]

# D. TURNING POINTS

The seven turning points below are interpretations of the opened record. “Still unseen” identifies a limit of the evidence or delivery model presented here. It does not imply that an institution ignored a group.

1. **1995-2008: mortality emergencies became a prevention field.** Chicago supplied a sharply visible event and a surveillance problem. Europe supplied another large emergency. Health agencies carried lessons into operational guidance as repeated events made prevention harder to treat as a one-off response. [S4, S7, S8] **Still unseen:** homebound and socially isolated older people were difficult to reach. Death certification could miss indirect effects. People with disabilities and informal workers were not consistently identifiable in the records used here. The evidence base was weighted towards well-documented northern cities.

2. **2010-2017: South Asian municipal experience became transferable practice.** Ahmedabad's emergency was followed by a plan with a designated municipal health role, forecast links and public communication. Karachi's emergency led to clearer inter-agency responsibilities. Municipal bodies, health services, meteorological institutions and partners carried the work. The timing connected recent loss with the next hot season. [S9, S17] **Still unseen:** a plan's publication did not establish who could afford to stop work, reach cool space or obtain care. Small cities, informal settlements and mobile workers remain less visible in this source set. Evaluation and continuity cannot be inferred from launch dates.

3. **2010-2021: humid heat changed the language of limits.** Theory made heat plus humidity a physiological question. South Asian projections connected it to densely populated agricultural areas. Observations and laboratory experiments then narrowed the distance between future risk and real exposure. Research institutions carried this sequence through peer-reviewed evidence. [S10, S18, S23, S27] **Still unseen:** a theoretical threshold did not represent the safety of older people, children, pregnant workers, people with chronic illness or workers in direct sun. Young healthy adults in controlled experiments do not represent every person or workplace. Tropical night heat and recovery conditions need separate attention.

4. **2018-2022: heat became a recurring health and labour account.** Annual Countdown indicators, ILO projections and AR6 made exposure and work losses comparable across reporting cycles. Health researchers, labour institutions and the IPCC carried complementary evidence. This coincided with growing demand for development-relevant climate metrics. [S19, S22, S24, S29] **Still unseen:** modelled work capacity is not identical to observed wages, household income or lost employment. Unpaid care, piece-rate work and the ability to take a break are not captured by one aggregate working-hours number. Women's exposure can remain hidden within household and informal activity.

5. **2022-2024: named climate risk gained named institutional roles.** Rapid attribution connected an India-Pakistan event with climate change while the emergency remained current. Dhaka North named a coordination role; Singapore specified workplace triggers; Bangladesh issued health guidance. COP28 and the UN call carried the issue into broader policy audiences. [S31, S33-S39] **Still unseen:** a visible role or declaration does not demonstrate staffing, budgets, enforcement or coverage. Outdoor and informal workers, children outside formal care, and people with disabilities may need delivery channels beyond alerts. Asia-wide first claims remain bounded partner claims.

6. **2023-2026: protection became a question of income and finance.** The SEWA insurance pilot translated hot days into an income-protection instrument. The Bangladesh assessment connected heat with national development losses. UNDRR's framework distinguished financing routes for heat resilience. Worker organisations, public institutions, development finance and philanthropy carried different parts of this shift. [S32, S42, S46, S60] **Still unseen:** coverage is not evidence of an adequate payout, lasting affordability or reduced exposure. Insurance cannot by itself establish safer housing, water access or working conditions. Small municipalities and unregistered workers are not demonstrated beneficiaries of every instrument.

7. **2025-2026: regional institutions and more specific health data emerged.** The Southeast Asia and South Asia hubs gave evidence exchange a regional home. Maternal and child-health indicators added a more specific monitoring route. Yet CRED's provisional disaster table also documented delayed heat-death reporting. Networks, health agencies and disaster-data institutions carried these parallel developments. [S40, S45, S48, S49] **Still unseen:** regional hubs do not establish equal national participation or local service coverage. Much of mainland and island Southeast Asia remains thin in this timeline. Small cities, humid-heat regions and groups absent from routine surveillance can remain statistically quiet even as institutional attention grows.

Across these turning points, three changes are distinct: **a hazard becomes measurable, its losses become countable, and an institution accepts a protection function**. They can occur years apart. Ahmedabad's plan and Singapore's workplace measures illustrate different protection functions; neither alone establishes complete coverage.

- **Full early IPCC heat audit.** Repeated access failures affected original FAR, AR4, SREX, SR1.5 and SROCC pages or PDFs. Official mirrors and summaries supported narrower entries. They did not establish every chapter, first term or confidence statement. The older special reports listed in C were identified through an opened bibliography, but their heat passages were not audited.
- **All working groups in every cycle.** Direct text was not obtained for every WGI, WGII, WGIII and synthesis contribution. In particular, FAR's original heat content, original AR4 heat passages, AR5 WGIII, and detailed AR6 WGII and Synthesis heat-health text remain unresolved. The table leaves these gaps visible.
- **First IPCC use of heatwave, humid heat or wet-bulb.** This requires a searchable full corpus and term-by-term checks, including spelling variants. No first-use date is claimed.
- **Early heat-health records beyond urban climatology.** The 1833 entry rests on an EPA historical account. The original was not read. Earlier military, occupational and medical heat records were not verified in primary texts.
- **2004 heatwave event-attribution milestone.** The original landmark European attribution paper could not be confirmed in accessible full text in this review. No date, risk ratio or first claim was entered. The verified 2022 WMO account is not presented as the beginning of attribution science.
- **Full mortality series for landmark events.** Final totals for Europe 2003, Russia 2010 and the combined 2015 South Asian emergencies were not reconciled across studies and official records. Those totals were omitted. Chicago's 465 figure is the opened CDC report's certified-death count and period, not a definitive final excess-mortality estimate.
- **Canadian aftermath of the 2021 northwestern heatwave.** The opened source supports US surveillance. A verified history of subsequent Canadian warning, building or health-policy changes was not completed.
- **Every country named in the scope.** Dedicated plan, programme or first-institution milestones were not verified for Maldives, Myanmar, Thailand, Viet Nam, Cambodia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines or Timor-Leste. Bhutan appears as a regional report host, rather than through a verified domestic heat-plan milestone. Sri Lanka appears through a regional workshop. These are research gaps, not claims of absence.
- **Independent firsts and institutional continuity.** The first-plan claim for Ahmedabad, first-in-Asia claim for Dhaka North's heat officer, and early insurance claims were not independently audited across all jurisdictions. Current staffing, budgets and continued operation were not established by the historical launch sources. No first national heat plan across Asia is asserted.
- **Plan effectiveness.** A primary evaluation of Ahmedabad's mortality effects was not opened and checked. Similar outcome evidence for other listed plans was not verified. Timeline launches are not treated as proven reductions in illness or deaths.
- **Heat-specific adaptation finance totals.** A consistent global or regional spending series separating heat from cooling, health, cities and wider adaptation was not verified. No estimate of a heat-finance gap is supplied.
- **Philanthropic heat-fund outcomes.** Announcements and specific support are visible in opened sources, but total disbursement, additionality and reach were not reconciled. A reported COP30 health-funding commitment was not entered because its underlying announcement could not be opened and confirmed.
- **Insurance outcomes.** The opened project record establishes first-year coverage, not verified claim-level payouts, basis risk, sustained affordability or health effects. Later payout figures were not confirmed in primary records.
- **Media and public attention.** The verified bibliometric study measures research publications, not news coverage or search interest. No comparable, reproducible South and Southeast Asian media series, Google Trends extract or multilingual coverage study was opened and verified. No media-attention peak is dated here.
