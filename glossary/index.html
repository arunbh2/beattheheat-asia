Plain-language glossary of extreme heat

42 terms that come up again and again in extreme heat, explained in a sentence or two.

Open the interactive map

What heat does to the body

Heatstroke
A medical emergency. The body’s core temperature rises above about 40 °C and its cooling system fails. Without fast cooling it can kill or cause lasting organ damage. Watch the explainer (0:32) Source: National Centre for Disease Control, India: heat-related illnesses
Heat exhaustion
The warning stage before heatstroke: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness and nausea. Rest, water and cooling usually bring recovery. Watch the explainer (0:33)
Heat stress
When the body takes in or makes more heat than it can shed, from hot air, humidity, sun and physical work. Watch the explainer (0:35)
Acclimatisation
The body slowly adjusting to heat over one to two weeks. New and returning workers are most at risk until it has. Watch the explainer (0:33)
Excess deaths
Deaths above the normal number for that place and time of year. Because death certificates rarely say “heat”, this is how the real toll of a heatwave is counted. Watch the explainer (0:34)

How heat is measured

Wet-bulb temperature
Heat and humidity combined into one number, read from a thermometer wrapped in a wet cloth. It shows whether sweat can still cool you. A wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C is often quoted as the limit of human survival; recent studies suggest the real limit is lower. Watch the explainer (0:35) Source: Vecellio et al., Journal of Applied Physiology, 2022, summarised by Penn State
Dry heat and humid heat
Dry heat is high temperature with low humidity: sweat still evaporates, but the body soaks up heat fast. It peaks in South Asia’s pre-monsoon months. Humid heat is warm, wet air in which sweat can’t evaporate, so the body can’t cool itself even at lower temperatures. It dominates Southeast Asia and builds through South Asia’s monsoon, and it is rising faster than dry heat. The two need different warnings and different cooling. Watch the explainer (2:03) Source: Ivanovich et al., Geophysical Research Letters, 2024
WBGT (wet-bulb globe temperature)
The workplace measure of heat. It adds sun and wind to heat and humidity, and is used to set rest breaks and stop work. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Heat index
The “feels-like” temperature in the shade, combining air temperature and humidity. In the Philippines, a heat index of 42 °C or more is officially a danger level. Source: PAGASA: heat index
Heatwave
A spell of unusually hot weather for that place. There is no single global definition: each country sets its own thresholds. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Hot nights
Nights that stay too hot for the body to recover from the day. They are often more dangerous than hot days, and they are rising faster. Watch the explainer (0:35)
Urban heat island
Cities running several degrees hotter than the countryside around them, especially at night, because of concrete, traffic, waste heat and few trees. Watch the explainer (0:34)
Mean radiant temperature
The heat your body takes in from the sun and from hot surfaces around it: roads, walls and roofs. In strong sun it shapes how hot you feel more than air temperature does, which is why shade matters so much. Watch the explainer (0:29) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Thermal comfort
How hot or cold a person feels. It depends on air temperature, humidity, wind, sun, clothing and activity, not on air temperature alone. Watch the explainer (0:27)

Acting before harm

Limits to adaptation
The point beyond which adjusting no longer prevents harm. Places used to heat cope better with days of 30–35 °C, but a study of 2.7 million firms found no adaptation to days above 40 °C. Cutting emissions keeps more places below that limit. Source: OECD Economics Department, Working Paper No. 1828, 2024
Heat action plan
A city’s or country’s plan for the heat season: who issues warnings, at what temperature, and what hospitals, employers and services do next. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Early warning system
A forecast that reaches people in time, in a form they understand, with clear advice on what to do. Watch the explainer (0:33)
Anticipatory action
Acting on a forecast, before a disaster peaks, by releasing pre-agreed money and help when a trigger is reached. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Cooling centre
A public place, such as a library, school or community hall, kept cool during heatwaves for people with no cool space at home. Watch the explainer (0:33)

Money and protection

Parametric insurance
Insurance that pays out automatically when a measured trigger, such as a temperature threshold, is crossed. There is no claim form and no damage assessment. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Basis risk
When the trigger and the real loss don’t match: people suffer from the heat, but the threshold isn’t crossed and no payout comes. Watch the explainer (0:36)
Risk pool
A group of countries, cities or insurers that share the cost of disasters, so no single member carries a catastrophe alone. Watch the explainer (0:35)
Social protection
Public support such as pensions, cash transfers and unemployment benefits that keeps households out of poverty when income stops. Watch the explainer (0:35)

Cooling, land and systems

Passive cooling
Cooling a building without using power: shade, airflow, insulation, orientation and reflective surfaces. Watch the explainer (0:33)
Cool roof
A roof painted or built to reflect sunlight, so less heat reaches the room below. Watch the explainer (0:33)
Refrigerant
The fluid inside air conditioners and fridges that carries heat away. Many common ones are powerful greenhouse gases if they leak.
Black carbon
Soot from diesel, wood, coal and brick kilns. It warms the air, and when it settles on snow and ice it darkens them so they melt faster.
Vicious cycle
A harm that feeds itself: each turn of the loop makes the next one worse. This map shows where those loops could be interrupted. Watch the explainer (0:35)
Maladaptation
A response to heat that makes things worse elsewhere or later, such as air conditioners that cool a room but heat the street.
Albedo
How much sunlight a surface reflects. A white roof has a high albedo and stays cooler; dark asphalt has a low albedo and stores heat. Watch the explainer (0:28)

Nature in the city

Evapotranspiration
Water evaporating from soil and leaves. It draws heat out of the air, so well-watered plants cool their surroundings. Plants short of water stop cooling. Watch the explainer (0:32) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Nature-based solutions
Using living systems such as trees, wetlands, soils and water to tackle problems like heat and flooding while also benefiting people and nature. A park only works as one if it is planned for those benefits and kept alive. Watch the explainer (0:27) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Ecosystem services
The benefits people get from nature: shade and cooling, cleaner air and water, rain soaking into the ground, food and places to rest. The same service is worth most to those with the fewest alternatives, such as cooling for people without air conditioning. Watch the explainer (0:29) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Blue-green infrastructure
A network of water (blue) and plants and soil (green), such as rivers, ponds, wetlands, parks and planted drains, designed to manage rain and heat together. Watch the explainer (0:27) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Grey infrastructure
Engineered, mostly concrete systems such as pipes, drains, embankments and pumps. Many good solutions mix grey and green. Watch the explainer (0:27) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Sponge city
A city designed to soak up, store, clean and slowly release rainwater through parks, wetlands, permeable ground and planted drains, not only pipes. China piloted it in 30 cities from 2014 to 2019; Chennai and Bangkok are building sponge parks. Watch the explainer (0:28) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Co-benefits
The extra gains a measure brings beyond its main aim, such as a park built to hold floodwater that also cools the street and gives children somewhere to play. Watch the explainer (0:29) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Ecosystem-based adaptation
Using nature to help people adapt to climate change, such as mangroves against storm surges or trees for shade. A kind of nature-based solution. Watch the explainer (0:28)
Green gentrification
When a new park or greening raises rents and land prices so much that the poorer residents it was meant to help are pushed out. Watch the explainer (0:29) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)
Environmental justice
A fair share of environmental benefits and harms, and a fair say in decisions about them. Poorer neighbourhoods are often hotter and less green. Watch the explainer (0:30) Source: Nature-Based Solutions for Cities (Edward Elgar, 2023, open access)

Also explained on the map

Aquifer
Underground layers of rock or sand that hold groundwater, the water that wells and pumps draw from.
Agroforestry
Growing trees alongside crops or livestock. The trees shade the soil, help it hold moisture and give farmers another source of income.
