The heat economy · Questions to ask

Questions to ask
before you buy, fund or start.

For city officers, employers, worker and community organisations, funders, investors and founders: twenty-four questions, the rules and ways of paying that have helped elsewhere, the common obstacles, and what each kind of organisation could do.

About this section. We gathered this from public websites and reports in October 2026. Being listed does not mean we recommend, partner with or rank anyone, and none of it is financial advice. Companies describe their own products; we have not tested them, so please check details with them directly. If something is missing or out of date, tell us.

Does it really protect people here?

  1. Who exactly will this protect, and from what kind of heat?Ask for the people, the place and the evidence. Selling a product is not the same as protecting someone.
  2. Will it work here, in our dry heat, humid heat and hot nights?Ask for tests in conditions like yours, with people inside the building.
  3. Will it make someone else hotter?Air conditioners and some roofs push heat into streets and neighbouring homes.
  4. Are the claims measured, estimated or marketing?Ask how a result was measured, and what it was compared with.
  5. Will it still work as heat, floods and water shortages grow?Ask how long it lasts and what it needs to keep working.

Is it fair?

  1. Can families on the lowest incomes use it without giving up food, school or medicine?Count the full cost: buying, running, repairs and travel. If they can’t, what is the free or subsidised option?
  2. Who is left out because they rent, or lack a meter, ID, bank account or phone?Make sure there is another way in for them.
  3. Can workers stop to rest without losing pay or their job?Equipment helps only if rest is paid and safe to take, including for contract workers.
  4. Could it push up rents or move residents and vendors out?Involve tenants and vendors early, and agree protections in writing.
  5. Does it add unpaid work, often for women?Pay for upkeep roles, and check opening hours, safety and toilets.
  6. Can older and disabled people reach it, use it and have a say?Check access, transport, communication and help on site, with them.
  7. Who sees people’s health, location or repayment data?Collect only what is needed, and keep a way to use the service without sharing data.

Who keeps it working?

  1. Who looks after it after the pilot, and with what budget?Name the owner, the repair plan and who pays each year.
  2. What happens when the power or phone network fails?Plan a backup for the hottest days, and keep essential services running first.
  3. What happens to electricity demand at the hottest hour?Start with shade and design, which need no power, before adding machines.
  4. Does it compete with drinking water?Check where the water comes from in a drought.
  5. Which cooling gas does it use, and who handles leaks and disposal?Use trained technicians and safe gases.
  6. Where do old fans, batteries and gases go?Agree safe collection and recycling from the start.
  7. Can the product, the installer and the claims be checked independently?Look for official tests or registration, a warranty, and a way to get faults fixed.
  8. Who can people complain to, and who decides to fix, change or stop it?Agree this before starting, and review it after each hot season.

Who pays, and who carries the risk?

  1. What happens after a missed payment or a bad season?Check the total cost, what happens to the equipment, and the options in hard times.
  2. Could people lose income and still get no payout, or get it too late?Ask how payouts are triggered, and what help exists if they don’t come.
  3. Who receives the subsidy, and what extra protection does it buy?Make sure the benefit reaches the people using it.
  4. If payment depends on results, could it reward the wrong thing?Pay for working services people can reach, not for units shipped or longer hours.

Rules that have helped create demand

Examples from Asia. A rule on paper helps most when it is checked and followed.

  • India: fans and many air conditioners must carry an energy label, which helps buyers choose efficient models. BEE standards and labelling current official listing (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Singapore: air conditioners must meet a higher minimum efficiency from April 2025. Singapore NEA MELS/MEPS history and current requirements (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Telangana, India: a cool-roof policy that applies to certain new buildings. Telangana Cool Roof Policy 2023–2028 (opens in a new tab) ↗ · Telangana building-permission orders (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • India: a building code for homes on keeping heat out and letting air through, where states adopt it. BEE residential building code official page (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Singapore: rules for outdoor work in the heat, with four more practices becoming mandatory on 1 December 2026. MOM current outdoor heat measures (opens in a new tab) ↗ · Singapore MOM announced heat-framework enhancements (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • India: a public company bought efficient fans and air conditioners in bulk and worked with private energy-service firms. EESL building energy ESCO partnerships 2021 (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • India: a national cooling plan covering buildings, cold storage, servicing and cooling gases. India Ozone Cell cooling action plan and implementation documents (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Cambodia: a national cooling plan. Cambodia National Cooling Action Plan (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • India: national disaster-mitigation funds can now pay for heatwave projects; upkeep needs its own budget. MHA mitigation fund operational guidelines 2026–2031 (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Bangkok: heat planning and public warnings. Thailand NESDC Bangkok heat action planning (opens in a new tab) ↗ · World Bank Bangkok heat study (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • ASEAN: guidance that helps lenders and investors recognise projects that protect people from climate risks, including heat. ASEAN mARs Guide Version 1 official release (opens in a new tab) ↗ · ASEAN mARs Guide Version 1 (opens in a new tab) ↗

Ways customers can pay

Each can lower the cost of getting started. Check the total cost, and what happens in a bad month, before choosing one.

  • Pay as you go. Pay in small instalments, and own the equipment at the end. Devidayal Solar PAYG finance (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Repay through the electricity bill. The cost of an efficient fan is added, in small amounts, to monthly bills. Allied Bank fan replacement programme terms (opens in a new tab) ↗ · State Bank Pakistan fan programme circular (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Pay for cooling, not the machine. A provider owns and maintains the equipment and charges for the cooling or storage used. Kaer cooling services (opens in a new tab) ↗ · ColdHubs service model (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Small loans for roofs. Microfinance loans for roof improvements that keep homes cooler. MHT gender and poverty barriers to climate finance 2023 (opens in a new tab) ↗ · CODI Baan Mankong (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Insurance with a safety net. Insurance combined with savings and emergency support, so people aren’t left with nothing if the insurance doesn’t pay. CGAP SEWA climate-health lessons (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Loan guarantees. A guarantee makes banks more willing to lend to cooling suppliers or their customers. DFC Ecozen public information summary (opens in a new tab) ↗ · State Bank Pakistan fan programme circular (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Investors who take the first loss. Patient investors agree to absorb the first losses, which makes it easier for others to invest. SEACEF II first close and first-loss investors (opens in a new tab) ↗ · State Bank Pakistan fan programme circular (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Paying for results. Suppliers are paid for each efficient appliance that reaches a user and works. CLASP Global LEAP RBF monitoring and verification (opens in a new tab) ↗ · CLASP Bangladesh retailer field study 2019 (opens in a new tab) ↗

Common obstacles

  • Many families can’t afford the upfront cost, or the running costs. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · MHT gender and poverty barriers to climate finance 2023 (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • The person who pays is often not the one who benefits: landlords and tenants, employers and workers. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · MHT gender and poverty barriers to climate finance 2023 (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Renters, and people without a meter or documents, often can’t qualify. MHT gender and poverty barriers to climate finance 2023 (opens in a new tab) ↗ · Allied Bank fan replacement programme terms (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Rules only work with enough inspectors and follow-up. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · MOM current outdoor heat measures (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Poor-quality or counterfeit products and cooling gases are hard to spot. UNEP South Asia servicing and enforcement network (opens in a new tab) ↗ · UNEP regional competency certification for RAC servicing (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Too few trained installers, spare parts and repair services. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · UNEP regional competency certification for RAC servicing (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Little shared information on who is exposed, what heat costs and what works. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · CGAP SEWA climate-health lessons (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Loans in foreign currency are risky when customers pay in local currency. DFC Ecozen public information summary (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Contracts are small, and many pilots stop when the first grant ends. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · TechEmerge results and lessons (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Unreliable power or water, or a product that doesn’t suit the climate. IFC/UNEP Cooler Finance original report (opens in a new tab) ↗ · PNNL evaporative cooling suitability (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Money to install is easier to find than money to run and repair. MHA mitigation fund operational guidelines 2026–2031 (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • Insurance payouts, loans and support don’t always arrive together, when they are needed. CGAP SEWA climate-health lessons (opens in a new tab) ↗ · Devidayal Solar PAYG finance (opens in a new tab) ↗

What each kind of organisation could do

Suggestions to discuss, not commitments.

National governments

  • Set clear rules for products, buildings and heat at work, and check they are followed.
  • Fund public protection, and make clear which budgets can pay for heat work, including upkeep.

City and local governments

  • Bundle needs across homes, schools, markets and clinics into larger, costed purchases that suppliers can bid for.
  • Approve the upkeep budget, and protections for tenants and vendors, before installing anything.

Health authorities

  • Set out what a heat-ready clinic needs: cooling, staff, supplies and referral.
  • Track heat illness while protecting patient data, and check what actually helps.

Weather and climate services

  • Give local warnings people can act on, through radio, SMS and other offline channels too.
  • Help others tell apart a weather warning, a trigger to act at work and an insurance payout trigger.

Employers and businesses

  • Buy protection as a package: shade, water, cooling, safe schedules and paid rest.
  • Stay responsible for all workers, including contract workers, and pay for upkeep.

Worker organisations and unions

  • Shape protection with workers: rest, pay, benefit rules and how to complain.
  • Buy together or run shared funds, without taking on duties that belong to employers or government.

Utilities and energy regulators

  • Help households use efficient cooling, and keep power to clinics, water pumps and shelters first.
  • Make sure repayment through bills doesn’t leave anyone cut off or shut out.

Water authorities

  • Plan enough clean water for drinking, cooling, trees and public facilities.
  • Coordinate backup pumping and drought rules with health services and communities.

Farmers’ organisations and agriculture agencies

  • Design crop, livestock, farm-worker and cold-storage services with farmers.
  • Keep fees and finance fair, and protect water rights and tenant farmers.

Schools and education authorities

  • Buy together for safe classrooms, drinking water, shade and equipment that can be maintained.
  • Keep children learning and safe when schools change hours or close.

Banks, insurers and financial regulators

  • Keep loans, public help and insurance clearly separate, and protect borrowers.
  • Be open about payout triggers, timing, data use and what subsidies really cover.

Development banks, donors and climate funds

  • Fund preparation, local skills, evidence and public access, as well as projects that can repay.
  • Link support to fair delivery and a real upkeep plan, and be open about terms.

Community and civil society groups

  • Make sure tenants, women, migrants, older and disabled people help design services.
  • Run trusted outreach and feedback, and paid maintenance where the community chooses it.

International bodies

  • Share guidance, technical help and lessons across countries, adapted to each place.
  • Support open evidence and trained servicing, without assuming one standard fits everywhere.
