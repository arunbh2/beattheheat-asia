The heat economy · What makes markets work

What turns a need for protection
into a fair market.

The rules that create demand, the ways of paying that reach people on low incomes, the barriers, a checklist of fairness questions to ask before buying or backing anything, and what each kind of institution could do.

How to read this. A targeted review of published sources, checked in October 2026, not a census of the market. Listing a business, investor or programme is not endorsement, partnership or investment advice, and nothing is ranked. Company descriptions are what the company says unless labelled otherwise. Amounts of different kinds (announced, closed, disbursed; equity, loans, grants, guarantees) are never added together.

Three distinctions run through this. A strategy can coordinate action without creating any obligation to buy. A way of financing can lower the upfront cost while still excluding the people in greatest need. And money to install protection is not money to run it. None of this lessens the value of any mechanism; each shows what must also be secured.

24 questions to ask before buying or backing a heat product or service

For cities, employers, funders, investors and worker organisations. These are researcher-designed due-diligence questions, not an official certification or a score.

  1. Who is protected, from which heat exposure, and what evidence supports that specific use?Require a named user, setting, exposure pathway and source; product sales alone do not prove protection.
  2. Will it work in this place’s dry heat, humid heat and hot nights?Review humidity, radiation, air movement, workload, building and equipment ratings; test occupied conditions.
  3. Does it heat someone else’s street, room or workplace?Assess heat rejection and displaced exposure, not only a cooler sensor inside the purchased space.
  4. What happens to electricity demand at the hottest hour?Specify peak load, passive reductions, rebound and limits on noncritical demand.
  5. What happens when power or communications fail?Fund safe fallback, critical-load priority, spare parts and a response operator.
  6. Does cooling compete with drinking water or worsen groundwater depletion?Cost source, quality, drought availability, reuse and lawful allocation; do not assume solar pumping conserves water.
  7. Which refrigerant is used, and who handles leaks, fire/toxicity risks and recovery?Require equipment-specific safety, GWP documentation, assessed servicing competence and lifecycle recovery.
  8. Can the poorest use it without sacrificing essentials?Check upfront, electricity, water, fees, travel, repairs and shock months; identify the subsidy or free public route.
  9. Who fails the tenancy, meter, ID, bank, phone or credit test?Document each exclusion and an offline/public alternative; do not infer eligibility from a global mission.
  10. Can workers stop and rest without losing pay or facing retaliation?Verify worker voice, paid rest, sanitation, water, grievance routes and subcontractor inclusion.
  11. Could the improvement raise rents or push residents and vendors out?Require resident/tenant representation, permissions and enforceable affordability/retention safeguards.
  12. Does it reduce or add unpaid care and maintenance work?Budget paid roles, safe access/hours, toilets and user decision power, including women and carers.
  13. Can disabled and older people reach, use and influence the service?Check accessible space, transport, communication, assistance and safeguarding with intended users.
  14. Who maintains it after the pilot, with which committed budget?Name the owner/operator, service schedule, parts, replacement reserve and budget payer.
  15. Are the product, installer and performance claims independently checkable?Use relevant model registration/test evidence, commissioning, warranty and defect remedies; distinguish certification from outcomes.
  16. Where do old fans, batteries, electronics, coatings and refrigerants go?Include safe collection/recovery/disposal, material compatibility and paid end-of-life duties.
  17. Who sees health, worker, location and repayment data, and can users refuse unnecessary collection?Minimise data; restrict use/retention; retain safe offline access and grievance/appeal routes.
  18. What happens after a missed payment or a season of low income?Review full cost, locking/recovery, electricity-service consequences, hardship, repair grace and exit rights.
  19. Could people suffer losses without a payout, or receive money too late?Explain basis risk, triggers, exclusions, renewal, appeals and separately funded assistance/prevention.
  20. Who receives the subsidy, and what additional protection does it buy?Track pass-through, access, competitive procurement, loss allocation and outcomes; a guarantee is not free cash to users.
  21. Are measured, modelled and marketing claims clearly separated?State metric, occupied baseline, weather/context, method and uncertainty; no automatic illness/workday claim from temperature.
  22. Could the payment metric reward exclusion, unsafe work or stockpiles instead of protection?Use independent verification of access and functioning services; avoid rewards for longer unsafe hours or unserved warehouse inventory.
  23. Will it remain useful under future heat, floods and water or energy constraints?Test plausible scenarios, material lifetime, lock-in and effects on neighbouring communities; classification alone is insufficient.
  24. Who can report failure, obtain a remedy and decide whether to change or stop?Publish an accountable operator, accessible grievance route, post-season review and safe exit/repair plan.

Rules that create demand

Each is a verified Asian example. A published rule is not the same as compliance or coverage achieved.

India mandatory cooling-appliance labelling

Official guidance or dataIndia

What is published: BEE lists ceiling fans and specified room-air-conditioner categories under mandatory labelling.

The demand it could create: Can create demand for tested efficient products, compliant retail inventories, laboratories and consumer verification; sales are not guaranteed.

Who would buy: Manufacturers/importers/retailers and institutional buyers

Check before relying on it: Check the model’s current schedule and label period. A star label is not proof of correct installation, affordability or heat-health protection.

Official guidance or dataSingapore

Telangana cool-roof policy and occupancy checks

Official guidance or dataIndia

What is published: Policy specifies mandatory government-building adoption, residential plot threshold of at least 600 sq yd, and government low-income-housing provisions; smaller residential plots have a voluntary route. Occupancy-stage verification is described.

The demand it could create: Can support coatings/membranes, competent installation, inspection and upkeep; low-income implementation still needs an identified public purchaser.

Who would buy: Public estate buyers, developers/owners and local programme buyers

Check before relying on it: Threshold is plot area, not roof area. Policy wording differs by stock/type; verify exact local application. No achieved cooling/coverage or blanket household subsidy inferred.

Singapore outdoor heat framework, including announced enhancements

Official guidance or dataSingapore

What is published: MOM requires heat-risk measures, with required/recommended distinctions; additional training, cool-water, emergency-supply and suitable-clothing practices become mandatory in December 2026.

The demand it could create: Can create demand for calibrated WBGT equipment, training, accessible shade/water, emergency readiness and accountable protection services.

Who would buy: Employers/occupiers; workers should not be made the purchasers of employer duties

Check before relying on it: As of 28 October the enhancements are future. Framework scope is outdoor work; no blanket individual safe-work threshold or universal fan rule inferred. Equipment is complementary to rest and wage protection.

Ways of paying that can reach people on low incomes

PAYG rent-to-own cooling

Reported, not evaluated

Equipment access financed over time; device access is linked to payment and ownership follows repayment.

Example: Devidayal Solar publishes PAYG refrigeration and lender/EMI/telemetry routes.

Who pays: Entrepreneur/household pays lender instalments and operating costs. Who benefits: Refrigeration users and their customers; this protects products and livelihoods, not necessarily worker heat exposure.

Reaching people on low incomes: Company describes rural/first-time-borrower and women-oriented channels; lowest-quintile take-up and sustainable net gains not verified.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Disclose total effective price, interest/fees and seasonal cash-flow stress.
  • Provide a repair/outage and hardship policy; avoid automatic essential-cooling loss.
  • Use telemetry only for transparent agreed purposes.

Risks: Remote locking, spoilage on default, repayment pressure, data use, e-waste and overestimating sales/income.

Devidayal Solar PAYG finance (opens in a new tab) ↗

On-bill efficient-fan finance

Reported, not evaluated

Consumer loan instalments are collected through the electricity bill.

Example: Allied Bank publishes the PM fan-programme application, bill-repayment and owner/account eligibility terms; SBP confirms the supporting programme framework.

Who pays: Eligible account-holder repays financing and pays separately specified installation charges. Who benefits: Household fan users who meet the offer criteria.

Reaching people on low incomes: Published terms exclude tenants and require the electricity account in the applicant’s name. This is a documented access limit, not proof of universal low-income access.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Separate electricity-service protections from loan recovery and verify any disconnection/default terms.
  • Test total bill burden, installation cash and affordability under variable use.
  • Provide an alternative public route for excluded tenants/non-account holders.

Risks: Housing/account/document exclusions; bill arrears and default consequences; assuming efficiency savings always cover instalments.

Cooling-as-a-service and pay-per-use storage

Reported, not evaluated

Provider retains/operates cooling assets; a customer pays for a defined service rather than owning equipment.

Example: Kaer describes delivered cooling and plant operation; Smart Joules has an India financed-service precedent; ColdHubs describes per-crate storage in Nigeria.

Who pays: Facility owner or storage user pays recurring service and relevant operating charges under contract. Who benefits: Building occupants or produce users. Free access for vulnerable people requires a public/collective payer.

Reaching people on low incomes: Commercial facility contracts are not household inclusion evidence. Per-use storage reduces the need to own a cold room, but affordability and poorest reach remain unverified.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Disclose service price, tariff escalation, performance, outages and exit rights.
  • Confirm enough utilisation and an accountable maintenance operator.
  • A public purchaser must fund any free/low-fee access commitment.

Risks: Long-term lock-in, monopoly connection costs, outage/spoilage liability, excessive peak load/water/heat rejection and assuming lower upfront cost means lower lifetime cost.

Microfinance-linked roof improvements

Reported, not evaluated

Credit/cooperative finance can spread eligible housing-upgrade costs; it does not replace a subsidy for households unable to repay.

Example: MHT qualitative report records credit-cooperative/microfinance channels for selected roof measures. CODI illustrates community-controlled upgrading finance and tenure negotiation, not verified heat effects.

Who pays: Borrower/cooperative members repay; government/grants can fund explicit subsidy. Who benefits: Eligible low-income residents with permission and repayment capacity.

Reaching people on low incomes: Specific low-income urban evidence exists; small qualitative sample and staff-reported channels do not establish representative reach, affordability, default or health impact.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Resolve tenure/permissions and structural/fire/moisture safety.
  • Stress-test repayments without assumed electricity savings for homes without AC.
  • Provide grants or public services for households without safe surplus income.

Risks: Over-indebtedness, landlord capture, rent increases, gendered liabilities, unsuitable materials and exclusion of tenants/documents.

Parametric cover with retained funds, savings and assistance

Reported, not evaluated

A pre-defined trigger transfers an agreed risk layer; retained/public/donor funds and noninsurance assistance address other needs.

Example: CGAP’s SEWA case describes programme evolution and a Climate Welfare Facility, distribution, savings/credit links, cash support and trigger/delay challenges.

Who pays: Premium payers/sponsors, member or donor pool contributions and insurers carry different costs/risks. Who benefits: Covered worker members who meet the relevant benefit rules.

Reaching people on low incomes: Grassroots distribution and worker focus documented; coverage does not ensure timely sufficient cash or universal eligibility. Do not mix carriers/terms across pilot years.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Separate warning/action, assistance and insurance triggers.
  • Explain basis risk, exclusions, renewal and claims arrival in accessible formats.
  • Do not make premium borrowing or new credit a condition of basic protection.

Risks: Basis risk, delayed or absent payment, correlated loss, renewal cost, privacy and unpayable credit; insurance is not prevention or guaranteed wage replacement.

Credit guarantees for cooling suppliers or lenders

Reported, not evaluated

A guarantor absorbs contractually specified lender losses; the borrower generally still owes the debt.

Example: DFC public summary proposes local-currency Ecozen financing for solar controllers and off-grid cold storage; SBP confirms a first-loss credit guarantee for participating fan-finance banks.

Who pays: Guarantor/sponsor carries agreed loss exposure; borrower repays lender. Who benefits: Eligible suppliers/borrowers, with downstream smallholder/household benefits conditional on actual delivery.

Reaching people on low incomes: Guarantee design does not prove lender additionality, affordable pricing or access among those otherwise excluded.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Verify guarantee cap, trigger, coverage, claims process and additional lending.
  • Pass any benefit through transparently and retain affordability checks.
  • Match local-currency receipts and liabilities where possible.

Risks: Guarantee mistaken for grant/debt forgiveness, risk shifted to public balance sheet, unchanged collateral/exclusions and subsidised lending without protection outcomes.

First-loss risk position: equity capital versus guarantee

Reported, not evaluated

A junior layer takes losses before a senior layer. This may be funded equity/capital or a contractual guarantee; they are different instruments.

Example: SEACEF II manager reports junior first-loss equity at its first close. This is a general clean-energy financing-architecture comparator; heat-specific allocation is not established here. Pakistan provides a cooling-specific first-loss credit-guarantee example.

Who pays: Philanthropic/public junior investors or guarantee sponsors accept specified losses; underlying commercial repayment remains necessary. Who benefits: Portfolio companies/eligible lenders and users only if benefits reach them.

Reaching people on low incomes: No verified first-loss-equity allocation producing affordable heat protection for the poorest in the collected evidence. Do not relabel general clean-energy fund capital as heat finance.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Define additionality, loss allocation, governance, fees and beneficiary access.
  • Require a credible payer and underlying project cash flow.
  • Disclose who bears residual/default risk.

Risks: Subsidising senior investors without fairer access, moral hazard, opaque loss allocation and an unbankable public good forced into debt/equity.

Results-based appliance incentives

Reported, not evaluated

Sponsor pays suppliers/distributors after pre-defined verified delivery or sales; outcomes depend on what the contract actually measures.

Example: CLASP’s historical Global LEAP programme account includes Bangladesh and fans/refrigerators; monitoring procures verification.

Who pays: Donor/public sponsor funds incentives; firms bridge working capital until verification/payment. Who benefits: Eligible appliance customers; household cooling/food access depends on actual use and after-sales service.

Reaching people on low incomes: Distribution evidence is not a causal affordability, poorest-quintile, illness-prevention or durable-use evaluation.

Conditions for fair use:

  • Verify use/access and repair alongside sale or delivery.
  • Pay on time and finance local firms’ working-capital needs.
  • Prevent duplicate claims, unsuitable products and exclusion of costly-to-serve users.

Risks: Metric gaming, warehouse stock counted as users, paperwork exclusion, delayed payment and incentives captured upstream.

Barriers

Upfront and recurring affordability

Official guidance or data

What the evidence shows: IFC/UNEP discusses purchase/energy access constraints; MHT’s qualitative study records financial constraints; fan-finance terms retain upfront installation payments.

Effect on the market: Spreading purchase payments may leave total bills, maintenance and shock exposure unaffordable.

A suggested response: Test full lifecycle cash burden, offer repair and explicit targeted subsidies, and retain free/public routes.

Different decision makers, payers and beneficiaries

Official guidance or data

What the evidence shows: Cooling-finance synthesis describes divided incentives along building value chains; MHT records gendered household choices.

Effect on the market: An owner may choose capex while tenants pay energy; worker benefits may not determine employer purchasing.

A suggested response: Write contracts around the real payer and protected beneficiary; combine landlord/worker safeguards with public procurement where needed.

Tenure, meter and document eligibility

Reported, not evaluated

What the evidence shows: MHT documents insecure housing/title conditions; Allied Bank’s published fan terms exclude tenants and require the applicant’s electricity account.

Effect on the market: The most exposed household may be unable to borrow or approve the roof/connection upgrade.

A suggested response: Create owner/tenant agreements and public subsidy routes with accessible documentation and rights support.

Rules need inspection, resources and verification

Official guidance or data

What the evidence shows: Sources distinguish formal requirements from verification, inspection and delivery; roof policy describes occupancy-stage checks and MOM specifies calibrated monitoring.

Effect on the market: A published rule may generate limited compliant demand if no authorised inspector, budget, test capacity or remedy is operational.

A suggested response: Fund inspection and grievance capacity, check certificates against installed/maintained performance and publish safe aggregate implementation data.

Official guidance or data

Local revenue and currency liability mismatch

Reported, not evaluated

What the evidence shows: DFC’s Ecozen summary specifies a local-currency guarantee structure.

Effect on the market: For imported equipment or foreign debt, local-currency revenue may not cover depreciating-currency debt or replacement costs; this is a financial inference, not an observed heat-sector default estimate.

A suggested response: Match currencies where feasible and cost any hedge, pass-through limits and replacement reserves; do not shift opaque FX risk to poor users.

Reported, not evaluated

What each kind of institution could do

Suggestions, not commitments or statements of any institution’s current mandate.

National governments

  • Set and enforce coherent product/building/worker requirements, with transparent transitions and competent inspection.
  • Fund public protection and national evidence; make grant, investment and recurrent budget eligibility explicit.

City and local governments

  • Aggregate climate-appropriate housing, school, market and clinic needs into costed portfolios with accessible procurement.
  • Approve recurrent maintenance and inclusion/tenure safeguards before installation.

Health authorities

  • Specify heat-ready care, surge/referral and facility uptime requirements with clinical oversight.
  • Maintain privacy-protecting surveillance and evaluate actual health/service outcomes.

Weather and climate services

  • Provide locally appropriate actionable hazard information through interoperable public channels, including offline routes.
  • Help distinguish warning thresholds from workplace-action and insurance-payout triggers.

Employers and businesses

  • Purchase environmental protection, safe work organisation and paid rest as an integrated service; retain accountability for workers.
  • Fund equipment upkeep and fair supplier contracts, including subcontracted workers.

Worker organisations and unions

  • Co-design needs, rest/pay protections, benefit rules and accessible complaints with workers.
  • Aggregate demand or co-govern protection pools without transferring employer/public duties or unaffordable costs to members.

Utilities and energy regulators

  • Enable efficient cooling and safe demand management while preserving critical loads and reliable access.
  • Scrutinise bill-finance/default protections, tariff burden and eligible-user exclusions.

Water authorities

  • Require a sustainable clean-water budget for drinking, cooling, trees and public facilities.
  • Coordinate pumping/backup and drought restrictions with health, utilities and community users.

Farmers’ organisations and agriculture agencies

  • Co-design crop/livestock, worker and cold-chain services with reliable local support and markets.
  • Negotiate safe fees and fair finance while protecting water rights and tenant access.

Schools and education authorities

  • Pool demand for safe classrooms, drinking water, shade and maintainable equipment.
  • Protect access, learning/care continuity and safe closure decisions, with child and disability inclusion.

Banks, insurers and financial regulators

  • Differentiate affordable asset finance, public assistance and licensed residual risk transfer; verify borrower protection and currency exposure.
  • Require transparent triggers/default terms, claims timing, data safeguards and additionality of guarantees/subsidies.

Development banks, donors and climate funds

  • Fund preparation, local capability, evidence and public access alongside suitable bankable assets.
  • Tie catalytic support to fair delivery and credible upkeep; disclose instrument, status and risk allocation.

Community and civil society groups

  • Represent tenants, women, migrants, older and disabled people in service design and governance.
  • Run trusted outreach, user feedback and properly paid maintenance where chosen and resourced.

International bodies

  • Provide interoperable guidance, technical assistance and cross-country learning with local adaptation.
  • Support transparent assessment, servicing competence and public-good evidence without implying one standard guarantees protection everywhere.

Known, inferred, uncertain

Known: Primary sources document Asian standards, code/plan frameworks, heat-work requirements, public funding constraints and cooling-finance mechanisms. Singapore enhancements effective December 2026 are future at the research date. Published Pakistan fan-finance terms identify owner/account requirements and tenant exclusion. India SDMF guidelines include heatwave mitigation and require separate funding for upkeep.

Inferred: Credible demand depends on a duty or need becoming an authorised, funded purchase with accountable delivery. Finance can reduce upfront barriers but requires separate evidence of affordability, access, risk allocation and protection. Costed public demand, appropriate subsidies and local maintenance capability may help viable services emerge.

Uncertain: Local implementation and enforcement, dedicated municipal HAP appropriations, current eligibility and actual user take-up across most examples. Bottom-quintile access, total finance burden, default/claims outcomes and independent heat-health impacts. Transferability across countries and climates; global prevalence of any barrier.
