The heat economy

Who is building protection from heat,
who pays for it, and what is still missing.

The businesses already selling protection from heat, the investors and programmes backing them, the businesses that could come up, and what it takes for a market to protect the people who need it most. Every part is tied to the vicious cycles it can help break.

How to read this. A targeted review of published sources, checked in October 2026, not a census of the market. Listing a business, investor or programme is not endorsement, partnership or investment advice, and nothing is ranked. Company descriptions are what the company says unless labelled otherwise. Amounts of different kinds (announced, closed, disbursed; equity, loans, grants, guarantees) are never added together.

A credible market begins when a need for protection becomes an authorised, funded purchase with accountable delivery and upkeep. Heat exposure, social value and a promising product do not, by themselves, identify a customer.

The central finding of the research

How the heat economy works

Three questions come before any product: who needs protection, who can authorise it, and who can pay for it? They are often three different people or institutions. A street vendor needs shade, but the city controls the pavement. A worker needs paid rest, but the employer controls the schedule.

Nineteen parts of the heat economy

Maturity describes whether the product or service model exists, not how fast it is growing or whether it protects enough people. “Can partly reach the poorest” means a route exists, not that it has been shown to work. The research deliberately gives no single size for the whole heat economy: product sales, loans, premiums and public spending overlap, and adding them would overstate it.

Cooling and safer places

Passive coolingEstablished

Cool roofs, coatings and membranes

Reflective roof products, installation, quality checks and recoating services intended to reduce solar heat gain.

Who pays: Owners, landlords, employers, housing authorities; grants/public purchasers for low-income stock.

Who benefits: Residents, tenants, home-based workers, pupils and clinic users.

Can partly reach the poorest. Subsidised/community retrofits can reach low-income occupants; roof control, rent and upkeep matter. No poorest-quintile reach verified.

Efficient fans and low-energy air movement

Ceiling, table, pedestal and ventilation fans, including efficient motors, installation and repair.

Who pays: Households, employers, schools, clinics and public procurement agencies.

District cooling and cooling-as-a-service

Central chilled-water networks and long-term building cooling services, including storage when contracted within the cooling asset.

Who pays: Developers, building owners, commercial tenants and public anchor customers.

Active coolingGrowing

Distributed food and medical cold chains

Off-grid/weak-grid cold rooms, precooling, refrigerated transport and medical refrigeration with appropriate separate standards.

Who pays: Farmers/traders/FPOs, food businesses, public health purchasers and logistics clients.

Who benefits: Growers, vendors, consumers and patients; benefit allocation depends on fees and bargaining power.

Can partly reach the poorest. Pay-per-use reduces equipment ownership barriers, but the poorest may lack produce volumes, transport or cash. Medical access normally requires institutional funding.

Using this

  • Cities, employers and health systems: start with the fairness questions before buying, and with what could come up for services to commission.
  • Funders and investors: see where money has gone and which ideas need public or pooled money to reach the poorest.
  • Founders: find support programmes and the barriers others have met.
  • Worker and community organisations: use the fairness questions to ask who is protected, who pays and who keeps it working.
