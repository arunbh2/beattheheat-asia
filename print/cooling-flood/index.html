Cooling options: Heat and waterlogging

What to build or change, what keeps it working, the traps to avoid and rough costs.

Protects. Neighbourhoods that bake in summer and flood in the monsoon, and the groundwater both depend on.
Key caution. Stop sewage and rubbish flowing in, and fund the desilting, before calling it blue-green.

What the evidence says

  • Over four years, Greater Chennai Corporation restored 70 ponds for ₹144.34 crore and built 88 rainwater-harvesting parks for ₹14.74 crore.
  • Bangkok’s Benjakitti Forest Park, a former tobacco factory turned wetland, is designed to hold 87 million litres of water.
  • China’s sponge-city pilots in 30 cities (2014–2019) often built projects one at a time instead of as a city-wide system, struggled to get engineers and designers working together, and left cities worried about paying for more once pilot money ended.
  • Jakarta’s green space was about 5.2–5.6% of the city in 2024–25, sources differ, against a legal target of 30%, now pushed back to 2045.
How you will know it works. Waterlogging hours after heavy rain, and summer temperature near the site, before and after
Five traps that waste cooling money: Buying before diagnosing · Built, with no one to keep it · Green without water · Promising degrees you haven’t measured · A list of activities, not a model.
Before you choose, ask: Does it need electricity, and is the supply reliable in the hottest weeks? · What happens in a power cut: does it stop, or keep cooling without power? · Does it need water, and where will that water come from in a dry spell? · Who services or repairs it, and how fast? · Who owns the running cost each year, and from which budget? · Who can use it: renters, women, older people, people with disabilities? Keep the outcomes apart: a cooler room, comfort, less heat strain, better health and kept income are different outcomes; measure the one you mean.
