# El Niño and heat in Asia

How a change in the Pacific affects rainfall, heat and the choices available to people.

## What El Niño is

El Niño is a warming of the central and eastern tropical Pacific, coupled with a shift in winds and rainfall. It is one phase of ENSO, which swings every three to seven years on average. It can tilt the odds towards drier or hotter conditions in parts of Asia. It does not decide whether a heatwave, drought or flood happens in any one place.

## What it may mean for Asia

Effects differ by region and season. Over Southeast Asia it typically brings drier conditions, mainly from June to November; in India, most years with above-normal April to June heatwave activity in 1961 to 2013 followed El Niño events: a pattern, not a forecast.

## What it cannot tell any place

UncertainA Pacific probability is not any town's chance of a heatwave, water shortage or flood. Strength odds describe the Pacific index, not local impacts; season, geography, the Indian Ocean and long-term warming all matter, and national outlooks and local warnings carry that detail.

Our readingThe odds favour El Niño persisting into the start of the hot season in much of South and Southeast Asia, then easing. That is a reason to get ordinary heat protection working early, in the places where people already spend the hottest hours. It is not a forecast of a heatwave anywhere.

## Before the hot season: a short checklist

- Which public service will we protect first, and where do people wait or work in the heat?
- Who receives the official local warning, and what does it change?
- Who authorises, pays for, runs and repairs each measure?
- How will we know who could not get water, shade or care?
- Which national service's seasonal outlook and district warnings will we follow, and who receives them?
