        Who heat hits hardest

        Same heatwave.
        Different barriers.

        Heat is not only a city problem. It reaches farms that depend on rain, coasts and riverbanks, and villages far from any clinic. Who is harmed depends on two things together: how exposed people are, and what stands between them and safety. Age, gender, disability, social identity, insecure tenure and unpaid care shape four questions.

          People in the same community may need different routes to the same outcome: livelihood security and dignity.

          Also on the map: violence by husbands and partners rose about 4.5% with each 1 °C of yearly warmth across India, Nepal and Pakistan (Zhu et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2023 (opens in a new tab)), and 466 million children now live where extremely hot days are at least twice as common as in the 1960s (UNICEF, 2024 (opens in a new tab)).

                Why “wicked”?

                No single cause.
                No single owner.
                No finish line.

                In 1973 the planners Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber called some problems “wicked”: problems that resist a clean definition and have no final solution. Extreme heat is one of them.

                1. There is no single cause to fix

                  Heat arrives through land, bodies, cities and wallets at the same time, and each system pushes on the others.

                2. Every solution changes the problem

                  Air conditioning cools a room and heats the street, a textbook case of . Shifting work to cooler hours protects one worker and cuts another’s wages.

                3. The problem feeds itself

                  Dry soils, melting ice, burning forests and fossil-powered cooling all loop back to make the heat worse.

                4. It lands unequally

                  The same heatwave is an inconvenience for some and a catastrophe for others: in a city flat or a rainfed field, on a coast or in a village hours from a clinic. The difference is protection, not temperature. See who heat hits hardest.

                How this map works

                What the map shows,
                and what it doesn’t.

                An illustrative systems map

                The links come from published evidence and expert judgment. Switching an action on shows which cycles it could help interrupt, not what it will achieve. Real effect depends on how many people it reaches, how well it is delivered and how long it lasts.

                Three kinds of evidence

                Evidence that one thing leads to another. Evidence that an action works. Evidence that a whole cycle runs as drawn. Well established means the whole cycle has been studied; Evidence emerging means each link has evidence but the full cycle has been studied less. The sources under “See the evidence” mostly show that links exist.

                What works, and at what cost

                This map focuses on how problems, institutions and actions connect. For ratings of how well interventions work, what they cost and how soon they help, see What Works Against Heat (opens in a new tab) ↗, launched at the 2026 Global Heat Forum by the Global Heat Health Information Network and the University of Sydney.
