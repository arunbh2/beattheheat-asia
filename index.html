                Who heat hits hardest

                Same heatwave.
                Different barriers.

                Heat is not only a city problem. It reaches farms that depend on rain, coasts and riverbanks, and villages far from any clinic. Who is harmed depends on two things together: how exposed people are, and what stands between them and safety. Age, gender, disability, illness, social identity, insecure tenure and unpaid care shape five questions.

                  Weather, work and the person

                  People in the same community may need different routes to the same outcome: livelihood security and dignity.

                  Also on the map: intimate partner violence rose about 4.5% with each 1 °C of yearly warmth across India, Nepal and Pakistan (Zhu et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2023 (opens in a new tab)), and 466 million children now live where extremely hot days are at least twice as common as in the 1960s (UNICEF, 2024 (opens in a new tab)).

                                Why “wicked”?

                                No single cause.
                                No single owner.
                                No finish line.

                                In 1973 the planners Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber called some problems “wicked”: problems that resist a clean definition and have no final solution. Extreme heat is one of them.

                                1. There is no single cause to fix

                                  Heat arrives through land, bodies, cities and wallets at the same time, and each system pushes on the others.

                                2. Every solution changes the problem

                                  Air conditioning cools a room and heats the street, a textbook case of . Shifting work to cooler hours protects one worker and cuts another’s wages.

                                3. The problem feeds itself

                                  Dry soils, melting ice, burning forests and fossil-powered cooling all loop back to make the heat worse.

                                4. Adaptation has limits

                                  Places used to heat cope better with hot days, but a study of 2.7 million firms (OECD, 2024; opens in a new tab) found no adaptation to days above 40 °C, and heatwaves did more damage where it was already hot. Past a point, only cutting emissions helps.

                                5. It lands unequally

                                  The same heatwave is an inconvenience for some and a catastrophe for others: in a city flat or a rainfed field, on a coast or in a village hours from a clinic. The difference is protection, not temperature. See who heat hits hardest.

                                An illustrative systems map

                                The links come from published evidence and expert judgment. Switching an action on shows which cycles it could help interrupt, not what it will achieve. Real effect depends on how many people it reaches, how well it is delivered and how long it lasts.

                                Three kinds of evidence

                                Evidence that one thing leads to another. Evidence that an action works. Evidence that a whole cycle runs as drawn. Well established means the whole cycle has been studied; Evidence emerging means each link has evidence but the full cycle has been studied less. The sources under “See the evidence” mostly show that links exist. Each one says what kind of source it is, where and how big it was, and what it cannot tell us. Where we draw a lesson the source does not state, it is marked Our reading, so you can tell our interpretation from the finding. Effects of heat depend on exposure, task and the person, so we avoid claims that hold everywhere.

                                Evidence labels

                                Practical action cards and cases carry a label saying what kind of evidence they cite. It describes the evidence, not how good the action is.
