## Developments worth understanding

### Singapore's heat stress measures for outdoor work become mandatory on 1 December

- Happened: 28 August 2026 (Announced; scheduled to take effect on 1 December 2026)
- Reviewed by Common Shade: 15 November 2026
- Outside this edition's period. Why it is here: Announced in August, before this edition's period, and first reviewed by Common Shade on 15 November 2026. It is included because it takes effect two weeks after the cut-off, which makes it the month's most consequential date.

Common Shade's reading: A requirement, rather than advice, can give workers, worker organisations and inspectors a common standard to refer to. Its value will show in how it is applied, which no source can show before 1 December.

What the sources establish:

- The Ministry of Manpower announced on 28 August 2026 enhancements to its heat stress framework that take effect on 1 December 2026. [1] Establishes: The announcement and the effective date. It does not establish how compliance will be inspected or reported.
- A legal update describes the measures, including drinking water, as mandatory for outdoor work from 1 December 2026. [2] Establishes: What the measures include, as read by a law firm. It is a secondary account, not the legal text.

Limits:

- An effective date is not compliance.
- How the measures reach subcontracted and migrant workers is not assessed in the sources checked.

What would strengthen or change this reading: Inspection or enforcement reports after 1 December 2026, and the legal text that sets the duties.

### The Philippines issues heat stress guidelines and compensation rules, as reported

- Happened: 27 October 2026 (Guidelines reported issued (compensation rules on 24 October, workplace guidelines on 27 October))
- Reviewed by Common Shade: 15 November 2026
- Outside this edition's period. Why it is here: Issued in late October and first reviewed by Common Shade in November. Included because it adds compensation for heat illness to workplace rules, a step beyond prevention.

Common Shade's reading: Compensation rules would mean heat illness at work is recognised as a work injury, which matters for workers' income when heat stops work. That reading depends on the text, which has not been seen.

What the sources establish:

- The Department of Labor and Employment issued guidelines requiring employers to protect workers from heat stress during El Niño. [3] Establishes: That a news report describes the guidelines. The advisory text itself, its coverage and its effective date are not verified.
- The Department set out how heat stress and heat-related illness at work are compensated. [4] Establishes: That a news report describes the rules. Claims filed or paid are not verified.

Limits:

- Both items rest on news reports; the original advisories were not located among the sources checked.
- Guidelines are not implementation, and compensation rules are not compensation paid.

What would strengthen or change this reading: The advisory texts, and compensation claims for heat illness being filed and paid.

### A survey of outdoor workers in Viet Nam shows why the question matters

- Happened: June 2026 (Survey reported)
- Reviewed by Common Shade: 15 November 2026
- Outside this edition's period. Why it is here: Reported in June 2026 and first reviewed by Common Shade in November. Included as context for the rules above: it describes the exposure the rules address, in two of the region's largest cities.

Common Shade's reading: Self-reported problems at this scale suggest the exposure that workplace rules address is common in large cities, not confined to a few trades.

What the sources establish:

- A 2026 study with UNDP surveyed 734 outdoor workers in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City; 94% reported at least one heat-related health problem while working during heatwaves. [5] Establishes: What workers reported, as summarised by a ministry. The answers are self-reported, and how workers were sampled is not known until the original study is read.

Limits:

- A secondary summary of the study, not the study itself.
- Self-reported health problems are not diagnoses, and the sample may not represent all outdoor workers.

What would strengthen or change this reading: The original study, with its sampling and questions, and whether Viet Nam's labour or social protection rules add heat.

## An opportunity the evidence supports

### Making a workplace heat rule checkable

A rule's value shows in how it is applied; published inspection findings on heat would let workers, employers and funders see that, and no source checked shows such findings yet.

- The problem: Outdoor workers covered by new heat rules, including subcontracted and migrant workers who may be hardest to reach.
- What to examine: Published inspection or enforcement findings on heat measures, by sector and by type of employment, so a rule's reach can be seen.
- The evidence:
  - How compliance with Singapore's measures will be inspected and reported is not set out in the sources checked. [1] Establishes: That the announcement sets the duties and the date. It does not describe reporting.
  - For the Philippines guidelines, the advisory texts, coverage and effective dates were not located among the sources checked. [3] Establishes: Only that a news report describes the guidelines.
- Its limits: Inspection records show what inspectors find, not every workplace. Publishing findings depends on the inspectorate's own rules and capacity.
- Who could take part (a proposal): A possible arrangement, for discussion. No organisation has agreed to it. Authorise: The authorities that set workplace rules. Deliver: Labour inspectorates, with employers' and workers' organisations. Finance: Public budgets, with philanthropy or research funding for an independent review. Maintain: Labour inspectorates and worker organisations.
- The main unresolved dependency: Whether inspection findings are recorded by sector and type of employment, and can be published.
- A next question to check locally: Ask the labour inspectorate where you work whether heat measures are part of its inspections, and whether its findings are published.

## A question that matters for action

### What public record would show whether a heat rule at work reaches the workers it covers?

> When a heat rule for outdoor work takes effect, what public record would show whether it reaches subcontracted and migrant workers?

- Why it matters now: A rule takes effect in Singapore on 1 December, and guidelines were reported in the Philippines in October. The first months after a rule takes effect are when a record of its reach is cheapest to start.
- What is known: The rules and their dates are known from an official announcement and from news reports. Most outdoor workers surveyed in Viet Nam's two largest cities report heat-related health problems.
- What is not known: How compliance is inspected and recorded, and whether records distinguish subcontracted and migrant workers.
- Which decision depends on it: Whether worker organisations, inspectorates and funders invest in monitoring a rule's reach, and what that monitoring records.
- What could answer it: An account from an inspectorate of how heat is inspected, or an independent review of a rule's first season, recording who is covered (our suggestion).

Revisiting the October 2026 question (How do heat-health pilots in Southeast Asia keep their institutional footing once their first funding ends?): new evidence: None was added to the knowledge base by the cut-off. Did the reading change? No: the reading in October stands. Still unresolved: Which parts of a pilot continue after the first grant, and who pays for them.
