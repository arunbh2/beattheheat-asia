Practical actions against extreme heat, by setting

34 practical actions against extreme heat in workplaces, homes, schools, health facilities, markets and neighbourhoods, and what municipalities can do before summer. Each answers eight questions: what to do, who it protects, where it suits, who must act, what it takes to run, the evidence, what could stop it, and what it only works with.

Evidence labels: Design concept: Designed, but not yet tried by the people it is for. Simulation or test: Tried in a drill, simulation or small trial. Reported, not evaluated: Described by those running it or in the news; nobody independent has checked the results. Official guidance or data: What a government or UN agency advises or counts. It shows what is expected, not what works. Measured in a study: A study, survey or analysis measured it, often the size of the problem rather than the effect of the fix. Independently evaluated: Someone outside the programme measured whether the action worked.

Outdoor and hot workplaces

People who cannot stop working when it is hot: builders, vendors, riders, farm, factory and sanitation workers. Print these cards · Open them on the map · Options, costs and traps

What workers told researchers: Stopping work means losing income · There is nowhere to recover · Safe drinking water costs too much · Home stays hot after the shift · Warnings don’t reach us in a useful way · No one local to install or fix it. People’s Courage International: Hot Cities Make Hard Work Harder, a study with workers in Delhi, Kathmandu, Dhaka, Jakarta and Quezon City

Worker cooling stations

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · Before the season

  • What to do: Rest points where workers already are, with shade or cooling, seating, drinking water, rehydration salts, a first-aid kit and a way to reach a clinic.
  • Who it protects: Construction workers, vendors, drivers, porters and loaders between jobs or on breaks.
  • Where it suits: Construction sites, markets, bus and taxi stands, labour chowks and community spaces with heavy foot traffic.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Employers and businesses, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Water, ice and salts every day; cleaning; power for fans; someone on site; repairs before each season.
  • Evidence: The Migrants Resilience Collaborative reported that more than 27,000 workers used its cooling stations in India, Nepal and Bangladesh in one summer. These are the programme’s own figures; health and income effects have not been independently measured. People’s Courage International, 2025: emergency response to the heatwave crisis in South and Southeast Asia
  • What could stop it: A station nobody can leave work to use, or one that closes when the day is hottest.
  • Works only if: Employers let workers use it without losing pay; It is open when people work; Someone refills and repairs it every day.

Delivery details:

  • Where: On the routes workers really use, not where land happens to be free
  • Opening hours: Matched to working hours, including early mornings and evenings
  • Services: Shade or cooling, safe water, seating, first aid, referral to a clinic
  • Run by: A named person who checks, cleans, refills and repairs it
  • Access barriers: Lost wages, permission to leave work, disability access, who feels welcome
  • How you will know: Visits, days open, user views, and health or income outcomes measured properly

Where it has been tried (Reported, not evaluated): At a Bengaluru construction site, a station cooled with water-soaked khus panels drew about 100–120 workers a day in April, falling to 70–80 after the monsoon began. The Migration Story

Warnings that reach workers

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · During the heat

  • What to do: Route official alerts through trusted worker messengers, voice notes and picture boards at stalls and labour chowks, with one clear action in each message and a way to report back.
  • Who it protects: Vendors, labourers and anyone without a smartphone, signal or the time to read.
  • Where it suits: Markets, labour chowks and worker unions or associations with leaders people trust.
  • Who must act: Weather and climate services, Worker organisations and unions, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: A messenger’s time every alert day, printed boards, and someone who answers when workers report a problem.
  • Evidence: In Delhi in 2025, the India Meteorological Department, worker organisations and Greenpeace India set up colour-coded heat boards at vending carts and sent bilingual audio and text alerts on WhatsApp to more than 20 worker leaders, who passed them on. Greenpeace India: inside India’s first community-led early warning system for informal workers
  • What could stop it: Alerts that tell people to stay indoors when they cannot, and no one listening when they report back.
  • Works only if: Each message comes with an action people can take; A named messenger passes it on; Workers can report missing water, shade or help, and someone responds.

Where it has been tried (Reported, not evaluated): Vendors call themselves ‘climate messengers’; workers who cannot read ask them what the board means. Mongabay India, July 2025

Safe water people do not have to buy

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · Over years

  • What to do: Free or cheap drinking water close to where people live and work: piped connections, community water points or refill stations.
  • Who it protects: Workers and families who now buy water and drink less than they need when it costs too much.
  • Where it suits: Informal settlements and work areas where water has to be bought.
  • Who must act: Water authorities, City and local governments, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Upkeep of pipes and points, water quality testing, and a fair way to bill or share costs.
  • Evidence: In Dhaka, the city water utility worked with local NGOs to bring 3,000 water connections into informal settlements through mini-networks joined to the city system. Agence Française de Développement: improving access to drinking water in Dhaka’s informal settlements
  • What could stop it: Rules that tie a connection to land title, so people in informal settlements cannot get one.
  • Works only if: Connections do not require land title; Water is tested and safe; Someone local can fix a broken point fast.

Local jobs to install and keep it working

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · Over years

  • What to do: Train and pay local people to install, run and repair cool roofs, water points, shade and green spaces.
  • Who it protects: Everyone who depends on the equipment working, and the workers who gain a skill and income.
  • Where it suits: Any programme that builds things that need upkeep.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups, Development banks, donors and climate funds
  • Running and upkeep: Wages and training, built into the running budget from the start.
  • Evidence: Community-run drinking water plants in Delhi’s low-income settlements created local jobs in running the plants and distributing water. Kumar, 2024: water security through the Tata Power DDL RO water plants
  • What could stop it: Projects that train people once and then stop paying them.
  • Works only if: Upkeep jobs are paid for every year; Training leads to real work.

Portable shade where there is no wall or tree

Evidence: Design concept · Before the season

  • What to do: A light shelter with a bench and shade that can be moved between work sites or fixed to a wall.
  • Who it protects: Outdoor workers at sites with no shade at all.
  • Where it suits: Short-term sites, roadsides and markets.
  • Who must act: Employers and businesses, City and local governments, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Moving and fixing it, and repairs; someone must own it.
  • Evidence: Bengaluru’s Neralu shelter won a 2024 design contest: about 15 kg, seats four, costs about US$175, and can be carried on an auto-rickshaw. It had not yet been tried by workers when it was shown in May 2025. AP News, June 2025, via KSAT
  • What could stop it: Nobody owning it once the pilot ends.
  • Works only if: Workers test the design first; Someone owns, moves and repairs it.

Paid rest breaks that switch on with the alert

Evidence: Measured in a study · During the heat

  • What to do: Agree rest, shade and water breaks that start automatically at each alert level, with earlier or split shifts on the hottest days.
  • Who it protects: Construction, farm, delivery, sanitation and street workers.
  • Where it suits: Any worksite where an employer, contractor or city department sets the hours.
  • Who must act: Employers and businesses, Worker organisations and unions, National governments, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: Little equipment; the cost is paid time. Someone has to check the rule is kept.
  • Evidence: In Singapore, monitors at 24 workplaces found workers could face more heat stress than nearby weather stations reported, so set breaks by heat measured on site. NUS Project HeatSafe, 2024
  • What could stop it: Piece-rate pay and fixed deadlines that make stopping cost money, and no inspection.
  • Works only if: Breaks are paid, or paired with income protection; Heat is measured where people work; Shade and water are a few minutes away; Inspectors or unions can check.

Shade and water on work routes

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · Before the season

  • What to do: Rest shelters with seating, drinking water and rehydration salts along the streets and yards where people work.
  • Who it protects: Street vendors, delivery riders, traffic police, sanitation and construction workers.
  • Where it suits: Busy junctions, markets and work yards. Misting only in dry, breezy spots.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Employers and businesses, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: A daily refill and cleaning roster, often ₹500–2,000 a day per water point, and repairs before each season.
  • Evidence: Nagpur put shade nets over 10–12 busy junctions in May 2026; several tore or collapsed in strong winds, and residents pointed out the heat had begun at the end of March. The Hitavada, 11 May 2026
  • What could stop it: Nobody named to refill and repair it, or it goes up after the heat has started.
  • Works only if: It is in place before the hot season; A named team refills and repairs it; Workers are allowed to stop and use it; Frames are fixed firmly for storms.

Income protection when it is too hot to work

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Cash that pays out automatically when heat crosses a set threshold, so workers can afford to stop.
  • Who it protects: Informal and daily-wage workers, especially women.
  • Where it suits: Where workers belong to an association, cooperative or scheme that can enrol them and pay by phone.
  • Who must act: Banks, insurers and financial regulators, Worker organisations and unions, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: Premiums or a public subsidy every season, plus enrolment and payment systems.
  • Evidence: In a survey of informal workers in Bangkok, nearly 80% had cut their hours because of heat. WIEGO and HomeNet Thailand, 2025
  • What could stop it: A trigger that misses the heat people feel, so they are harmed without a payout, and slow payments.
  • Works only if: The trigger counts humidity, not only temperature; Money arrives within days; Workers know they are covered.

Heat-illness first aid on every shift

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · During the heat

  • What to do: Train supervisors and co-workers to spot heat illness, cool a person fast and get help, with water, shade and ice at hand.
  • Who it protects: Everyone on the site, especially new and older workers.
  • Where it suits: Construction sites, factories, brick kilns and farms with a supervisor.
  • Who must act: Employers and businesses, Worker organisations and unions, Health authorities
  • Running and upkeep: A refresher before each season, and a steady supply of water and ice.
  • Evidence: Doctors at two Indian hospitals with heatstroke units said more than 90% of the patients they treated for heat illness in 2024 were outdoor workers. Context (Thomson Reuters Foundation), 2024
  • What could stop it: Training given once and forgotten, and no transport to a clinic.
  • Works only if: Someone trained is on every shift; Everyone knows the emergency number; Treatment is free at the clinic.

Measure and cool hot sheds and factories

Evidence: Measured in a study · Over years

  • What to do: Measure heat at the workstations, then cool the roof and walls, add air flow, water and breaks.
  • Who it protects: Garment, factory, kiln and warehouse workers, many of them women.
  • Where it suits: Indoor workplaces under metal roofs or beside furnaces.
  • Who must act: Employers and businesses, Worker organisations and unions, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: A heat meter and someone to read it daily; upkeep of roofs and fans; power for ventilation.
  • Evidence: At three garment factories in Phnom Penh, workers reporting heat symptoms rose from 68% in the cooler months to 88% in the hot months, by their own reports. Chea et al., BMC Public Health, 2025
  • What could stop it: Nobody measuring heat indoors, and factory owners who rent the building.
  • Works only if: Heat is measured where people stand; Workers can report symptoms without risk; Fans have power when it is hottest.

Homes

Where people sleep, cook and care for others, often under thin roofs that stay hot after dark. Print these cards · Open them on the map · Options, costs and traps

Cool roofs

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: A white, solar-reflective coating on tin, asbestos or concrete roofs, so less heat reaches the room below.
  • Who it protects: Families in informal and low-income homes, older people, young children and the sick.
  • Where it suits: Sound roofs in hot, sunny places, where residents can stay long enough to benefit.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups, Development banks, donors and climate funds
  • Running and upkeep: Recoating every few years, and a thermometer to check it works.
  • Evidence: In a two-year trial in Burkina Faso, cool roofs lowered indoor temperatures by 1.2–1.7 °C and residents’ heart rates fell; a trial in about 400 Ahmedabad homes began in 2025. Reuters, via South China Morning Post, 2025
  • What could stop it: Promising degrees nobody measured, renters who cannot change the roof, and no money to recoat.
  • Works only if: Rooms are measured before and after; Tenants are protected from rent rises; Someone pays to recoat.

Where it has been tried (Reported, not evaluated): In Dhaka’s Kerail settlement, reflective paint went on 105 buildings, including a day care centre, a school and two garment factories. Indoor air at peak heat fell by 7.72 °C in one building and by 3.5 °C on average; one roof’s surface fell by 12.3 °C. Thin corrugated roofs made it hard to apply, and some local paints were not reflective enough. Sustainable Energy for All and the Kigali Cooling Efficiency Program, via PreventionWeb, 2021

Where it has been tried (Reported, not evaluated): In an industrial building in Indonesia, a 5,200 m² cool roof brought indoor air at noon down from 40.8 °C to 30.4 °C for about 500 shift workers. These are indoor air readings; roof-surface drops are always larger. Sustainable Energy for All and the Kigali Cooling Efficiency Program, via PreventionWeb, 2021

Shade the walls and let the air through

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Bamboo blinds, jute screens or awnings on the sunny side, and higher vents that let hot air out at night.
  • Who it protects: Everyone indoors, especially at night.
  • Where it suits: Most homes; screens are cheap and households can fit them.
  • Who must act: Community and civil society groups, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: Fixing and replacing screens each season; vents need rain and mosquito screens.
  • Evidence: In low-income homes measured in Dhaka, Delhi, Faisalabad and rural Maharashtra, tin-roofed houses stayed warmer indoors than outside because they cooled so slowly at night. Tasgaonkar et al., Scientific Data, 2022
  • What could stop it: Openings that let in dust, rain or intruders, and screens that block all air.
  • Works only if: Air can still move; Vents are secure; Someone replaces worn screens.

Fans and power people can afford

Evidence: Measured in a study · Over years

  • What to do: Efficient fans, lifeline electricity tariffs and reliable supply in the hottest weeks.
  • Who it protects: Low-income households without any way to cool down.
  • Where it suits: Homes with a grid connection; solar where the grid is weak.
  • Who must act: Utilities and energy regulators, City and local governments, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: Monthly power bills, and a grid that holds in a heatwave.
  • Evidence: 1.12 billion of the rural and urban poor are at high risk because they lack access to cooling. Sustainable Energy for All, 2023
  • What could stop it: Power cuts at the hottest hours, and bills people cannot pay.
  • Works only if: Power is reliable when it is hottest; Tariffs protect the poorest; Fans are efficient.

Cooler, cleaner kitchens

Evidence: Measured in a study · Over years

  • What to do: Let heat and smoke out of the kitchen, move cooking to cooler hours, and make cleaner fuel affordable through the season.
  • Who it protects: Women and girls who cook, and children near the stove.
  • Where it suits: Homes cooking indoors on wood, dung, kerosene or gas.
  • Who must act: Community and civil society groups, Utilities and energy regulators, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: Fuel costs every month, and upkeep of vents and chimneys.
  • Evidence: In Nepal’s Madhesh Province, women cooking in hot, smoky kitchens carry a daily ‘thermal labour’ that falls hardest on women in the poorest single-room homes with little access to clean energy or credit. Ghimire and others, Frontiers in Public Health, 2025
  • What could stop it: Clean fuel bought once but not refilled, and kitchens with no way for air to escape.
  • Works only if: Refills stay affordable all season; There is an opening for heat and smoke; Women decide how it is designed.

Check on people alone through hot nights

Evidence: Measured in a study · During the heat

  • What to do: Neighbours, health workers or volunteers call or visit older, ill and disabled people who live alone, day and night, during alerts.
  • Who it protects: Older people, people with disabilities or long-term illness, people living alone.
  • Where it suits: Any neighbourhood with a list of who lives alone.
  • Who must act: Community and civil society groups, Health authorities, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: A list kept up to date, and volunteers’ or health workers’ time during alerts.
  • Evidence: Data from 47,628 people in 68 countries shows hot nights shorten sleep, with bigger losses for older people and in lower-income countries. Minor et al., One Earth, 2022
  • What could stop it: No list of who lives alone, and calls that stop at sunset.
  • Works only if: Someone keeps the list; Calls continue after dark; Callers know when to send help.

Schools and childcare

Children in hot classrooms learn less and fall ill more easily. Most fixes are small and seasonal. Print these cards · Open them on the map · Options, costs and traps

Cool the hottest classrooms first

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: A reflective coating or a ventilated second roof over the classrooms that get hottest.
  • Who it protects: Pupils and teachers.
  • Where it suits: Single-storey schools with metal or concrete roofs.
  • Who must act: Schools and education authorities, City and local governments, Development banks, donors and climate funds
  • Running and upkeep: A line in the school maintenance budget for recoating; anchors checked before the monsoon.
  • Evidence: In the United States, each 1 °F hotter school year without air conditioning cut that year’s learning by about 1%, with the largest losses for disadvantaged students. Effects in Asian classrooms are not yet measured. Park, Goodman, Hurwitz and Smith, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2020
  • What could stop it: Canopies not anchored for monsoon winds, and no budget to recoat.
  • Works only if: Classroom heat is logged before and after; There is money to maintain it.

Water, and time to drink it

Evidence: Official guidance or data · Before the season

  • What to do: Enough safe, cool drinking water for every child, with breaks in the timetable to use it.
  • Who it protects: Every pupil, especially the youngest.
  • Where it suits: Every school.
  • Who must act: Schools and education authorities, Water authorities
  • Running and upkeep: Filter changes, cleaning and a supply that lasts the hottest weeks.
  • Evidence: Heatwaves disrupted schooling for an estimated 171 million students in 2024, and South Asia was the most affected region. UNICEF, 2025
  • What could stop it: One tap for hundreds of children, or filters nobody changes.
  • Works only if: Enough taps for the number of children; Teachers build drinking into the day.

Heat-ready timetables

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · During the heat

  • What to do: Start earlier, shorten the hottest afternoons, move exams and sports, and keep learning going if schools must close.
  • Who it protects: Pupils, teachers and parents who travel in the heat.
  • Where it suits: Education departments and school boards, set before the season.
  • Who must act: Schools and education authorities, National governments, Weather and climate services
  • Running and upkeep: Little money; it needs agreed triggers and a plan for remote or make-up learning.
  • Evidence: Bangladesh closed schools for about 33 million children in its April 2024 heatwave, and Cambodia cut the school day by two hours. NBC News, 2024
  • What could stop it: Closing schools with no plan for learning, and decisions made on the day.
  • Works only if: Triggers are agreed before the season; There is a plan to make up lost learning.

Shaded play space

Evidence: Measured in a study · Over years

  • What to do: Trees, shade structures and less hard paving where children play and queue.
  • Who it protects: Children at break times and assembly.
  • Where it suits: Schools with open yards and a water source.
  • Who must act: Schools and education authorities, City and local governments, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Watering through the holidays, when nobody is at school.
  • Evidence: Researchers studying how age groups use green spaces found children wanted trees for shade and places to play: ask them before you design. Frantzeskaki et al., chapter 11 in Nature-Based Solutions for Cities, 2023
  • What could stop it: Planting that dies in the first summer break.
  • Works only if: Someone waters through the holidays; Children help choose where shade goes.

Health facilities

Clinics and hospitals fill up in a heatwave just as power, water and staff come under strain. Print these cards · Open them on the map

Ready to cool heatstroke patients fast

Evidence: Official guidance or data · During the heat

  • What to do: Rapid assessment and active cooling of suspected heatstroke, with ice packs, IV fluids, rehydration salts and trained staff.
  • Who it protects: Anyone with heat illness, above all outdoor workers and older people.
  • Where it suits: Every hospital and health centre in heat-prone districts.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: Stocks of ice, fluids and medicines through the season, and training every year.
  • Evidence: In June 2024 India’s health ministry asked central government hospitals to set up special heatwave units, and advised rapid assessment and active cooling of suspected heatstroke, with stocks of rehydration salts, IV fluids and ice packs. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, India, press release, 19 June 2024
  • What could stop it: Supplies that run out at the peak, and staff who have never practised the protocol.
  • Works only if: Staff train before the season; Ice and fluids are stocked early; Care for heat illness is free.

Keep the power on and the building cool

Evidence: Official guidance or data · Over years

  • What to do: Backup or solar power, cooling in waiting and treatment areas, and cool roofs, shading and rainwater harvesting.
  • Who it protects: Patients, staff, and medicines and vaccines that spoil in heat.
  • Where it suits: Facilities on weak grids or under hot roofs, especially in rural areas.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, Utilities and energy regulators, Development banks, donors and climate funds
  • Running and upkeep: Fuel or battery upkeep, servicing of cooling, and a power company that prioritises the facility.
  • Evidence: About 12% of health facilities in low- and lower-middle-income countries in South Asia have no electricity at all, and rural facilities are worse served than urban ones. WHO fact sheet: electricity in health-care facilities, 2023
  • What could stop it: Backup generators with no fuel budget, and cooling nobody services.
  • Works only if: The power company knows the facility comes first; Someone services the backup; There is a running budget.

Protect the staff

Evidence: Measured in a study · During the heat

  • What to do: Cool rest rooms, drinking water, and shorter rotations for staff in full protective gear.
  • Who it protects: Nurses, doctors, community health workers and cleaners.
  • Where it suits: Wards, emergency rooms and outreach teams in hot weather.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, Worker organisations and unions
  • Running and upkeep: Staff time for rotations, and a cooled rest space.
  • Evidence: Across 3,082 health workers in India, Indonesia, Singapore, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam, poor practices with protective equipment were linked to heat-related symptoms. Yang et al., BMC Nursing, 2024
  • What could stop it: Too few staff to rotate during a surge.
  • Works only if: Rotas plan for the surge; Staff can report symptoms early.

A surge plan linked to the warning

Evidence: Independently evaluated · During the heat

  • What to do: Extra beds, staff and supplies that switch on with each alert level, agreed with the weather service and the city.
  • Who it protects: Everyone who falls ill in a heatwave.
  • Where it suits: District and city health systems with a heat warning.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, Weather and climate services, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: Planning time before the season, and overtime or reserve staff during alerts.
  • Evidence: After Ahmedabad’s heat action plan prepared hospitals and warned the public, an estimated 1,190 deaths a year were avoided. Hess et al., Journal of Environmental and Public Health, 2018
  • What could stop it: Warnings that reach the public but not the hospital.
  • Works only if: Hospitals receive the alert directly; Each level names what the hospital does.

Shade and water for people waiting outside

Evidence: Official guidance or data · Before the season

  • What to do: Shade, seating and drinking water where patients queue, and appointment slots so fewer wait at midday.
  • Who it protects: Patients and carers, especially older people, pregnant women and children.
  • Where it suits: Outpatient departments, health centres and vaccination days.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: Someone to refill water and keep the space usable.
  • Evidence: India’s June 2024 advisory asked facilities in heat-prone areas to provide shade outside, and cooling in waiting areas. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, India, press release, 19 June 2024
  • What could stop it: Shade built where the queue isn’t.
  • Works only if: It is where people actually wait; Someone refills the water.

Markets and neighbourhoods

The streets, markets, stops and public spaces where people spend the hottest hours. Print these cards · Open them on the map · Options, costs and traps

Shade and water in markets, designed with vendors

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Shade over stalls and aisles, and drinking water points, planned and run with the vendors’ association.
  • Who it protects: Street vendors and their customers.
  • Where it suits: Open and street markets.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups, Worker organisations and unions
  • Running and upkeep: Storm-proof fixing, replacing worn cloth, and a refill and cleaning roster.
  • Evidence: Of 721 Delhi street vendors surveyed in April and May 2024, 80% saw fewer customers during heatwaves and 49% lost income. Greenpeace India and National Hawkers Federation, 2024
  • What could stop it: Beautification that pushes informal vendors out, and shade that blocks fire access.
  • Works only if: Vendors have a say in design and upkeep; Nobody is evicted to make room.

Cool waiting points

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Shade, a bench, drinking water and the heat alert on a board, at bus stops, ration shops and other queues.
  • Who it protects: Commuters, older and disabled people, carers with children.
  • Where it suits: Busy stops and queues with no shade.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Refilling the water, and keeping access clear for wheelchairs.
  • Evidence: Measuring how hot people feel on summer days, researchers found shade from buildings cooled most by day, then trees, then light structures such as sails. Middel et al., 50 Grades of Shade, Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society
  • What could stop it: Counting shelters built instead of people protected.
  • Works only if: It is where people actually wait; Older and disabled people can reach it.

Cooling centres people can actually use

Evidence: Official guidance or data · During the heat

  • What to do: Cool public spaces, such as libraries, community halls or places of worship, opened during alerts, with water and somewhere to sit.
  • Who it protects: People with no cool place at home, and outdoor workers on breaks.
  • Where it suits: Neighbourhoods with many hot homes and a suitable public building nearby.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups, Health authorities
  • Running and upkeep: Staff, power and water for every alert day, and transport for those who cannot walk there.
  • Evidence: A review of the evidence and strategies for running cooling centres to prevent heat-related illness. US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, in the GHHIN resource library
  • What could stop it: A cooled building nobody can reach, open at the wrong hours, or a worker who cannot leave work to use it.
  • Works only if: People can reach it, including those who can’t walk far; It opens at the right hours, evenings too; Power and water are secure; Someone staffs and funds it; Workers are free to leave work to use it.

Trees that survive

Evidence: Measured in a study · Over years

  • What to do: Look after existing trees first; plant heat-hardy species where people walk and wait, with water and three years of care.
  • Who it protects: Pedestrians, vendors and everyone waiting outside.
  • Where it suits: Streets and open spaces with root space and a water source.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Water authorities, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Watering and care for at least three years after planting.
  • Evidence: In one Berlin park more than 500 of 870 new trees died within ten years despite watering: count trees that survive, not saplings planted. Hansen et al., chapter 10 in Nature-Based Solutions for Cities, 2023
  • What could stop it: No water plan, and counting saplings instead of canopy.
  • Works only if: Water is secured before planting; Someone is paid to care for them; Greening doesn’t push residents out.

Before summer: for municipalities

The decisions a city or district makes before the first heatwave decide what happens during it. Print these cards · Open them on the map

Name an owner and set aside the money

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Give heat a named lead office, and ring-fence a heat budget before the season starts.
  • Who it protects: Everyone the plan covers, by making sure it can be paid for.
  • Where it suits: Every city, district or state with a heat plan, or about to write one.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, National governments, Development banks, donors and climate funds
  • Running and upkeep: A budget line each year, and staff time in the lead office.
  • Evidence: Of 37 Indian heat action plans reviewed, 11 discussed funding, and 8 of those simply asked departments to find their own money. Centre for Policy Research, 2023
  • What could stop it: A plan with no money and no single owner.
  • Works only if: The money is set aside before the heat; One office answers for the plan.

Set local alert levels, and who acts at each

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Set thresholds from local health records, and agree who decides, who acts, who pays and by when at each level.
  • Who it protects: Everyone, by turning warnings into action.
  • Where it suits: Cities and districts with a weather forecast and health data.
  • Who must act: Weather and climate services, Health authorities, City and local governments
  • Running and upkeep: A yearly review of thresholds and who does what.
  • Evidence: Ahmedabad set its alert temperatures from the local link between daily maximum temperature and deaths; in 2023 only about 10 of 37 Indian heat plans appeared to set local thresholds. Centre for Policy Research, 2023
  • What could stop it: A warning with no named person who must act on it.
  • Works only if: Every level has someone named to act; Warnings reach people without phones.

Map who is most exposed, with them

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Find the neighbourhoods, workers and households most at risk, by walking the area with residents and their organisations.
  • Who it protects: The people a plan usually misses.
  • Where it suits: Every ward or district, before choosing actions.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Community and civil society groups, Health authorities
  • Running and upkeep: A few weeks of fieldwork, updated every year or two.
  • Evidence: Of the 37 Indian heat action plans reviewed, only two included a vulnerability assessment. Centre for Policy Research, 2023
  • What could stop it: Mapping from an office, without the people at risk.
  • Works only if: Residents and workers take part; The map changes what gets built.

Get the assets ready before the heat

Evidence: Reported, not evaluated · Before the season

  • What to do: Shade, water points, cooling spaces and roof coatings installed and tested by the end of winter.
  • Who it protects: Outdoor workers, people in hot homes, people waiting outside.
  • Where it suits: Any city with a known hot season.
  • Who must act: City and local governments, Water authorities, Community and civil society groups
  • Running and upkeep: Upkeep through the season, with named teams and a budget.
  • Evidence: Nagpur’s junction shade nets went up in May 2026, though residents said the heat had begun at the end of March. The Hitavada, 11 May 2026
  • What could stop it: Procurement that starts when the heat does.
  • Works only if: Contracts are signed in winter; Every asset has someone to keep it working.

Prepare clinics, and count heat illness

Evidence: Official guidance or data · During the heat

  • What to do: Check clinics are ready, and record heat illness and deaths every day of the season so the plan can respond.
  • Who it protects: Everyone who falls ill, and future plans that need the numbers.
  • Where it suits: District health offices and hospitals.
  • Who must act: Health authorities, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: Daily reporting through the season, and training for doctors who certify deaths.
  • Evidence: India’s surveillance logged 48,385 suspected heatstroke cases by 28 July 2024, but only 185 confirmed heatstroke deaths. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, reply in India’s Rajya Sabha, 6 August 2024
  • What could stop it: Deaths recorded as heart failure, so heat stays invisible.
  • Works only if: Doctors know how to record heat as a cause; Numbers are reviewed during the season.

Release money on the forecast

Evidence: Measured in a study · Before the season

  • What to do: Agree in advance what money is released, and for what, when the forecast crosses a threshold, before the heat peaks.
  • Who it protects: People who would otherwise get help only after the damage.
  • Where it suits: Cities and districts with a reliable forecast and a pre-agreed fund.
  • Who must act: Weather and climate services, Development banks, donors and climate funds, National governments
  • Running and upkeep: A fund topped up each year, and rules for releasing it.
  • Evidence: US$800 million spent on early warning systems in developing countries could avoid US$3–16 billion in losses every year. Global Commission on Adaptation, 2019
  • What could stop it: Money that needs a declared disaster before it can move.
  • Works only if: Release rules are agreed in advance; Money reaches people within days.

Where it has been tried (Simulation or test): In Dhaka, the Red Cross and Red Crescent tested heatwave triggers in a 2021 simulation and gave cash grants of BDT 4,500; most recipients spent it on food, and many on medical costs. In April 2024 the Bangladesh Red Crescent activated its heatwave early action protocol for real. Anticipation Hub: the story of heatwave anticipatory action in Dhaka

What is known:

  • Doctors at two Indian hospitals said more than 90% of their heat-illness patients in 2024 were outdoor workers. Context (Thomson Reuters Foundation), 2024

Bangladesh

  • Rahim, rickshaw puller. Rahim rents a rickshaw from a garage owner every morning. Whatever he earns, the day’s rent is owed when he returns it.

What is known:

  • Rickshaw pullers in Dhaka have described their earnings falling from about Tk 1,200 to Tk 700–800 a day in extreme heat, with fewer passengers and shorter hours. The Business Standard
  • In a 2024 heatwave, one puller returned his rickshaw after a few hours and still owed the day’s Tk 150 rent. The Daily Star, 2024
  • In April 2024 the Bangladesh Red Crescent acted on the heat forecast in Dhaka: cash grants of at least Tk 5,000 to 4,000 households, three cooling stations with more than 15,000 visits, and caps for 3,500 rickshaw and van pullers. Rockefeller Foundation case study, 2024
  • Dhaka North appointed a chief heat officer in 2023, and the city has given rickshaw pullers umbrellas, water and saline, and planned shaded cooling zones. The Daily Observer, October 2024

Nepal

  • Kamala, brick-kiln worker. Kamala moulds bricks in the open from first light, paid by the thousand. She took a pay advance from the labour contractor before the season; part of each day’s pay goes against it.

What is known:

  • A national survey found 176,373 manual workers, including family members, in Nepal’s brick kilns, which operate seasonally from October to June. Over three-quarters (75.7%) received a pay advance from a labour contractor. Central Bureau of Statistics, ILO and UNICEF, 2020
  • In late May 2026, temperatures in Dhangadhi and Nepalgunj in the western Terai reached around 38 °C before noon, and heatstroke patients were arriving at Bheri Hospital every day. Kantipur, 2026
  • Nepal recorded 89 days above the historical 90th-percentile temperature between May 2024 and May 2025. The Kathmandu Post, 2026
  • Under the national health insurance programme, a family of up to five pays Rs 3,500 a year for treatment worth up to Rs 100,000. The Himalayan Times
  • Researchers in Nepal have proposed heat stress limits for different workloads, drawing on measurements in sectors including brick kilns. Heat Standard Across Different Workloads in Nepal, 2024

Sri Lanka

  • Nimal, fish seller. Nimal buys fish at the landing site at dawn and sells it from an iced box on his bicycle. In the heat, fish spoils faster: he needs more ice, and what does not sell by midday loses its value.

What is known:

  • In March 2024 the Department of Meteorology issued heat advisories for the Western Province and others, urging people to stay hydrated and work in the shade. News 1st, 2024
  • In 2023, when fish traders at Peliyagoda raised concerns about the price of ice, the Ministry of Fisheries brought traders and ice suppliers together and agreed a lower price: Rs 650 for 50 kg instead of Rs 850. Ministry of Fisheries, 2023
  • A national strategy on fish loss names the high cost of energy for fishing, ice and transport as a weakness, and aims to cut quality loss in the multi-day fishery by 30% by 2033. FAO: food loss and waste in fish value chains, Sri Lanka
  • Sri Lanka has provided free public health care to its whole population since 1951. ILO social protection brief

Cambodia

What is known:

  • The 2026 minimum wage for garment, footwear and travel goods workers is US$210 a month, plus a US$7 transport or housing allowance and a US$10 monthly attendance bonus. DFDL, on Prakas 214/25, 2025
  • In April 2024 the Ministry of Labour asked factories to spray water on roofs, install enough fans, open windows, provide more clean drinking water and assign staff to watch workplace thermometers. CamboJA News, 2024
  • At three garment factories in Phnom Penh, workers reporting heat symptoms rose from 68% in the cooler months to 88% in the hot months, by their own reports. Chea et al., BMC Public Health, 2025
  • The National Social Security Fund’s health care scheme covers private-sector workers at contracted public and private facilities. UN Global Marketplace notice, 2024

Indonesia

Singapore

  • Kumar, migrant construction worker. Kumar works on a building site and sleeps in a dormitory room. His basic pay is monthly; overtime is what he sends home. The law already gives him rest breaks and medical care.

What is known:

  • Singapore’s heat stress framework for outdoor work requires rest under shade, regular drinking water and at least a week of acclimatisation, with hourly rest breaks of at least 15 minutes for heavy work in the highest heat stress.   • The Ministry of Manpower has taken enforcement action against workplaces found not meeting the heat measures, including for inadequate shaded rest breaks. HRD Asia
  • Employers must pay for medical treatment of their Work Permit holders and hold at least S$60,000 of medical insurance a year for each. Ministry of Manpower, written answer, 2025
  • Standards for new dormitories, announced in 2021, cap rooms at 12 residents and require enough fans for air circulation; existing dormitories are to be upgraded over time. Ministry of Manpower, 2021
