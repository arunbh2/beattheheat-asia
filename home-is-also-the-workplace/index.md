# When home is also the workplace

Where do you recover when the place you earn, cook, care and sleep remains hot?

For many home-based workers, the room is also the workshop, the kitchen and the bedroom. Heat from the roof, the walls, a stove and machines builds through the day and is still there at night, when the body would normally recover. Weather forecasts describe the air outside; the heat that matters here is indoors.

### What the evidence says

- Worker-organisation research HomeNet South Asia's study of 202 women home-based workers describes how climate change reaches paid work done at home, alongside unpaid care work. [HomeNet South Asia](https://www.homenetinternational.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/HNSA-Impact-of-Climate-Change-on-Home-based-workers.pdf), 2022
- Worker-organisation research WIEGO and HomeNet Thailand studied how heat affects home-based workers and street vendors in Bangkok (2025). [WIEGO and HomeNet Thailand](https://www.wiego.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Too-Hot-to-Work-Heat-Impacts-on-Home-Based-Workers-and-Street-Vendors-in-Bangkok.pdf), 2025
- Guidance WHO notes that gender can shape heat exposure, for example where women are mainly responsible for cooking indoors during hot weather. [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health), 2026
- Guidance WHO describes urban and rural poor people as often more exposed to overheating because of low-quality housing and lack of access to cooling. [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health), 2026
- Modelling study A biophysical modelling study of healthy young adults found that whether a fan helps depends on humidity-dependent temperature thresholds. [Morris et al., The Lancet Planetary Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34119011/), 2021

Each line says what kind of evidence it is. Guidance recommends; a study observes; a report describes what an organisation did. None of them alone shows that a measure improved health.

## A day in one home

Illustrative situation, a composite rather than a real person or household. No temperatures or health outcomes are predicted.

A two-room rented home with a metal roof. One adult does garment piecework here for a contractor; another works outside; a school-age child and an older relative share the rooms. Choose a part of the day.

### Morning

**Where the heat comes from**: The night's heat still held in a tin or concrete roof; little air movement in a small room.

**What people are doing, and what limits them**: Water collection, cooking breakfast on a stove indoors, getting a child ready for school, helping an older relative.

**What could protect them**: Shade or reflective treatment on the roof; a window or vent that can open safely; an earlier water supply schedule.

**Who can make it happen**: Landlord or housing authority (roof), the water utility (supply times), the household (who does which task).

**What needs measuring**: Indoor temperature and humidity at waking; who did the morning tasks, and how long they took.

### Paid work

**Where the heat comes from**: Heat from the roof and walls building through the day; machines or irons adding heat; work done close to the hottest surfaces.

**What people are doing, and what limits them**: Piecework against a delivery date set by a contractor; breaks cost output; light needed for close work keeps shutters half closed.

**What could protect them**: Delivery dates that move in heat waves; piece rates that allow for slower work; a cooler shared work space nearby; reliable power for a fan where humidity allows.

**Who can make it happen**: The contractor and the buyer above them (dates and rates), municipal or worker-run work centres, the electricity provider.

**What needs measuring**: Hours worked in the hottest part of the day; earnings in a hot week compared with a cooler one.

### Cooking and care

**Where the heat comes from**: A stove in a closed room; steam; a child or older person who needs attention in the same heat.

**What people are doing, and what limits them**: Cooking the main meal, feeding and washing, care for an older or ill relative, often by the same person who does the paid work.

**What could protect them**: A ventilated or outdoor cooking space; cleaner, cooler stoves; care services that stay open in heat, so care is shared rather than piled on one person.

**Who can make it happen**: Housing and clean-cooking programmes, health and care services, other household members.

**What needs measuring**: Time spent on care in hot weeks; who covers when a service closes.

### Evening

**Where the heat comes from**: Walls and roof releasing stored heat; outdoor air cooling faster than the room.

**What people are doing, and what limits them**: Catching up on paid work after care; travel to a market or a clinic; deciding whether to run a fan or a cooler, and who pays for it.

**What could protect them**: Affordable electricity tariffs in heat waves; cooling spaces open in the evening and safe to reach; space to work outdoors safely.

**Who can make it happen**: Electricity regulators and providers, the municipality (cooling spaces, lighting, transport).

**What needs measuring**: Electricity spending and cuts in hot weeks; whether evening cooling spaces are used, and by whom.

### Night-time recovery

**Where the heat comes from**: Indoor heat that does not drop; several people sharing one room; doors and windows closed for safety or privacy.

**What people are doing, and what limits them**: Sleep, interrupted by heat, a child or an older person; work that was not finished in the day.

**What could protect them**: Roof insulation or shading, safe night ventilation, reliable power, and protection from losing a home after it is improved.

**Who can make it happen**: Landlords, housing programmes and their rules on rent and tenure, electricity providers.

**What needs measuring**: Indoor temperature at night; hours of sleep; whether improvements led to higher rent.

## What could change, and on what it depends

- [Cool, affordable housing](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#coolhousing) Roof and ceiling improvements, external shading and ventilation lower indoor heat; they work differently by climate, building and season.**Watch for:** In 16 Ahmedabad homes compared over one week, most roof treatments lowered daytime indoor heat, but one coating left rooms hotter than untreated roofs. Designs differ, and night-time effects were not measured.
- [Affordable cooling for the poorest](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#affordablecool) Suitable cooling and electricity people can afford to run, with reliable supply.**Watch for:** In laboratory trials with older adults, fans slightly cooled the body in hot, humid air but raised body temperature in very hot, dry air. These were short trials, not homes.
- [Cool nights and support](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#coolnights) Support for cooler nights, so recovery is possible.
- [Income protection in heatwaves](https://beattheheat.asia/actions/#heatcash) Cash that pays out automatically when temperatures cross a danger threshold, so people can afford to stop. Triggers need careful design to limit basis risk: heat that hurts people without triggering a payout.**Watch for:** A payout trigger set too high can pay nothing: the first year of a heat insurance scheme for informal women workers in India paid nothing for that reason, until the threshold was redesigned.

Trade-offs to plan for

- Opening a window may not be possible for security, privacy, noise or pollution.
- A better roof can bring a higher rent or a threat to tenure, unless tenants are protected.
- Moving work out of the hottest hours can push it into the evening, eating into rest.
- A fan helps only below humidity-dependent temperature limits, and electricity has to be there and affordable.
- Contractors and buyers set delivery dates and piece rates; changing them changes whether rest is possible.

Tenant–landlord agreements on improvements and rent, buyer and contractor arrangements on dates and rates, and finance that covers upkeep as well as installation all decide whether a physical fix reaches the people inside. Solutions that name tenant protections: [Solutions library, filtered](https://beattheheat.asia/solutions/?eq=v-tenure&eqs=any#passive).

### What we know, and what we do not

**What we know**:
- Gender can shape exposure where women cook indoors in hot weather.
- Poor housing and lack of cooling increase exposure.

**What has been tested**:
- Few evaluations in the sources checked here follow both indoor temperature and the health or earnings of people who work at home.

**What remains uncertain**:
- Whether roof and ventilation changes improve sleep and recovery, not only indoor temperature.
- Whether improvements lead to higher rent or eviction for tenants.

**What a useful next study would ask**: Do housing improvements support recovery as well as reduce indoor temperature, and for whom in the household?

**What needs measuring**: Indoor temperature by day and night, sleep, earnings in hot weeks, rent after improvement.

Not the same thing: a cooler room and better health; a service being open and people being able to use it; a warning sent and protective action taken; a committee seat and a say in decisions; reported symptoms and a diagnosis; an association and a cause.
