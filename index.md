## What does a hot day do to people?

Before any diagram: a single hot day, lived by people at home, at work and in the services everyone depends on. Each moment is drawn from the evidence.

- **An older woman living alone**She has a fan but rations it to keep the electricity bill down. On a hot night she falls ill, the cost of care adds to what she owes, and next summer she can afford to run the fan even less.

- **A nurse at a primary health centre**On the hottest days the waiting room fills with people who are dizzy, vomiting or confused. With so many waiting, some are seen late, and by then their illness has become more serious.

- **A water-pump operator on the city’s edge**During a heatwave the power fails, so the pumps stop and taps run dry. Meanwhile the power station upstream is short of water to cool itself, and the cuts come more often.

- **A neighbourhood gardener**She waters the street trees she planted years ago, but a hot, dry summer still kills some of them. Without their shade the pavement heats up, and the trees that remain struggle even more.

- **A girl in her early teens**When heat closes her school for weeks and money is tight at home, she starts helping her mother instead. She falls behind, and each year it becomes harder for her family to send her back.

- **A day-wage construction worker**He knows the midday sun is dangerous, but stopping means losing a day’s pay. When the heat makes him ill, he loses several days instead, and the next week he cannot afford to rest at all.

A composite, drawn from the evidence for each cycle. Not a real person.

Why “wicked”?

## No single cause.
No single owner.
No finish line.

In 1973 the planners Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber called some problems “wicked”: problems that resist a clean definition and have no final solution. Extreme heat is one of them.

- There is no single cause to fix

Heat arrives through land, bodies, cities and wallets at the same time, and each system pushes on the others.

- Every solution changes the problem

Air conditioning cools a room and heats the street, a textbook case of . Shifting work to cooler hours protects one worker and cuts another’s wages.

- The problem feeds itself

Dry soils, melting ice, burning forests and fossil-powered cooling all loop back to make the heat worse.

- Adaptation has limits

Places used to heat cope better with hot days, but [a study of 2.7 million firms](https://doi.org/10.1787/19d94638-en) found no adaptation to days above 40 °C, and heatwaves did more damage where it was already hot. Past a point, only cutting emissions helps.

- It lands unequally

The same heatwave is an inconvenience for some and a catastrophe for others: in a city flat or a rainfed field, on a coast or in a village hours from a clinic. The difference is protection, not temperature. [See who heat hits hardest](#at-risk).

How this map works

## What the map shows,
and what it doesn’t.

### An illustrative systems map

The links come from published evidence and expert judgment. Switching an action on shows which cycles it could help interrupt, not what it will achieve. Real effect depends on how many people it reaches, how well it is delivered and how long it lasts.

### Three kinds of evidence

Evidence that one thing leads to another. Evidence that an action works. Evidence that a whole cycle runs as drawn. **Well established** means the whole cycle has been studied; **Evidence emerging** means each link has evidence but the full cycle has been studied less. The sources under “See the evidence” mostly show that links exist. Each one says what kind of source it is, where and how big it was, and what it cannot tell us. Where we draw a lesson the source does not state, it is marked **Our reading**, so you can tell our interpretation from the finding. Effects of heat depend on exposure, task and the person, so we avoid claims that hold everywhere.

### Evidence labels

Practical action cards and cases carry a label saying what kind of evidence they cite. It describes the evidence, not how good the action is.

### What works, and at what cost

This map focuses on how problems, institutions and actions connect. For ratings of how well interventions work, what they cost and how soon they help, see [What Works Against Heat ](https://wmo.int/node/39067), launched at the 2026 Global Heat Forum by the Global Heat Health Information Network and the University of Sydney.

### Timing and the heat network

“How soon it works” tags are editorial judgments. Organisations are listed for relevant published work: inclusion does not imply partnership, affiliation or endorsement, and a low count shows a gap in this directory’s coverage, not proof that nobody is working on it.

### Privacy

Visits are counted with [GoatCounter](https://www.goatcounter.com/), which uses no cookies, collects no personal data and does not follow you across sites. It also counts which tools get used, such as films watched and plans printed. What you type into a plan stays in your own browser.
