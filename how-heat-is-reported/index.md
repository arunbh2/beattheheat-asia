# The damage is well reported. The strongest reporting also follows how protection is organised, paid for and kept going.

Extreme heat is now reported as a human and economic crisis. A collection of 71 articles from South and Southeast Asia shows three well-covered areas: the human toll, lost work and income, and cooling. The connection between them is reported less often: why known, measurable heat risks have not yet become steady protection for workers, reliable funding, and clear responsibility.

## Six recurring patterns

## The missing middle

Two valuable lenses run through the collection. Economic commentary links heat to growth, construction, food prices, power and decarbonisation. Close-up reporting follows delivery riders, port engineers, safety rules and earnings. Between the two sits a question asked less often.

The big picture

Productivity, growth, competitiveness.

The under-examined middle

**Who pays? Who coordinates? What incentives change? Who is accountable?**

The close-up

Worker safety, wages, households, practical cooling.

This is not an unreported subject. It is a chance to connect existing reporting into a clearer account of how an economic problem becomes an organised, funded response.

## A wider look, across languages

To test the first reading, a pilot review looked for reporting on work and protection in the countries and languages the collection missed. It found more of the missing middle than expected. The sharper question is not whether protection is reported, but whether coverage follows it all the way: from a named responsibility, through money and delivery, to results.

## Reporting that connects the dots

Six pieces that follow protection furthest: who pays, who decides and who checks. They are offered as examples to learn from, not as a ranking, and a report is not the same as a finding.

### Workers measuring the heat

Combines worker-collected exposure data, factory responses, legal standards and an undisclosed-budget question.

**Who pays**: Factory describes infrastructure spending; actual budget is not disclosed.

**Who decides**: Managers control equipment and production; public bodies set rules.

**Who checks**: Reporters compare measurements and worker accounts with employer statements and regulatory documents.

[Heat stress growing with targets, strain on workers](https://mekongindependent.com/2026/10/heat-stress-growing-with-targets-strain-on-workers/)Mekong Independent · 2026 · Cambodia · English

### From fainting incidents to inspection

Connects specific fainting incidents with corrective orders, inspection demands and a request for clarification.

**Who pays**: Factories are asked to improve conditions; actual expenditure not disclosed.

**Who decides**: Labour authorities issue orders; employers implement; unions call for stronger inspections.

**Who checks**: Unions question compliance; ministry clarification is sought. Completed inspection findings are not established.

### Power supply and factory heat

Links worker heat to electricity agreements and outages, retaining competing explanations and seeking response.

**Who pays**: Power tariffs and investment are discussed; disputed payment claims remain attributed.

**Who decides**: Electricity authorities and plant operators affect supply; factories control indoor protection.

**Who checks**: Union complaints, cited documents and requests for response are used; allegations are not adjudicated.

[Myanmar’s worsening power woes leave factory workers in dangerous heat](https://www.rfa.org/english/myanmar/2024/11/27/myanmar-energy-crisis/)Radio Free Asia · 2024 · Myanmar · English

### Going back to an insurance pilot

Returns to a no-payout pilot and asks what redesign and cash layers changed for women.

**Who pays**: Member contributions and programme support are described; whole long-term financing is not independently audited.

**Who decides**: SEWA and insurance partners redesign thresholds and assistance.

**Who checks**: Reporters revisit previous results and hear worker experiences; a causal impact evaluation is not presented.

[Rising Temperatures, Rising Inequalities: How a New Insurance Protects India’s Poorest Women](https://www.ipsnews.net/2025/06/rising-temperatures-rising-inequalities-how-a-new-insurance-protects-indias-poorest-women/)Inter Press Service · 2025 · India · English

Explains trigger setting, premium subsidy and the trade-off between wider eligibility and payment size.

**Who decides**: Insurers and risk modellers set parameters; implementing organisations negotiate changes.

## Three deeper patterns

### Heat can become a poverty trap, not just lost hours

A worker too hot to work loses income, so may work longer or in worse conditions, gets ill, and has less to spend on rest or cooling. A study of nearly 400 workers in two Delhi settlements followed daily for a month in the summer of 2019 found their net earnings were about **40% lower** during the two heatwaves in that month, and medical spending rose. It describes those workers in that period, not a national figure. [Das and Somanathan, Environmental Research Letters, 2024](https://heathealth.info/resources/heat-causes-large-earnings-losses-for-informal-sector-workers-in-india/)

On the map

[The poverty–heat trap](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#poverty), and the solutions that can break it

### Heat hits markets, not only workers’ bodies

Street vendors can lose working hours and customers at the same time, and take on more unpaid care at home. In a WIEGO survey of Delhi street vendors cited by the [OECD Development Centre](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/securing-the-livelihoods-of-informal-economy-workers-in-times-of-global-changes_7b0d17a9-en/full-report/informal-workers-and-emerging-risks-in-the-era-of-environmental-degradation-and-climate-change_3e3c5b34.html), **96% said they had fewer customers** in extreme heat; in Bangkok, [nearly 80% of surveyed workers](https://www.wiego.org/advocacy-worker-education-resources/too-hot-to-work-home-based-workers-street-vendors-bangkok/) cut their working hours. The same report estimates heat costs informal workers about **2.4% of working hours**, against 1.4% for formal workers ([as summarised by The Policy Edge](https://www.policyedge.in/p/oecd-finds-informal-workers-face-unequal-risks-from-heat-automation-and-ageing)). In Dhaka, [IIED’s 2026 study](https://www.iied.org/22755iied) found outdoor workers lose about 24 working days a year to heat, and garment workers indoors report heat exhaustion almost as often. Its national estimates come in two sizes: wage losses alone (0.91% of GDP), and those losses plus modelled kidney-disease costs (1.38%), so figures quoted in the news can differ without contradicting each other.

On the map

[The heat credit squeeze](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#squeeze), and the solutions that can break it

### Protection has a shared-benefit, single-payer problem

A cooler workplace helps workers, the factory, its buyers, insurers and the public purse, but often no single one of them has reason or money to pay for all of it. [AP’s 2026 reporting from India’s textile factories](https://apnews.com/article/eab8494242ecfdc108e12685535a4df3) shows cooling competing with every other cost an employer faces.

On the map

[The protection-gap loop](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#protection), and the solutions that can break it

## Questions worth investigating

For reporters, researchers and anyone who funds or plans protection. A strong evidence base is now emerging beyond India and Singapore: [IIED’s August 2026 study of informal workers in Dhaka](https://www.iied.org/22755iied) is one place to start.

- Industry and investmentHow much of the region’s manufacturing depends on people and machines working safely in hotter conditions, and which investment decisions would need to change?
- Informal workWhen rest breaks protect health but cut earnings, who could share the cost: employers, platforms, public programmes or insurance? Which approaches have evidence that they work?
- CoordinationWhy do heat warnings, worker protection, cooling, health services and social protection so often sit with different institutions, and what does good coordination look like in one city?

## Five questions, and where this site answers them

Every link between a harm and a response on Common Shade is a question someone can check: each claim carries its source and how strong the evidence is.
