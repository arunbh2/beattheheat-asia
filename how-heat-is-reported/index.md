# Heat’s damage is well reported. Does reporting follow protection all the way?

A first reading of 71 English-language articles suggested that how protection is organised, paid for and kept going was rarely reported. A pilot that read 32 articles in full, in 10 languages, found more of it than expected. Together they point to a more precise question: does coverage follow protection from a named responsibility, through money and delivery, to results?

**Watch: Follow the protection** · 0:20 · narrated, with captions

For journalists, researchers, practitioners and funders: find reporting across countries and languages, see what each article contains, and follow how protection is authorised, financed, delivered and examined.

## What we first read, and what a wider look changed

- 1 · The first reading

### The damage is well reported; how protection is organised looked thin

A curated collection of 71 English-language articles (2018 to October 2026, from 22 outlets) covers three areas well: the human toll, lost work and income, and cooling. Read at the level of headlines and listings, the connection between them looked less reported: why known, measurable heat risks have not yet become steady protection for workers, reliable funding and clear responsibility.

The pilot does not yet compare coverage before, during and after heatwaves, or set coverage against measured heat. Every article links to the original. These examples are worth learning from; they do not show that one language reports heat better than another.

- 3 · The sharper question

### Not whether protection is reported, but whether coverage follows it all the way

The combined reading changes the question: from a named responsibility, through money and delivery, to results. Of the 22 pilot articles that describe how protection is organised, 7 revisit an earlier action or incident, and 2 cite budget figures. [Show the follow-ups](?collection=pilot&has=followup#explore)

The big picture

Productivity, growth, competitiveness.

The under-examined middle

**Who pays? Who coordinates? What incentives change? Who is accountable?**

The close-up

Worker safety, wages, households, practical cooling.

This is not an unreported subject. Economic commentary links heat to growth, construction, food prices, power and decarbonisation; close-up reporting follows delivery riders, port engineers, safety rules and earnings. The opportunity is to connect existing reporting into a clearer account of how an economic problem becomes an organised, funded response.

**Read with care.** Neither collection is a sample of all reporting, and a gap in them is not an absence of reporting. The curated collection was read at listing depth and the pilot in full, so their counts are not compared with each other.

## Develop a reporting question

A starting point for an investigation, built from the records on this page and Common Shade's own pages. It suggests kinds of records and perspectives to look for; it does not name documents that exist or people to interview, and its question is not a finding.

- **Heat at work.** In the place you report on, who is responsible for protecting people most exposed to heat from heat at work, what resources pay for that protection, and what public record would show whether it reaches them?
- **Income when heat stops work.** In the place you report on, when heat stops work for people most exposed to heat, what replaces lost income, who pays for it, and what records show payments reaching people?
- **Schools and children.** In the place you report on, how does heat guidance reach people most exposed to heat, who decides on closures or changed hours, and what resources support learning in the heat?
- **Health services and warnings.** In the place you report on, how do heat warnings turn into action for people most exposed to heat, which services prepare, and what records show who was reached?
- **Power and water in the heat.** In the place you report on, who decides how power and water are shared during a heatwave, and how does that affect people most exposed to heat?
- **Counting heat's toll.** In the place you report on, how are heat-related deaths and illness among people most exposed to heat recorded, and how do those records shape the resources that follow?

## What research adds to the reporting

Three patterns where checked research, rather than news reports, establishes the figures. Each figure was checked against its source in October 2026.

### Heat can become a poverty trap, not just lost hours

A worker too hot to work loses income, so may work longer or in worse conditions, gets ill, and has less to spend on rest or cooling. A study of nearly 400 workers in two Delhi settlements followed daily for a month in the summer of 2019 found their net earnings were about **40% lower** during the two heatwaves in that month, and medical spending rose. It describes those workers in that period, not a national figure. [Das and Somanathan, Environmental Research Letters, 2024](https://heathealth.info/resources/heat-causes-large-earnings-losses-for-informal-sector-workers-in-india/)

On the map

[The poverty–heat trap](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#poverty), and the solutions that can break it

### Heat hits markets, not only workers’ bodies

Street vendors can lose working hours and customers at the same time, and take on more unpaid care at home. In a WIEGO survey of Delhi street vendors cited by the [OECD Development Centre](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/securing-the-livelihoods-of-informal-economy-workers-in-times-of-global-changes_7b0d17a9-en/full-report/informal-workers-and-emerging-risks-in-the-era-of-environmental-degradation-and-climate-change_3e3c5b34.html), **96% said they had fewer customers** in extreme heat; in Bangkok, [nearly 80% of surveyed workers](https://www.wiego.org/advocacy-worker-education-resources/too-hot-to-work-home-based-workers-street-vendors-bangkok/) cut their working hours. The same report estimates heat costs informal workers about **2.4% of working hours**, against 1.4% for formal workers ([as summarised by The Policy Edge](https://www.policyedge.in/p/oecd-finds-informal-workers-face-unequal-risks-from-heat-automation-and-ageing)). In Dhaka, [IIED’s 2026 study](https://www.iied.org/22755iied) found outdoor workers lose about 24 working days a year to heat, and garment workers indoors report heat exhaustion almost as often. Its national estimates come in two sizes: wage losses alone (0.91% of GDP), and those losses plus modelled kidney-disease costs (1.38%), so figures quoted in the news can differ without contradicting each other.

On the map

[The heat credit squeeze](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#squeeze), and the solutions that can break it

### Protection has a shared-benefit, single-payer problem

A cooler workplace helps workers, the factory, its buyers, insurers and the public purse, but often no single one of them has reason or money to pay for all of it. [AP’s 2026 reporting from India’s textile factories](https://apnews.com/article/eab8494242ecfdc108e12685535a4df3) shows cooling competing with every other cost an employer faces.

On the map

[The protection-gap loop](https://beattheheat.asia/loops/#protection), and the solutions that can break it

### Questions worth investigating

For reporters, researchers and anyone who funds or plans protection. A strong evidence base is now emerging beyond India and Singapore: [IIED’s August 2026 study of informal workers in Dhaka](https://www.iied.org/22755iied) is one place to start.

- Industry and investment How much of the region’s manufacturing depends on people and machines working safely in hotter conditions, and which investment decisions would need to change?
- Informal work When rest breaks protect health but cut earnings, who could share the cost: employers, platforms, public programmes or insurance? Which approaches have evidence that they work?
- Coordination Why do heat warnings, worker protection, cooling, health services and social protection so often sit with different institutions, and what does good coordination look like in one city?

## How this page was made

**Curated collection**: Gathered in October 2026 by searching for prominent English-language reporting on heat in South and Southeast Asia. The searches captured more of 2024's prominent reporting, and some countries not at all. Depth of review: headline and listing. Headline, outlet, year, place and a short story angle written by the curator. The full text was not coded; patterns drawn from it rest on headlines, descriptions and some full texts not recorded one by one.

**Multilingual pilot review**: Found in October 2026 by searching for reporting on work and protection in the countries and languages the collection missed, then read one by one. Chosen to see what such reporting can look like, not how common it is. Depth of review: full text, provisional coding. The full article was opened and read, and coded once with AI assistance, without a second coder. Every code cites the passage it rests on. Treat the codes as provisional until reviewed.

**Counting**: 103 publication records (71 in the collection, 32 in the pilot) make 101 unique records: 2 stories appear in both, matched by their address. A check for near-identical headlines found no other copy of the same story; syndicated copies are noted on their record. Counts on this page say whether they count the collection, the pilot or unique records.

**Translation**: 18 of 32 pilot headlines are in a national language, each with the reviewer’s English translation, which the publisher has not authorised and a native speaker has not yet reviewed. Original titles are shown first, in their own script and direction.

Coding definitions

**Organisation of protection described**: The pilot's code for an article that names who acts and explains how help is delivered, paid for or followed up, at more than a passing mention (the codebook's levels 2 and 3). Levels 0 and 1 mean the reviewer did not find that depth in the text read.

**Follow-up**: The article revisits a specific earlier promise, action or incident.

**Affected people speak**: A worker, vendor, parent or resident directly affected describes heat in their own words, not only through officials or experts.

**Informal work; women and care**: A passage in the reviewed text concerns informal work, or gender and unpaid care, under the codebook's definition.

**Cycle on the map**: The pilot's provisional link between the article and a vicious cycle on Common Shade's map.

**Publication record and story**: A publication record is one listing of one URL. The same story can appear in both collections, or under another outlet's name as syndicated copy. Counts on this page say which they count.

**Reporting trail states**: Described in the reviewed material; Explicitly reported as unknown; Not found in the reviewed material; Not assessed. These describe what the review found in the article, not what happened in the world. "Not found" means the reviewer did not find it in the text read, not that a journalist left something out or that an institution failed. "Not assessed" means the review did not look.

The pilot's full codebook is not in the repository. The definitions above are taken from the codes and the evidence notes each record carries.

About this reading, and what it is not

This is an editorial reading, not a claim that nobody has reported how protection is organised; several journalists have covered heat’s human and economic effects in depth. The collection was curated in October 2026, and the searches captured more of 2024’s prominent reporting, so the counts describe the collection, not all coverage. Patterns come from headlines, descriptions and selected full texts, not a formal content analysis; any stronger research claim would need a sampling method and a second coder. Headlines are the outlets’ own; listing an article is not an endorsement of its framing. Nothing here scores or ranks outlets, countries or languages.

A link check on 30 October 2026 opened the collection’s links: some publishers block automated checks or ask for payment, and every article was kept. Articles and headlines belong to their publishers and authors; this page links to them and quotes only short passages from the review’s own notes.
