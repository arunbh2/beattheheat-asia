    Heat in the news · a reading of the coverage

    The damage is well reported. The strongest reporting also follows how protection is organised, paid for and kept going.

    Extreme heat is now reported as a human and economic crisis. A collection of 71 articles from South and Southeast Asia shows three well-covered areas: the human toll, lost work and income, and cooling. The connection between them is reported less often: why known, measurable heat risks have not yet become steady protection for workers, reliable funding, and clear responsibility.

    About this reading. This is an editorial question, not a claim that nobody has reported it; several journalists have covered heat’s human and economic effects in depth. The collection was curated in October 2026, and the searches captured more of 2024’s prominent reporting, so the counts describe the collection, not all coverage. Patterns come from headlines, descriptions and selected full texts, not a formal content analysis. Headlines are the outlets’ own; listing an article is not an endorsement of its framing.

    The missing middle

    Two valuable lenses run through the collection. Economic commentary links heat to growth, construction, food prices, power and decarbonisation. Close-up reporting follows delivery riders, port engineers, safety rules and earnings. Between the two sits a question asked less often.

    The big picture

    Productivity, growth, competitiveness.

    The under-examined middle

    Who pays? Who coordinates? What incentives change? Who is accountable?

    The close-up

    Worker safety, wages, households, practical cooling.

    This is not an unreported subject. It is a chance to connect existing reporting into a clearer account of how an economic problem becomes an organised, funded response.

    A wider look, across languages

    To test the first reading, a pilot review looked for reporting on work and protection in the countries and languages the collection missed. It found more of the missing middle than expected. The sharper question is not whether protection is reported, but whether coverage follows it all the way: from a named responsibility, through money and delivery, to results.

    32articles, each opened and readChecked one by one in October 2026, from 13 countries, published 2023 to 2026.
    18 of 32not in English10 languages in all, from Nepali and Sinhala to Filipino, Thai and Vietnamese.
    22 of 32explain how protection is organisedThey name who acts, describe how help is delivered or paid for, or follow up on what happened.
    13let affected people speakWorkers, vendors, parents or residents describe heat in their own words, not only through officials or experts.

    Reporting that connects the dots

    Six pieces that follow protection furthest: who pays, who decides and who checks. They are offered as examples to learn from, not as a ranking, and a report is not the same as a finding.

    Workers measuring the heat

    Combines worker-collected exposure data, factory responses, legal standards and an undisclosed-budget question.

    Who pays
    Factory describes infrastructure spending; actual budget is not disclosed.
    Who decides
    Managers control equipment and production; public bodies set rules.
    Who checks
    Reporters compare measurements and worker accounts with employer statements and regulatory documents.

    Three deeper patterns

    Heat can become a poverty trap, not just lost hours

    A worker too hot to work loses income, so may work longer or in worse conditions, gets ill, and has less to spend on rest or cooling. A study of nearly 400 workers in two Delhi settlements followed daily for a month in the summer of 2019 found their net earnings were about 40% lower during the two heatwaves in that month, and medical spending rose. It describes those workers in that period, not a national figure. Das and Somanathan, Environmental Research Letters, 2024 (opens in a new tab)

    On the map

    The poverty–heat trap, and the solutions that can break it

    Heat hits markets, not only workers’ bodies

    Street vendors can lose working hours and customers at the same time, and take on more unpaid care at home. In a WIEGO survey of Delhi street vendors cited by the OECD Development Centre (opens in a new tab), 96% said they had fewer customers in extreme heat; in Bangkok, nearly 80% of surveyed workers (opens in a new tab) cut their working hours. The same report estimates heat costs informal workers about 2.4% of working hours, against 1.4% for formal workers (as summarised by The Policy Edge (opens in a new tab)). In Dhaka, IIED’s 2026 study (opens in a new tab) found outdoor workers lose about 24 working days a year to heat, and garment workers indoors report heat exhaustion almost as often. Its national estimates come in two sizes: wage losses alone (0.91% of GDP), and those losses plus modelled kidney-disease costs (1.38%), so figures quoted in the news can differ without contradicting each other.

    On the map

    The heat credit squeeze, and the solutions that can break it

    Protection has a shared-benefit, single-payer problem

    A cooler workplace helps workers, the factory, its buyers, insurers and the public purse, but often no single one of them has reason or money to pay for all of it. AP’s 2026 reporting from India’s textile factories (opens in a new tab) shows cooling competing with every other cost an employer faces.

    The protection-gap loop, and the solutions that can break it

    Questions worth investigating

    For reporters, researchers and anyone who funds or plans protection. A strong evidence base is now emerging beyond India and Singapore: IIED’s August 2026 study of informal workers in Dhaka (opens in a new tab) is one place to start.

    1. Industry and investmentHow much of the region’s manufacturing depends on people and machines working safely in hotter conditions, and which investment decisions would need to change?
    2. Informal workWhen rest breaks protect health but cut earnings, who could share the cost: employers, platforms, public programmes or insurance? Which approaches have evidence that they work?
    3. CoordinationWhy do heat warnings, worker protection, cooling, health services and social protection so often sit with different institutions, and what does good coordination look like in one city?

    Five questions, and where this site answers them

    Every link between a harm and a response on Common Shade is a question someone can check: each claim carries its source and how strong the evidence is. A reporter following delivery riders, for example, can trace heat to pay per delivery, platform rules, rest spaces, city responsibilities and household budgets.
