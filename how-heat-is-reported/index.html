Heat in the news · a reading of the coverage

The damage is well reported. How protection is organised, paid for and kept going is less so.

Extreme heat is now reported as a human and economic crisis. A collection of 71 articles from South and Southeast Asia shows three well-covered areas: the human toll, lost work and income, and cooling. The connection between them is reported less often: why known, measurable heat risks have not yet become steady protection for workers, reliable funding, and clear responsibility.

About this reading. This is an editorial question, not a claim that nobody has reported it; several journalists have covered heat’s human and economic effects in depth. The collection was curated in October 2026, and the searches captured more of 2024’s prominent reporting, so the counts describe the collection, not all coverage. Patterns come from headlines, descriptions and selected full texts, not a formal content analysis. Headlines are the outlets’ own; listing an article is not an endorsement of its framing.

The missing middle

Two lenses run through the collection. Economic commentary, such as David Fickling’s Bloomberg Opinion columns, links heat to growth, construction, food prices, power and decarbonisation. Close-up reporting, such as David Fogarty’s for The Straits Times, follows delivery riders, port engineers, safety rules and earnings. Between the two sits a question asked less often.

The big picture

Productivity, growth, competitiveness.

The under-examined middle

Who pays? Who coordinates? What incentives change? Who is accountable?

The close-up

Worker safety, wages, households, practical cooling.

This is not an unreported subject. It is a chance to connect existing reporting into a clearer account of how an economic problem becomes an organised, funded response.

Three deeper patterns

Heat can become a poverty trap, not just lost hours

A worker too hot to work loses income, so may work longer or in worse conditions, gets ill, and has less to spend on rest or cooling. A study of nearly 400 workers in two Delhi settlements found their net earnings were about 40% lower during the two heatwaves in the study period, and medical spending rose. Das and Somanathan, Environmental Research Letters, 2024 (opens in a new tab)

On the map

The poverty–heat trap, and the solutions that can break it

Heat hits markets, not only workers’ bodies

Street vendors can lose working hours and customers at the same time, and take on more unpaid care at home. An OECD Development Centre report estimates heat costs informal workers about 2.4% of working hours, against 1.4% for formal workers (as summarised by The Policy Edge (opens in a new tab)). In Dhaka, IIED’s 2026 study (opens in a new tab) found outdoor workers lose about 24 working days a year to heat, and garment workers indoors report heat exhaustion almost as often.

On the map

The heat credit squeeze, and the solutions that can break it

Protection has a shared-benefit, single-payer problem

A cooler workplace helps workers, the factory, its buyers, insurers and the public purse, but often no single one of them has reason or money to pay for all of it. AP’s 2026 reporting from India’s textile factories (opens in a new tab) shows cooling competing with every other cost an employer faces.

The protection-gap loop, and the solutions that can break it

Questions worth investigating

For reporters, researchers and anyone who funds or plans protection. A strong evidence base is now emerging beyond India and Singapore: IIED’s August 2026 study of informal workers in Dhaka (opens in a new tab) is one place to start.

  1. Industry and investmentHow much of the region’s manufacturing depends on people and machines working safely in hotter conditions, and which investment decisions would need to change?
  2. Informal workWhen rest breaks protect health but cut earnings, who could share the cost: employers, platforms, public programmes or insurance? Which approaches have evidence that they work?
  3. CoordinationWhy do heat warnings, worker protection, cooling, health services and social protection so often sit with different institutions, and what does good coordination look like in one city?

Five questions, and where this site answers them

Every link between a harm and a response on Common Shade is a question someone can check: each claim carries its source and how strong the evidence is. A reporter following delivery riders, for example, can trace heat to pay per delivery, platform rules, rest spaces, city responsibilities and household budgets.
