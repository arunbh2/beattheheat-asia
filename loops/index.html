    Vicious cycles of extreme heat, and where they can be broken

    21 vicious cycles in which extreme heat feeds itself, traps people or overwhelms institutions, with where each one plays out in Asia, the evidence, and the actions that could break it.

    Open the interactive map

    Loops that feed the heat itself

    The global cooling loop

    Cooling powered by fossil fuels adds to the warming that makes us need more cooling.

    The cycle: Extreme heat → Energy → Cooling demand peaks → Emissions from cooling rise → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (World): 3×. The energy used for cooling could triple by 2050 as air conditioners grow from 1.6 billion to 5.6 billion, unless they become more efficient. International Energy Agency, 2018

    In Southeast Asia (Southeast Asia): 40m → 300m. air conditioners in Southeast Asia between 2017 and 2040, half of them in Indonesia, unless efficiency improves. Cooling could then make up as much as 30% of the region’s peak electricity demand. International Energy Agency, 2019

    Where it plays out: Fast-growing cities across South and Southeast Asia, where air-conditioner ownership is rising quickly.

    Where it breaks: Clean, efficient cooling

    Solutions to try: Minimum energy performance standards for cooling appliances (cuts the electricity each air conditioner needs, so the same cooling adds less warming); Transition to purpose-designed low-GWP room AC (moves to refrigerants that warm the planet far less when they leak); Solar-reflective roof coatings (keeps buildings cooler without power, so less cooling is needed at all); National cooling action plans and cross-sector implementation roadmaps (plans the country’s cooling demand as a whole, so efficiency and clean power keep pace with growth). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Southeast Asia’s air conditioners could rise from 40 million in 2017 to 300 million in 2040, half of them in Indonesia. Without more efficient units, cooling could drive as much as 30% of the region’s peak electricity demand. International Energy Agency, 2019
      Agency report, with projections · Southeast Asia · Region-wide energy model. Limit: A projection to 2040 under stated policies, not a forecast; it changes with efficiency rules.
    • The world’s stock of air conditioners is set to grow from 1.6 billion to 5.6 billion by 2050, and the energy they use to triple, unless efficiency improves. International Energy Agency, 2018
      Agency report, with projections · Global · Global energy model. Limit: A projection to 2050 that assumes current trends; better efficiency changes it.

    The dry-soil loop

    Heat dries the soil. Dry soil can’t cool itself, so the land heats up faster.

    The cycle: Extreme heat → Nature and ecosystems → Drying soils → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs is not yet measured.

    Where it plays out: Rain-fed farmland on the Indian subcontinent and in mainland Southeast Asia during the hot, dry pre-monsoon months.

    Where it breaks: Deep emission cuts, Keep water in the soil

    Solutions to try: Organic residue mulch for cooler crop root zones (keeps moisture in the soil, so it can keep cooling itself); Shade-tree agroforestry for crops (shades the ground and holds water in the land); Permeable pavements retaining rainfall for evaporative surface cooling (in towns, lets rain soak in instead of running off, so the ground stays moist longer). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Reviews the evidence that dry soils amplify hot temperatures: with less water to evaporate, more of the sun’s energy goes into heating the air. Seneviratne et al., Earth-Science Reviews, 2010
      Peer-reviewed review · Global · Review of many studies. Limit: Explains a general mechanism; how strong it is varies by region and season.

    The melting-ice loop

    Heat melts ice. Darker ground and water absorb more sunlight, and the melting speeds up.

    The cycle: Extreme heat → Nature and ecosystems → Glacier and snow melt → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (Hindu Kush Himalaya): 30–50%. of the region’s glacier ice could be gone by 2100 at 1.5–2 °C of warming, after melting 65% faster in the 2010s than the decade before. ICIMOD, 2023

    In Southeast Asia (Papua, Indonesia): 99%. of the glacier area on Puncak Jaya, Southeast Asia’s last tropical ice, has gone since 1850. Only about 0.17 km² was left in 2024, and it will very likely disappear around 2030. Ibel, Mölg and Sommer, The Cryosphere, 2025

    Where it plays out: The Hindu Kush Himalaya, whose snow and ice feed rivers such as the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra.

    Where it breaks: Deep emission cuts, Cut black carbon

    Solutions to try: Only cutting the warming slows this loop. These cut cooling’s own share of it; nothing local stops the melting. Minimum energy performance standards for cooling appliances (cuts the electricity, and so the emissions, each air conditioner needs); Transition to purpose-designed low-GWP room AC (moves to refrigerants that warm the planet far less); National cooling action plans and cross-sector implementation roadmaps (plans cooling growth so it adds as little warming as possible). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Puncak Jaya’s glaciers, the last in Southeast Asia, have lost more than 99% of their area since 1850 and about 65% since 2018. About 0.165 km² remained in 2024, and they will very likely be gone around 2030. Ibel, Mölg and Sommer, The Cryosphere, 2025
      Peer-reviewed study · Puncak Jaya, Indonesia · One glacier field, satellite and field data. Limit: One site; the 2030 date is the authors’ estimate.
    • Glaciers lost ice 65% faster in the 2010s than in the decade before. At 1.5–2 °C of warming, glaciers across the region lose 30–50% of their volume by 2100. ICIMOD, 2023
      Agency assessment · Hindu Kush Himalaya · Region-wide synthesis. Limit: Volume losses are projections that depend on how much warming happens.

    The wildfire loop

    Heat dries forests into fuel. Fires release carbon and strip away the trees that cool the land.

    The cycle: Extreme heat → Nature and ecosystems → Wildfires → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (World): +50%. more extreme wildfires are projected by the end of the century, and up to 14% more by 2030. UNEP and GRID-Arendal, 2022

    In Southeast Asia (Indonesia): US$16.1 billion. was the cost to Indonesia of the forest and peatland fires of June to October 2015. World Bank, 2016

    Where it plays out: The dry-season burning belt of northern Thailand, Myanmar and Laos, and Indonesia’s peatlands.

    Where it breaks: Deep emission cuts, Fire-smart landscapes

    Solutions to try: Breaking this loop needs forest and fire management, which this library does not yet cover. These protect people while it turns. Locally calibrated impact-based heat-warning triggers (warns people early, based on what the heat will do locally, not just the temperature); Pre-agreed heat anticipatory action and forecast finance (releases agreed money before the worst days, rather than after); Pooled pre-arranged finance for heat action (shares the cost of bad years across places, so one disaster does not empty a budget). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Indonesia’s forest and peatland fires of June to October 2015 cost the country an estimated US$16.1 billion. World Bank, 2016
      Agency report, economic estimate · Indonesia · National, one fire season (2015). Limit: One exceptional year, in an El Niño; costs in other years differ.
    • Climate change and land-use change are making wildfires worse. Extreme fires are projected to rise by up to 14% by 2030 and 50% by the end of the century. UNEP and GRID-Arendal, 2022
      Agency report, with projections · Global · Global assessment. Limit: Global projections; not broken down for Asian countries.

    Loops that break the systems we rely on

    The cooling trap

    Air conditioning heats the street, and a hotter city needs even more air conditioning.

    The cycle: Cooling demand peaks → Urban heat islands → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (Singapore): Up to 7 °C. warmer: how much hotter Singapore’s commercial centre has measured than its outskirts on calm, clear nights. Cooling Singapore, citing Chow and Roth, 2006

    Where it plays out: Dense, high-rise city cores such as Hong Kong, Bangkok and Metro Manila.

    Where it breaks: Cool by design, Green, cool cities

    Solutions to try: Solar-reflective roof coatings (keeps rooms cooler without power, so less air conditioning runs); Street trees and retained mature canopy (shades and cools the street the air conditioners heat); Efficient BLDC ceiling fans (gives comfort for a fraction of the power and waste heat of air conditioning); Coordinated fans and higher-setpoint air conditioning (lets air conditioning run at a higher setting with a fan, cutting its load). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • In a Phoenix heatwave, waste heat from air conditioners raised night-time air temperatures by more than 1 °C in parts of the city. Salamanca et al., Journal of Geophysical Research, 2014 (summary by Arizona State University)
      Peer-reviewed modelling study · Phoenix, United States · One city, simulated heatwave. Limit: A desert city outside Asia; the size of the effect in humid Asian cities is not measured here.
      Our reading: We use it for the mechanism, not the number: waste heat from cooling warms the street outside.
    • China, India and Indonesia are expected to account for half of the world’s air conditioners by 2050. International Energy Agency, 2018
      Agency report, with projections · Global · Global energy model. Limit: A projection to 2050 that assumes current trends.
    • Chennai’s built-up area more than doubled between 2000 and 2020, and night-time land surface temperatures in its urban areas run about 2 °C higher than on its peri-urban edges. Tamil Nadu State Planning Commission, 2024
      Government strategy · Chennai and Tamil Nadu, India · State-wide, satellite land surface temperature. Limit: Land surface temperature, not the air temperature people breathe; the two differ.
    • Singapore’s commercial centre has measured up to about 7 °C warmer than its outskirts on calm, clear nights. Cooling Singapore, citing Chow and Roth, 2006
      Research programme summary of a peer-reviewed study · Singapore · City-wide measurements. Limit: The 7 °C is a maximum on calm, clear nights, not a typical night.

    The energy–water loop

    Water systems need power and power plants need water. In a heatwave, each failure deepens the other.

    The cycle: Power cuts and grid stress → Water shortages → Power plants cut output → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (India): 14 TWh. of electricity lost in India in 2016 because thermal power plants ran short of water. World Resources Institute, 2018

    In Southeast Asia (Vietnam): US$1.4 billion. was the World Bank’s early estimate of what northern Vietnam’s power cuts cost in May and June 2023, about 0.3% of GDP, after heatwaves pushed up demand and dried hydropower reservoirs. World Bank estimate, reported by AFP via The Manila Times, 2023

    Where it plays out: Coal-fired power plants along water-stressed rivers in India.

    Where it breaks: Power that doesn’t need water, Backup power for water

    Solutions to try: Solar/hybrid pumping with water storage for resilient water service (keeps water pumping on solar or stored power when the grid fails); Heatwave grid-readiness planning and emergency resource coordination (prepares the grid and its water supply before the heat season); Demand response and scheduled flexible loads to relieve heat-period peaks (moves flexible loads off the peak, so the grid is less likely to fail); Minimum energy performance standards for cooling appliances (lowers the peak itself, by making every air conditioner use less). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Heatwaves in May and June 2023 pushed up demand while hydropower reservoirs ran low, and rolling blackouts hit northern Vietnam. The World Bank’s early estimate put the cost at about US$1.4 billion, roughly 0.3% of GDP. World Bank estimate, reported by AFP via The Manila Times, 2023
      News report of a World Bank early estimate · Northern Vietnam · National, one season (2023). Limit: An early estimate reported in the press; drought and demand both played a part.
    • 14 of India’s 20 largest thermal utilities shut down at least once for lack of water between 2013 and 2016. Water shortages cost 14 TWh of power in 2016. World Resources Institute, 2018
      Research institute analysis · India · 20 largest thermal utilities, 2013–2016. Limit: Data from before 2017; plants and water rules have changed since.

    The groundwater loop

    Dry fields need more water, so farmers pump harder, aquifers fall and the shortage deepens.

    The cycle: Water shortages → Irrigation demand soars → Groundwater over-pumped → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (India): 109 km³. of groundwater lost from Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana in six years (2002–2008), mainly to irrigation, while rainfall stayed close to normal. Rodell, Velicogna and Famiglietti, Nature, 2009

    In Southeast Asia (Mekong delta, Vietnam): 2.5 cm+. a year: how fast parts of the Mekong delta are sinking as its aquifers are pumped down. Minderhoud et al., Environmental Research Communications, 2020

    Where it plays out: Punjab and Haryana in northwest India, where groundwater tables have been falling for decades.

    Where it breaks: Smart irrigation and groundwater rules

    Solutions to try: Organic residue mulch for cooler crop root zones (keeps water in the root zone, so fields need less pumping); Shade-net structures for crop microclimate protection (cuts the heat and water stress on crops); Shade-tree agroforestry for crops (shades crops and soil, so less water is lost). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Groundwater pumping has sped up the sinking of the Mekong delta, to more than 2.5 cm a year in some areas. Minderhoud et al., Environmental Research Communications, 2020
      Peer-reviewed modelling study · Mekong delta, Vietnam · Delta-wide model. Limit: Sinking rates vary across the delta; the top rate is not everywhere.
    • Satellites show Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana lost 109 km³ of groundwater between 2002 and 2008, mainly to irrigation, while rainfall stayed close to normal. Rodell, Velicogna and Famiglietti, Nature, 2009
      Peer-reviewed study, satellite data · Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana, India · Three states, 2002–2008. Limit: An older period; it measures water lost, not the heat link itself.
      Our reading: The heat link is ours: hotter, drier seasons raise the pull on groundwater that this study measures.

    The dying-green loop

    Heat and drought kill city trees. Without their shade the streets get hotter, and hotter streets kill more trees.

    The cycle: City trees and parks die → Urban heat islands → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (164 cities worldwide): 76%. of urban tree and shrub species in 164 cities could face temperatures beyond their natural range by 2050, up from 56% today. Risk is highest in cities such as New Delhi and Singapore. Esperon-Rodriguez et al., Nature Climate Change, 2022

    Where it plays out: Hot, fast-growing cities such as New Delhi and Singapore, where every urban tree species studied is at risk from rising heat.

    Where it breaks: Keep city trees alive

    Solutions to try: Street trees and retained mature canopy (protects mature canopy and plants trees that can survive the heat); Community maintenance arrangements backed by public/service support (pays for watering and care through the dry season, when young trees die); Blue-green river corridors and naturalised drainage landscapes (keeps water in the city’s landscape, so trees are not left dry); Permeable pavements retaining rainfall for evaporative surface cooling (lets rain reach tree roots instead of draining away). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Of 3,129 tree and shrub species in 164 cities, 56% already face temperatures beyond those in their natural range, rising to about 76% by 2050 under a mid-range warming scenario. Risk is highest in low-latitude cities such as New Delhi and Singapore. Esperon-Rodriguez et al., Nature Climate Change, 2022
      Peer-reviewed study · 164 cities in 78 countries · 3,129 species. Limit: Compares climate to each species’ natural range; trees can survive outside it with care.
    • Drought stops urban trees from cooling through transpiration. In one Berlin park more than 500 of 870 new trees died within ten years despite watering, and Berlin cut about 44,000 street trees between 2012 and 2019 while planting about 18,000. Hansen et al., chapter 10 in Nature-Based Solutions for Cities, 2023
      Book chapter, peer-reviewed · Berlin, Jakarta, Melbourne and Santiago · Four city cases. Limit: The tree figures are from Berlin; Asian evidence on tree loss in heat is thinner.
      Our reading: We take the lesson that new trees need years of water and care, wherever they are planted.
    • Tree shade sharply lowers the radiant heat people feel by day, which is the cooling lost when trees die. Middel et al., Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society
      Peer-reviewed study · Tempe, United States · Street-level measurements. Limit: A desert city; humid cities gain less from shade alone, though shade still cuts radiant heat.

    The overwhelmed-clinic loop

    Heat illness floods clinics. Overwhelmed clinics treat people later, and more cases become severe.

    The cycle: Heatstroke and heat exhaustion → Health systems overwhelmed → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What breaking it saves (Ahmedabad, India): 1,190. deaths a year were estimated to be avoided after Ahmedabad’s heat action plan prepared hospitals and warned the public. Hess et al., Journal of Environmental and Public Health, 2018

    Where it plays out: District and city hospitals during South Asia’s pre-monsoon heatwaves.

    Where it breaks: Heat action plans, Heat-ready health services

    Solutions to try: Heat-ready clinic protocols and staff capability (prepares staff and protocols to treat heat illness early, before it turns severe); Heat surge staffing, triage and referral coordination (adds staff, triage and referral for heat surges); Resilient power for health-facility cooling and cold-chain loads (keeps the clinic cool and its medicines safe when the power fails); Locally calibrated impact-based heat-warning triggers (warns ahead, so fewer people reach the clinic already severely ill). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Heat-related deaths among people over 65 reached a record 167% above the 1990s level in 2023. Lancet Countdown, The Lancet, 2024
      Peer-reviewed global assessment · Global · Global indicators. Limit: A global figure; it does not give Asia’s share.
    • After Ahmedabad’s heat action plan prepared hospitals and warned the public, an estimated 1,190 deaths a year were avoided. Hess et al., Journal of Environmental and Public Health, 2018
      Peer-reviewed evaluation · Ahmedabad, India · One city, before and after the plan. Limit: A pilot evaluation; other changes over the same years could explain part of the drop.

    Loops that trap people

    The poverty–heat trap

    People who can’t afford to stop working fall ill. Illness costs income, and lost income makes stopping even less affordable.

    The cycle: Informal workers can’t stop work → Heatstroke and heat exhaustion → Lost work hours and wages → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (Bangladesh): US$1.78 billion. Heat-related illness cost Bangladesh 250 million workdays in 2024, about 0.4% of its GDP. World Bank, 2025

    In Southeast Asia (Bangkok, Thailand): 75%. of home-based workers in Bangkok lost at least a day’s work a month to their own or a family member’s heat illness. Nearly 80% of informal workers surveyed cut their hours because of heat. WIEGO and HomeNet Thailand, 2025

    Where it plays out: Construction sites, brick kilns, farms and street markets across South and Southeast Asia.

    Where it breaks: Heat-safe work, Income protection in heatwaves, Social protection floors

    Solutions to try: Paid rest, shade and drinking-water protocols (gives paid rest, shade and water, so stopping does not cost a day’s income); Reschedule work and reduce peak-heat task exposure (moves heavy work away from the hottest hours); Parametric heat insurance for income shocks (pays out when heat crosses a set level, replacing lost income); Scale up existing social protection for heat risk (tops up existing payments in a heatwave, so families are not forced to work through it). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • In a survey of informal workers in Bangkok, nearly 80% had cut their hours because of heat, and 75% of home-based workers lost at least one day of work a month to their own or a family member’s heat illness. WIEGO and HomeNet Thailand, 2025
      Survey · Bangkok, Thailand · Informal workers in one city. Limit: Self-reported, from workers in one city.
    • Across 2.7 million firms in 23 advanced economies, including Japan and Korea, ten extra days above 35 °C cut yearly labour productivity by 0.3%, and over 1.5% above 40 °C. Very humid places lost 0.75%, more than double the average. Korea has already lost about 0.15% of productivity to added heat since the early 2000s. OECD Economics Department, Working Paper No. 1828, 2024
      Working paper · 23 advanced economies, including Japan and Korea · 2.7 million firms. Limit: Rich economies with formal firms; informal work in South and Southeast Asia is not covered.
      Our reading: If formal firms in rich economies lose this much, the losses where work is outdoors and informal are likely larger, but that is not measured here.
    • Up to three-quarters of India’s workforce, about 380 million people, depend on heat-exposed labour, work that produces nearly half of the country’s GDP. World Bank, 2022, reported by Down To Earth
      Agency report, reported by the press · India · National estimate. Limit: A broad estimate of heat-exposed work, not a count of workers harmed.
    • Heat stress is projected to cost 2.2% of all working hours by 2030, equal to 80 million full-time jobs. South Asia is among the worst hit, losing about 5% of working hours. International Labour Organization, 2019
      Agency report, with projections · Global, with regional figures · Global model. Limit: A projection to 2030 at about 1.5 °C of warming.
    • Heat exposure put a record 512 billion potential labour hours at risk in 2023, with potential income losses of US$835 billion. Lancet Countdown, The Lancet, 2024
      Peer-reviewed global assessment · Global · Global indicators. Limit: Potential hours and income at risk, not hours actually lost.
    • Heat-related illness cost Bangladesh 250 million workdays in 2024, worth up to US$1.78 billion, about 0.4% of GDP. Since 1980 the ‘feels-like’ temperature has risen by 4.5 °C. World Bank, 2025
      Agency report · Bangladesh · National survey and model. Limit: Combines survey and modelling; the money value is an upper estimate.

    The medical-debt trap

    Treatment costs push families into debt. Debt removes any option to rest, and working through the heat brings more illness.

    The cycle: Heatstroke and heat exhaustion → Medical costs and debt → Informal workers can’t stop work → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (India): 29 million. Indian households faced catastrophic spending on medicines alone in 2011–12. This is the scale of the trap from all illness, not heat alone. Selvaraj, Farooqui and Karan, BMJ Open, 2018

    In Southeast Asia (Cambodia): US$5,800+. was the average microloan in Cambodia in 2024, over four times the median income per person. This is all borrowing, not heat alone, but it is the debt families draw on when illness or a lost harvest strikes. Human Rights Watch, 2025

    Where it plays out: Households without health insurance, where most medical costs are paid out of pocket.

    Where it breaks: Heat-safe work, Free care for heat illness

    Solutions to try: Reduce financial barriers to heat-related care (removes the cost of heat-related care, so treatment does not create debt); Heat-triggered cash outside an insurance contract (pays cash when heat strikes, so families need not borrow); Scale up existing social protection for heat risk (uses existing social payments to reach families quickly in a heatwave); Community-health-worker heat education and referral (helps people spot heat illness early, before treatment becomes expensive). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Cambodia’s average microloan was over US$5,800 in 2024, more than four times the country’s median annual income per person. Human Rights Watch, 2025
      Human rights investigation · Cambodia · National loan data with interviews. Limit: Shows how heavy debt is; it does not measure the part heat plays.
    • Medicine costs alone were catastrophic for 11.2% of Indian households in 2011–12, about 29 million households. Selvaraj, Farooqui and Karan, BMJ Open, 2018
      Peer-reviewed study · India · National household survey, 2011–12. Limit: Older data on all medicine costs, not heat illness alone.
      Our reading: We read it as the hole a heat illness can fall into; the study does not single out heat.
    • In a survey of more than 2,000 residents of five cities, heat harmed health, cut incomes and working hours, and pushed some households towards high-interest loans. Women and people with disabilities faced the hardest trade-offs between health, work and care. American Red Cross, Red Cross Red Crescent Climate Centre, Nepal Red Cross Society and Bangladesh Red Crescent Society, 2026
      Survey · Five cities in Nepal and Bangladesh · More than 2,000 residents. Limit: Reported experiences; it does not measure how often loans followed heat.

    The energy-poverty trap

    People who can’t afford to cool fall ill. Illness brings debt, and debt puts cooling even further out of reach.

    The cycle: Can’t afford to keep cool → Heatstroke and heat exhaustion → Medical costs and debt → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (World): 1.12 billion. of the world’s rural and urban poor are at high risk because they lack access to cooling. Sustainable Energy for All, 2023

    Where it plays out: Tin-roofed informal settlements in cities such as Dhaka, Karachi and Mumbai.

    Where it breaks: Free care for heat illness, Affordable cooling for the poorest

    Solutions to try: Prevent heat-period electricity/water disconnection for inability to pay (stops electricity and water being cut off in a heatwave because a bill is unpaid); Solar-reflective roof coatings (cools the home without power, at low cost); Efficient BLDC ceiling fans (gives cooling for a fraction of the running cost); On-bill financing and tariff-based cost recovery for efficient cooling (spreads the cost of efficient cooling across bills, so poorer households can afford it). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • 1.12 billion of the rural and urban poor are at high risk because they lack access to cooling. Sustainable Energy for All, 2023
      Agency report · Global, with country estimates · Global model. Limit: Risk of lacking cooling, estimated from income, power and heat data, not a direct survey.

    The displacement loop

    People forced to move often land in heat-trapping housing, fall ill, lose income, fall further behind, and are forced to move again.

    The cycle: Forced migration → Homes that trap heat → Heatstroke and heat exhaustion → Lost work hours and wages → Deepening inequality → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (South Asia): 40 million. people in South Asia could be forced to move within their own countries by 2050 without action, of up to 216 million worldwide. World Bank, 2021

    In Southeast Asia (East Asia and the Pacific): 49 million. people in East Asia and the Pacific could be forced to move within their own countries by 2050 without action. World Bank, 2021

    Where it plays out: Migrant settlements on the edges of fast-growing cities such as Dhaka.

    Where it breaks: Income protection in heatwaves, Cool, affordable housing

    Solutions to try: Independent over-roof shade, including ventilated PV shade (shades the hot tin roofs common in the housing people move into); Public air-conditioned cooling centre or shared refuge (gives a cool place to go when home is too hot); Multilingual heat information and routes for migrant workers (reaches newcomers in their own language, with routes to help); Heat-vulnerability mapping linked to beneficiary targeting (maps who is most exposed, so help reaches new arrivals). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Without action, as many as 49 million people in East Asia and the Pacific could become internal climate migrants by 2050. World Bank, 2021

      Agency report, with projections · Six world regions · Global model. Limit: A worst-case scenario to 2050; covers all climate drivers, not heat alone.

    The lost-schooling loop

    Children pulled out of school earn less as adults, and poorer families pull more children out of school.

    The cycle: Children pulled out of school → Lower skills and earnings → Deepening inequality → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (World): 171 million. students had their schooling disrupted by heatwaves in 2024. South Asia was the most affected region. UNICEF, 2025

    In Southeast Asia (Philippines): 3.6 million. students in the Philippines lost classroom learning in early April 2024, when 5,288 schools switched to remote learning as the heat index reached danger levels. AFP via Malay Mail, April 2024

    Where it plays out: School systems that closed classrooms during the 2024 heatwaves, including in the Philippines and Bangladesh.

    Where it breaks: Keep children in school

    Solutions to try: School heat operating plans, water access and escalation (keeps schools open safely, with water, plans and clear steps to escalate); Closure decisions with offline learning continuity (keeps children learning when schools have to close); Schoolyard greening, depaving and shade as a coordinated retrofit (shades and cools school grounds); Heat-responsive school timetables and activity changes (moves the school day and activities away from peak heat). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Without air conditioning, each 1 °F hotter school year cuts that year’s learning by about 1%, and the losses fall hardest on disadvantaged students. Park, Goodman, Hurwitz and Smith, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2020
      Peer-reviewed study · United States · 10 million students. Limit: US schools and tests; the size of the effect in Asian classrooms is not known.
      Our reading: We use it for direction: hotter school years mean less learning, and the poorest lose most.

      Agency report · Global, by region · Global count from news and official reports. Limit: Counts students affected by closures or shorter days, not learning lost.

      News report · Philippines · 5,288 schools, April 2024. Limit: A snapshot of one week, from official figures quoted in the press.
    • Bangladesh closed schools for about 33 million children in its April 2024 heatwave, and Cambodia cut the school day by two hours. NBC News, 2024
      News report · Bangladesh and Cambodia · National, April 2024. Limit: News figures from official announcements.

    The sleepless-nights loop

    Hot nights wreck sleep. Poor sleep frays mental health, and stress makes sleep harder still.

    The cycle: Sleepless hot nights → Mental health suffers → back to the start. Evidence: well established.

    What this cycle costs (India): 70%. of India’s districts gained five or more very warm nights each summer over the last decade. Mumbai gained 15. Council on Energy, Environment and Water (CEEW), 2025

    In Southeast Asia (Singapore): 76 → most nights. Singapore has averaged 76 warm nights a year, above 26.3 °C, over the last 40 years. By the end of the century such nights could come on most nights of the year. National Environment Agency, Singapore, 2024

    Where it plays out: Tropical cities whose nights stay hot, such as Bangkok, Chennai and Ho Chi Minh City.

    Where it breaks: Cool nights and support

    Solutions to try: Night flushing / night-purge ventilation (lets cool night air flush out the day’s heat); Solar-reflective roof coatings (stops the roof soaking up heat that it gives off at night); Efficient BLDC ceiling fans (gives cheap air movement through the night); Night-time and round-the-clock outreach with heat escalation (checks on people at risk after dark, when hot nights do harm). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

      Government climate study · Singapore · National, with projections. Limit: End-of-century figures depend on the emissions scenario.

    The green-displacement loop

    Green space follows wealth. When greening reaches poorer districts without protection, rents rise, residents are pushed into hotter housing, and the gap grows.

    The cycle: Deepening inequality → Green space follows wealth → Greening pushes people out → Homes that trap heat → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (28 cities in North America and Europe): 17 of 28. cities studied saw greening in the 1990s and 2000s strongly linked to gentrification between 2000 and 2016. Anguelovski et al., Nature Communications, 2022

    Where it plays out: Riverbanks, canals and old inner-city sites turned into parks, as studied in Bangkok and Seoul.

    Where it breaks: Green without pushing people out

    Solutions to try: Greening helps only if residents can stay. Protection for tenants is not yet in this library, so pair these with it. Women-led co-design and worker governance (lets residents shape where greening goes and who it serves); Heat-vulnerability mapping linked to beneficiary targeting (puts shade where heat risk is highest, not where land values are); Community maintenance arrangements backed by public/service support (keeps greening in residents’ hands, with public support). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Across 28 cities in 9 countries, greening in the 1990s and 2000s was strongly linked to gentrification between 2000 and 2016 in 17 of them. Anguelovski et al., Nature Communications, 2022
      Peer-reviewed study · 28 cities in Europe and North America · 28 cities, 2000–2016. Limit: No Asian cities in the sample.
      Our reading: We treat it as a warning for Asia’s greening plans, not proof it happens the same way there.
    • Green space tends to favour wealthier neighbourhoods, and new parks meant to fix that can raise housing costs and displace the residents they were meant to help. Small, local projects are more likely to reach those who need them. Wolch, Byrne and Newell, Landscape and Urban Planning, 2014

    The heat credit squeeze

    Heat empties shops and small firms miss repayments. Banks tighten credit, so firms can’t afford to adapt, and the next heatwave hits harder.

    The cycle: Small businesses close → Loan repayments slip → Borrowing costs rise → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (Delhi, India): 49%. of 721 Delhi street vendors surveyed lost income in the 2024 heatwaves, and 80% saw fewer customers. Greenpeace India and National Hawkers Federation, 2024

    Where it plays out: Market towns and city high streets in heat-exposed districts, and lenders whose loans are collected door to door, as in microfinance across northern and central India.

    Where it breaks: Heat-smart lending

    Solutions to try: Heat-relevant housing and cooling credit with technical appraisal (offers credit for cooling and heat-proofing, with a check that it will work); Public shade structures, sails and market/queue canopies (shades markets and queues, so customers keep coming); Parametric heat insurance for income shocks (pays out when heat crosses a set level, so repayments are not missed); A fallback funding layer when an index misses real harm (covers the harm an index misses, so a missed payout does not become default). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Across 2.7 million firms in 23 advanced economies, heat cut the productivity of small firms while medium and large firms showed no loss, and the largest grew revenue about 0.6 points faster than small firms after hot years. Losses lasted up to two years. Restaurants and hotels were hit hard, partly through lower demand. OECD Economics Department, Working Paper No. 1828, 2024
      Working paper · 23 advanced economies · 2.7 million firms. Limit: Rich economies; small informal firms in Asia are not covered.
    • Loan-level bank data from Mexico show that ten extra days of extreme heat in a quarter raise small-business loan delinquency by about 4%, mainly among firms that depend on local customers. Banks then raise rates, ask for more collateral and lend less for two to three quarters. Aguilar-Gomez et al., summarised in VoxEU, 2024
      Research summary of a peer-reviewed study · Mexico · National bank loan data. Limit: Outside Asia; how Asian banks respond is not yet measured.
      Our reading: We read it as a likely pattern for Asian lenders, to be checked against their own loan books.
    • India’s microfinance industry body said loan quality worsened in April–June 2024, partly because heatwaves cut borrowers’ incomes and lenders’ collections. Sa-Dhan, reported by Business Standard, 2024
      News report of an industry statement · India · National microfinance sector, one quarter. Limit: Heat is one named cause among several, not measured on its own.
    • A caution for lenders: in Cambodia, researchers found that borrowing to get through climate shocks often delayed the harm rather than removing it, leaving households more indebted for the next shock. King’s College London, 2023
      Research summary · Cambodia · Fieldwork with borrowers. Limit: About climate shocks in general, not heat alone.
    • Of 721 Delhi street vendors surveyed in April and May 2024, 80% saw fewer customers during heatwaves and 49% lost income. Greenpeace India and National Hawkers Federation, 2024
      Survey · Delhi, India · 721 street vendors. Limit: Self-reported, from one city in one season.

    The farm-debt loop

    A failed harvest pushes farmers into debt. Debt leaves less for seed, water and fertiliser, so the next harvest fails more easily.

    The cycle: Failing harvests → Farmers fall into debt → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (Philippines): −10%. rice yield for each 1 °C rise in growing-season night temperature, at the International Rice Research Institute’s farm in the Philippines. Peng et al., PNAS, 2004

    Where it plays out: Rain-fed farming districts of India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, where a failed season often means borrowing from moneylenders.

    Where it breaks: Crop insurance and fair credit

    Solutions to try: Shade-net structures for crop microclimate protection (protects crops from heat damage); Organic residue mulch for cooler crop root zones (keeps crop roots cooler and moist); Parametric heat insurance for income shocks (pays out after a heat-hit season, so farmers need not borrow for seed); Pre-agreed heat anticipatory action and forecast finance (releases money before the damage, while it can still protect the harvest). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

      Peer-reviewed study · India · National, 1967–2013. Limit: An association that has been debated; it does not prove debt is the cause.
    • Days above 34 °C make wheat in northern India age early, and crop models may underestimate yield losses at +2 °C by as much as 50%. Lobell, Sibley and Ortiz-Monasterio, Nature Climate Change, 2012
      Peer-reviewed study, satellite data · Northern India · Wheat region, satellite records. Limit: About wheat only; it shows how models can miss losses.

      Peer-reviewed field study · Los Baños, Philippines · One research farm, 1992–2003. Limit: One site; later studies find varied effects across regions.

    Loops inside institutions

    The invisible-deaths loop

    Heat deaths go uncounted, so heat protection goes unfunded, so more people die uncounted.

    The cycle: Heat deaths → Heat deaths under-recorded → Heat protection underfunded → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (India): 48,385 vs 185. suspected heatstroke cases against confirmed heatstroke deaths in India in 2024, up to 28 July: most of the toll goes uncounted. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, reply in India’s Rajya Sabha, 2024

    Where it plays out: Much of Asia, where death certificates rarely record heat as a cause.

    Where it breaks: Count every heat death, Cooling centres and check-ins

    Solutions to try: Timely heat-illness and death surveillance (counts heat illness and deaths as they happen); All-cause and excess-mortality analysis for heat attribution (measures the full toll by comparing deaths with a normal year); Heat-death investigation and consistent certification (records heat as a cause of death consistently); Dedicated and traceable heat operating budgets (turns the counted toll into money set aside for protection). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

      Government data · India · National surveillance. Limit: Counts what gets reported; reporting improves over time, so part of the rise is better counting.
    • In 2020, India’s crime records bureau recorded 530 heatstroke deaths while the disaster agency recorded four. Different systems count differently, which makes the full toll hard to see. Associated Press via PBS NewsHour, 2024
      News report · India · National, two agencies’ records. Limit: Compares two official counts in one year.
    • Death certificates usually record only the immediate cause, such as heart failure, so heat as the trigger goes unrecorded. Down To Earth, 2024

    The reactive-spending loop

    Stretched budgets skimp on prevention. Emergencies multiply, and costly emergency response stretches budgets further.

    The cycle: Public budgets strained → Heat protection underfunded → Emergency services stretched → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What breaking it saves (Developing countries): US$3–16 billion. in losses could be avoided every year by spending US$800 million on early warning systems in developing countries. Global Commission on Adaptation, 2019

    In Southeast Asia (Bangkok, Thailand): Up to 0.7%. of Bangkok’s economy is what heat deaths cost, an estimated 593 a year in 2016–18. The upper estimate is more than the city’s whole public health budget for 2020. Park and Rubinyi, World Bank working paper, 2026

    Where it plays out: Disaster budgets designed for floods and cyclones rather than heat.

    Where it breaks: Dedicated heat budgets, Anticipatory action

    Solutions to try: Dedicated and traceable heat operating budgets (sets aside and tracks money for prevention); Pre-agreed heat anticipatory action and forecast finance (agrees in advance what money is released, and when); Heat action plans with a seasonal responsibility matrix (assigns who does what before each heat season); Recognise heat in disaster-funding rules and eligibility (lets disaster funds be spent on heat). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    The protection-gap loop

    As insurers retreat, public budgets absorb the losses, protection is cut, and the risk drives insurers further away.

    The cycle: Insurance losses and retreat → Public budgets strained → Heat protection underfunded → back to the start. Evidence: emerging (each link is evidenced; the full cycle less so).

    What this cycle costs (Asia): 92%. of Asia’s US$65 billion in natural catastrophe losses in 2025 was uninsured, falling on households, businesses and governments. Swiss Re Institute sigma, reported by Global Reinsurance

    Where it plays out: Fast-growing Asian economies where few households or small businesses carry insurance.

    Where it breaks: Dedicated heat budgets, Shared risk pools

    Solutions to try: Pooled pre-arranged finance for heat action (shares heat risk across places, so no single budget carries it alone); Targeted premium support and renewal finance (helps those most at risk keep their cover); A fallback funding layer when an index misses real harm (backs up index cover when it misses real harm); Recognise heat in disaster-funding rules and eligibility (opens public disaster funds to heat losses). A suggested match, not a measured effect on the cycle. Business ideas for this cycle.

    • Only about 8% of Asia’s US$65 billion in natural catastrophe losses in 2025 was insured. The rest fell on households, businesses and governments. Swiss Re Institute sigma, reported by Global Reinsurance
      Industry research, reported by the press · Asia · Region-wide, 2025. Limit: Covers all natural catastrophes, not heat alone; heat losses are rarely insured at all.

      Industry research report · Asia · Region-wide review. Limit: A qualitative assessment; it does not measure the whole cycle in any one place.
      Our reading: We read it as the cycle on the map: repeated heat losses squeeze incomes, cover becomes harder to afford, and more of the loss falls on households and public budgets.
