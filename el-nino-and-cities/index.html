    Before the season

    El Niño and India's smaller cities: preparing for summer 2027

    A practical guide for municipal teams and community organisations in India's Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. Choose where people already have to be in the hottest hours (a school, a market, a clinic or a waiting space), agree who will keep protection working, and decide what changes when a local warning arrives.

    • For smaller Indian cities
    • Schools, markets, clinics, waiting spaces
    • Printable worksheet

    What a seasonal outlook is for

    A seasonal El Niño outlook gives cities a reason to review preparations early. The local heat season, water supply and places where people spend the hottest hours decide what a city puts in place. District forecasts and official warnings decide when to step up the response.

    The first decision

    Where do people already have to be in the hottest hours, which one place will we protect first, and who has the authority to keep it working?

    One setting, chosen with the people who use it, is enough to start. Getting one change working across several schools or markets may protect more people than starting all four settings at once. The rest of this guide helps a municipal team and a local organisation decide in one meeting, with information they already hold.

    Where people already have to be

    The best place to offer heat respite may be the place someone already has to be.

    A child at the midday meal, a vendor at a stall, a patient in a clinic queue, a passenger at a bus stand: none of them can walk away without losing something. NIUA's Learning Note 1 describes public spaces becoming safe zones during heatwaves (p. 85). Here are four settings where a town can act before the hot season.

    1. 1SchoolsPupils in the midday meal queue, at assembly and in the hottest classrooms.Worked example: a school at lunchtime →
    2. 2Markets and vending streetsVendors at occupied stalls through peak trading hours.Worked example: a market at 2 p.m. →
    3. 3Clinics and health-centre queuesPatients and caregivers waiting outside to be seen.The decision and the check →
    4. 4Public waiting and transit spacesPassengers at bus stands and people in ration-shop queues.The decision and the check →

    These are choices, not a checklist. Pick the one where your town can remove the most avoidable exposure before the first hot spell. The worksheet to print has all four, and room for your own.

    01The evidence

    Where the outlook stands

    A planning horizon, not a forecast. For March to May 2027, NOAA Climate Prediction Center puts the chance of El Niño at 80%, most likely weak (53%) or moderate (25%); for April to June 2027, ENSO-neutral becomes more likely (61%) than El Niño (38%). A hot season can be dangerous with or without El Niño, so what follows is preparation that pays off in either case.

      A forecast for the Pacific Ocean, not for an Indian city. Next issue: 10 December 2026.

      The seasonal average anomaly is forecast at about +3.8 °C, peaking around December 2026; a positive Indian Ocean Dipole is forecast to decline to +0.3 °C. WMO's latest quarterly El Niño Update, of 3 September 2026, gave a nearly 100% likelihood that El Niño persists through February 2027.

      Strength in the Pacific does not decide how severe impacts are in any one country.

    What a seasonal signal can and cannot tell a city

    It can

    Give a reason to start ordinary protection early. In India's records for 1961-2013, most years with above-normal heatwave activity (April to June) followed El Niño events: an India-wide pattern, not a city forecast.

    It cannot

    Give your city's chance of a heatwave, a water shortage or a flood. An ENSO probability describes the Pacific, never a municipality.

    Long-term warming raises the baseline; the local heat island and failing infrastructure (a pump, a feeder, a drain) decide much of the harm, and those are within a city's reach.

    02Four protections

    Four protections that keep working

    Four protections decide much of the harm in a hot season. For each, the same nine questions show whether an arrangement can keep working. The answers below are prompts for a local agreement, not national rules; confirm each role with the body that holds the mandate.

    The question: What has to be working before the hot season, and who keeps it working?

    Workplace protection without income loss

    Who is exposed
    Outdoor and semi-outdoor workers paid by the day or the piece: construction, loading, vending, sanitation, delivery. They lose money if they stop.
    Where they spend the hottest hours
    Worksites, labour chowks where day workers wait for hire, markets and delivery routes, from late morning to late afternoon.
    What can be ready before the season
    Water and shade at worksites and labour chowks; agreed shifted hours for the heaviest work; rest breaks that do not cut pay; someone on each site who knows the signs of heat illness and who to call.
    What activates more action
    The official district heat warning under the state or district heat plan. The seasonal El Niño outlook sets the timetable for preparation, not the day-to-day trigger.
    Who authorises it
    The labour department and the district administration, under the heat plan; each employer or contractor on its own site. In April 2026 the Government issued a nationwide heatwave advisory to safeguard workers, as reported by All India Radio; whether it binds an employer depends on the directions each state issues.
    Who operates it
    Employers and contractors on their sites; the municipal body at labour chowks and public worksites.
    Who pays the recurring costs
    The employer for its own workforce. For own-account and informal workers there is no employer to pay: options include a municipal or state budget line and welfare or insurance schemes that replace lost earnings, which need an agreed payer before the season.
    How workers and communities shape it
    Unions, workers' associations and vendors' committees agree the hours, breaks and water points with employers and the municipality, and report when they fail.
    How to check it is usable
    Is there water at the site at 2 p.m.? Did anyone lose a day's pay for resting? Count breaks actually taken and complaints received, not posters put up.

    Heat-safe homes, including homes used for paid work

    Who is exposed
    Households under tin or asbestos roofs in informal settlements; women who do paid work at home (stitching, food processing, piece-rate work); older people and people who are ill.
    Where they spend the hottest hours
    Inside the home, often a single room or a top floor that stays hot into the night; for a home-based worker, the home is also the workplace.
    What can be ready before the season
    Roof coatings or shading, ventilation and water storage; a list, made with residents, of households that cannot cool their homes; agreed check-in visits for people living alone.
    What activates more action
    The district warning starts check-in visits and longer water-supply hours in the settlement.
    Who authorises it
    The municipal body or housing department for work on roofs and settlement services, with the consent of owners and tenants.
    Who operates it
    Community organisations and municipal staff; local fabricators and painters.
    Who pays the recurring costs
    A coating or shade is a one-off cost; repainting, repairs and water supply recur. Agree who pays them, or the protection fades after one season.
    How workers and communities shape it
    Residents choose which measures suit their homes; women who work at home say which hours and rooms they need to keep working.
    How to check it is usable
    Indoor temperatures in a sample of homes before and after; whether coatings still reflect after a season; whether home-based workers kept their working hours.

    What the evidence says: Mahila Housing Trust's 2024 study in an Ahmedabad informal settlement attributes almost 75% of women's heat-related productivity losses to unpaid labour, including care for others made ill by heat. NIUA notes that informal settlements face higher exposure to heat, flooding and water scarcity.

    Continuity of care, including childcare and maternal services

    Who is exposed
    Pregnant women and newborns, young children, older people and anyone who depends on regular care; and the carers, usually women, who lose earnings when care moves home.
    Where they spend the hottest hours
    Clinic queues and waiting areas, labour rooms, anganwadi and childcare centres, and homes.
    What can be ready before the season
    Shaded, ventilated waiting space and drinking water at clinics; heat checks during antenatal visits; referral routes agreed for warning signs; a plan for childcare when schools or centres change hours.
    What activates more action
    The district warning brings extended or shifted clinic hours, queues moved indoors and calls to people at highest risk.
    Who authorises it
    The district health department, under the state health department's guidance; the department that runs anganwadi and childcare centres.
    Who operates it
    Clinic staff, community health workers and anganwadi workers.
    Who pays the recurring costs
    Health department budgets for clinic measures; staff time, water and cooling recur and need a line in the annual budget.
    How workers and communities shape it
    Women and carers say which hours and places they can actually reach; community health workers report gaps.
    How to check it is usable
    Waiting times in the heat; whether pregnant women received heat checks; appointments missed on hot days; families left without childcare.

    What the evidence says: NCDC's advisory for summer 2026 asked state health departments to report heatstroke cases and deaths daily from 1 March 2026 and to consider public cooling and drinking water facilities. NCDC's guidance on heat and pregnancy notes higher risks for pregnant women and the fetus, and lists checks such as temperature, blood pressure, hydration and fetal heart rate, with timely referral.

    School-based protection and appropriately governed respite

    Who is exposed
    Pupils, especially at assembly and in the midday meal queue; teachers and cooks; parents waiting at the gate.
    Where they spend the hottest hours
    Classrooms under tin roofs, open assembly grounds, the midday meal queue and school transport.
    What can be ready before the season
    Shade and drinking water at assembly and meal points; timetable options agreed in advance; first aid and a named person for heat illness. If a school is also to offer respite to the public, decide it explicitly beforehand.
    What activates more action
    The district warning, through an instruction from the education authority, shifts the timetable or moves activities indoors.
    Who authorises it
    The education authority and school management committee. A school does not automatically become a public shelter: respite for the public needs written permission from the education authority, agreed with the district administration.
    Who operates it
    The head teacher and staff during school hours; for public respite outside school hours, a named operator who is not the teaching staff by default.
    Who pays the recurring costs
    The education budget for school measures; respite operations (staffing, cleaning, water, toilets) need their own budget line.
    How workers and communities shape it
    Parents and pupils, through the school management committee, and neighbours if the school will open to them.
    How to check it is usable
    Water available at lunchtime; pupils taken ill; whether a timetable change reached families in time. For respite: whether it opened when needed, who used it, and whether children stayed safe.
    Before a school offers respite to the public

    Before a school offers respite to the public, settle: permission and liability; safeguarding, with children and adults from outside kept apart; drinking water and toilets for the extra numbers; staff or volunteers on duty and their hours; access for people with disabilities; and what happens if the site floods or loses power. NDMA's guidelines for cooling centres cover site selection in high-risk areas, retrofitting, and operations and maintenance.

    What the evidence says: The Ministry of Education has issued guidelines on precautions schools take against heat waves (as published on Vikaspedia).

    03Your city

    Adapt this to your city

    Cities differ in when heat arrives, how humid it is, where water comes from, which public spaces people rely on and which authority manages them. Find the rows that describe your city; most cities fit more than one. They are illustrative situations, not categories, and a city's size says nothing on its own about its climate risk or its powers.

    Conditions your city may recognise, and the decision each raises
    If this describes your cityThe decision to makeInformation you may already hold

    04The evidence

    Six linked risks

    Each row keeps apart what the evidence shows, where it stops, and what your own city's information can add.

    Two compound scenarios

    • Scenario

      Heat with the taps off

      An afternoon of heat, the ward's supply cut for a repair, the market water point running low by noon. What helps: storage sized to a hot day's demand, a refill arranged with enough lead time for the tanker to arrive, and someone who calls when it runs low.

    • Scenario

      A downpour in a dry year

      A below-normal monsoon, then one night of very heavy rain before the season turns. Covers tear, a lane floods, and a cover that blocks a drain turns one hazard into two. What helps: covers fixed against wind, drains cleared around each site, and an agreed alternative site.

    05Warnings

    Your warning route

    This guide sets no warning thresholds of its own. Find who issues your warning and who acts on it:

    1. IMD district warnings (opens in a new tab)The official warning for your district, issued by the India Meteorological Department.
    2. Your regional or state meteorological centre (opens in a new tab)IMD works through Regional Meteorological Centres, assisted by Meteorological Centres at the state level that disseminate advisories and warnings for their region.
    3. Your state or district disaster authorityThe state and district heat action plans say which department acts at each alert level. Find yours in the India heat action plan archive.
    4. The municipal officer or service operator named locallyThe person who extends water hours, moves a queue indoors or moves a school assembly into the shade. If no one is named for your chosen service, name them before the season.

    06Calendar

    By lead time: months, weeks, days, during, afterwards

    Read each window against your city's own season, not a national date. El Niño is context for the months-ahead work; local forecasts and official warnings set the days-ahead action.

    Five decision windows, by lead time
    WindowWhat happensWho decides
    Months aheadSelect the service and sites, confirm permissions, budgets and recurring costs, procure what has to be bought, and sign service agreements with whoever will operate and repair. Work backwards from when heat usually arrives in your city and how long procurement takes. The seasonal El Niño outlook is a reason to start early; it is not the trigger.The body that manages each site (municipal body, education authority or school management, market committee, health department or transport authority), the operator and the local organisation.
    Weeks aheadCheck that each measure works: water flows, shade is up, keys are with the right person, staff know their hours. Read the national seasonal and extended-range outlooks for your region, and confirm who receives the official warning.Each site's operator, with the local organisation checking from the user's side.
    Days ahead: an official local warningThe core protection is already working. Agree in advance who receives the warning and what is added under the applicable plan: an operator extends water hours, brings a refill forward, moves a clinic queue indoors or shifts a school timetable.IMD issues district warnings; departments act under the state and district heat plans.
    During an eventKeep each service running at the hours people rely on it: refill water, staff the site, keep cooling and care open during power cuts. Check on people who cannot come to a site.Each site's operator and the named repair officer, with the local organisation reporting what users see.
    AfterwardsReview who could not get water, shade, cooling or care, which measures failed and how fast they were fixed, and what it cost. After heavy rain, check covers and drains before reopening.     Before opening a site, check how people will get drinking water during a power cut.     07Worked example

    A school at lunchtime

    Illustrative scene

    Children queue for the midday meal in an open courtyard, in full sun. Along one side, a veranda is in shade at the time the meal is served. Could the school move the serving point to the veranda, keeping the route step-free and leaving kitchen staff room to serve the children quickly?

    Nothing had to be built to see this. Watching one ordinary part of the school day, at the hour it happens, changed the proposal: from new shade over the whole courtyard to moving where the meal is served.

    How a municipal team turns that observation into something it can deliver:

    1. 1Choose the right exposure

      The meal queue is one exposure. Where a classroom is the main problem, there are alternatives: move the hottest lessons to a cooler room that is free at that time, shade the sun-facing windows, or treat a suitable roof. Match the choice to the building, the hours the room is used and the permissions available.

      Cool the hottest classrooms first · Shaded play space

    2. 2Give people a real design decision

      • Childrencan point out where they stand in the sun.
      • Meal staffknow where they can serve safely and quickly.
      • Teachersknow which rooms are occupied during the hottest lessons.
      • The headteacher and the School Management Committeecan help choose a change that works in this school.
      • The education authority or building ownerapproves changes to the building and maintains them. Who that is varies by town, so confirm it before the plan is agreed.

    3. 3Use the school calendar

      Ask which schools are open, on which dates and at what hours during the hot weeks. Count reach only for the days children are present. Opening a school as neighbourhood respite during the holidays is a separate decision, needing permission, staff, drinking water, a step-free entrance and safeguarding.

    4. 4What changes on a heat-warning day

      Something within the school's own authority: hold the morning assembly in the shaded area instead of the open yard, and check that the drinking-water point works before pupils arrive. Changes to school hours or closures follow the education department and the state or district heat action plan. A shorter school day also moves care to families and can cost a parent paid work, so changes are easier to carry when agreed with families early.

      Find your state's and district's heat action plan · Water, and time to drink it · Heat-ready timetables

    5. 5Check the result

      For the meal queue, time how long pupils wait in direct sun during comparable lunch periods, before and after the change. For a roof or a classroom change, compare temperatures in occupied rooms at similar times of day and in comparable weather; NIUA's Table 2 pairs roofs treated with lower indoor temperatures (Learning Note 2, p. 54). Claims about attendance, learning or illness need evidence of their own.

    The question for the education team

    Which schools share this problem, are open during the relevant weeks, and could have the change in place before the first hot spell?

    Worked example: a market at 2 p.m.

    Illustrative design

    A vegetable seller would lose sales if she walked to a distant cooling room. So protection goes to her stall, in the hours she trades.

    The local organisation's design role

    Vendors, through their association or a local organisation walk the market at 2 p.m. and mark which occupied stalls lose shade in the afternoon. Their map can move the shade: from the entrance, where it looks visible, to the aisle where sellers actually sit after lunch.

    Four roles, which can sit with different bodies

    • Authorises the siteWhoever manages the market: a municipal body, a market committee or another authority. Gives permission for shade and a water point.
    • Pays for installationMay be the municipal budget, a state scheme or a district allocation; agreed before procurement starts.
    • Covers summer operationsWater, cleaning and small repairs through the season; may be a different body from the one that paid to install.
    • Authorises a repair or emergency refillOne named person who can order a tanker or fix a torn cover the same day.

    On a warning day

    Water hours extend and the refill comes forward, timed to the storage on site and the tanker's delivery time; the association checks the water point at noon and mid-afternoon.

    One trade-off, and the check

    A badly sited cover can trap heat, pour rain onto the walkway or block a wheelchair or handcart. The check: stand under it at 2 p.m., pour a bucket on it, walk the aisle with a handcart.

    Failure reportsThe association or local organisationOne number that answers; the operator records when each failure was fixed

    09Reach

    Reach: how much protection people got

    Start with one setting and show how much of it can be protected this season. A change working across many schools or markets is what reaches a city: NIUA's Learning Note 2 describes city-scale resilience planning as a coordinated approach across a city's systems, moving beyond isolated projects (section 2.6, p. 40), and Learning Note 1 points to entry points within municipal functions.

    • Service coverageFunctioning sites out of eligible sites, always with the eligible count stated: say 6 of 40 schools open during the hot weeks (an illustrative figure).
    • People reachedRegular users counted once, and repeated visits counted separately: a pupil present on 30 school days is one pupil and 30 school-day visits. Footfall is not protection.
    • Protection deliveredWhat was available at the hour that matters, and to whom. Claim no avoided deaths or illness without health data and a fair comparison.
    Reach, setting by setting: state the denominator, and keep people apart from visits
    SettingCoverage, with its denominatorPeople and visits, kept apart
    SchoolsParticipating schools with the change working, out of eligible schools open during the hot weeksPupils reached, each counted once, and school-day visits (pupils times days present), reported apart. Count only days children are present.
    Markets and vending streetsMarkets, or occupied stalls, shaded and watered at peak trading hours, out of those trading through the hottest hoursRegular vendors counted once; shopper footfall reported apart, as visits.
    Clinics and health-centre queuesClinics with shaded or indoor waiting, out of clinics where patients queue outsidePatient visits are visits: one person may come several times. Count caregivers who wait too.
    Public waiting and transit spacesStops or queues shaded where people actually wait, out of those with peak-hour queuesBoardings and ration collections are trips, not people; report them as visits.

    Pair each output with an observable result (after NIUA's Table 2, Learning Note 2, p. 54):

    • Output: Shade installed→Observable result: Occupied vendor stalls shaded at 2 p.m.
    • Output: Water point installed→Observable result: Drinking water available during peak trading hours
    • Output: Cooling space opened→Observable result: People using it at peak hours, with staff on duty
    • Output: Meal served from the shaded veranda→Observable result: Minutes pupils wait in direct sun at lunch, on comparable days
    • Output: Classroom roof treated→Observable result: Lower temperature in occupied rooms at similar times and in comparable weather
    • Output: Clinic waiting moved into shade→Observable result: Share of the wait still spent in direct sun
    • Output: Bus-stand shelter installed→Observable result: Waiting line shaded at the peak hour, with the arriving bus in view

    Give users a simple way to report a failure (a number on the post, the association's contact) and ask the operator to record when it was fixed. The log shows whether protection held, not only whether it was built.

    Exposure is uneven: NIUA's Learning Note 1 notes that informal settlements face disproportionately higher exposure to heat, flooding and water scarcity, and that smaller cities often work with constrained resources (pp. 33, 35). Count who the service reaches in those places.

    11In practice

    Preparedness that has happened in India

    A published plan shows a planning decision, an installed facility shows installation, and measured benefit needs outcome evidence; each example says which it is, and cites its own source.

    • Installed, reported by a partner in the plan

      Jodhpur, Rajasthan Hot, dry

      The decision: where to put protection. In April 2024 the city corporation and its partners opened a net-zero cooling station in a heat-vulnerable ward of Jodhpur North, chosen through the vulnerability assessment in the city's heat action plan. The 2023 plan also sets out passing heat warnings by SMS and WhatsApp.

    • An allocation, reported in the news

      Kerala Humid, coastal

      The decision: who can pay at short notice. In March 2026 the state decided to give each district collector ₹1 crore for summer measures, and asked officials to set up drinking water kiosks where people gather: markets, junctions and bus stands. The government order itself was not found online.

    • A state method, with district thresholds reported

      Uttar Pradesh Northern plains

      The decision: what triggers a step-up. The state disaster management authority set yellow, orange and red alert temperatures for each of its 75 districts, from percentiles of each district's own temperature record, so an alert reflects local conditions. Its 2024 heat plan sets out the method, first drafted for Agra.

    A large city, with outcome evidence Ahmedabad. A 2018 evaluation estimated about 1,190 deaths a year avoided after Ahmedabad's heat action plan was fully implemented (95% confidence interval 162 to 2,218): an association, not proof of cause. The scale differs; the practice a smaller city can adapt is a named plan in which a warning sets agreed actions moving across departments. Heat action plans, with Ahmedabad's.
