## How this route reads heat risk

Risk comes from several things at once. Some belong to the body; most belong to the conditions people live and work in, and those can change.

- **Environmental exposure**Social and working conditions
- **Workload and activity**Social and working conditions
- **Physiological susceptibility**The body
- **Housing and infrastructure**Social and working conditions
- **Income and employment arrangements**Social and working conditions
- **Care responsibilities**Social and working conditions
- **Access to services**Social and working conditions
- **Decision-making power**Social and working conditions
- **The ability to recover**The body and conditions

Women are not one group, and nobody is helpless. Gender shapes who cooks, who cares, who controls spending and who is listened to, and it does so differently by place, class, caste, age, disability, migration, tenure and work. Some risks fall harder on men in some jobs. Transgender and gender-diverse people can face particular barriers to services and toilets. Situations on these pages are illustrative unless a source documents them, and none assigns anyone a risk score.

## The shared provisions

The plan workbook, the Solutions library, the heat action plan review, funding records and Signals use the same words. Three things are kept apart: **who and where**, **what is provided**, and **how far it is shown**. A record that mentions a group does not provide anything for it; a provision on paper is not delivery; a service open to everyone is not equal access.

What is provided (10)

**Earnings protection**: People do not lose pay or work by using the protection: paid breaks, income support or cash in heat. Counts when: The arrangement itself pays, compensates or protects earnings. Does not count: Saying earnings matter, or that income loss is a risk.

**Work-rest coverage**: Rest, changed hours or lighter loads in heat, and who they cover. Counts when: The arrangement sets rest, schedules or workload in heat. Does not count: General advice to rest.

**Water, sanitation and privacy**: Drinking water, toilets and private, safe facilities people can actually use. Counts when: The arrangement provides water, toilets, washing or privacy. Does not count: Advice to drink water without provision.

**Accessible services and cooling**: Cooling or support that disabled, older and other intended users can reach and use: step-free, nearby, open when needed, with dependants welcome. Counts when: The arrangement addresses physical access, distance, opening hours or transport. Does not count: A service existing somewhere.

**Care-service continuity**: Childcare, eldercare, disability support and health services keep running through heat, without moving the work onto unpaid carers. Counts when: The arrangement keeps a care or health service running in heat. Does not count: A health service that exists.

**Tenant protections**: People who rent can benefit without rent rises, eviction or needing a landlord's permission they cannot get. Counts when: The arrangement addresses rent, tenure or landlord permission. Does not count: Housing improvements with no word on tenure.

**Confidential, non-punitive reporting**: People can report symptoms or barriers privately, without losing pay or work, and someone responds. Counts when: The arrangement sets a reporting channel with protection from retaliation or a named responder. Does not count: Encouraging people to speak up.

**Participation in decisions**: Intended users can shape or challenge how the protection works: committees with real say, worker or community voice in budgets and rules. Counts when: The arrangement gives users a role in decisions. Does not count: Consultation alone, or a seat without a say.

**Operational funding**: Money for running, staffing and maintaining the protection, not only for building it. Counts when: The arrangement names recurring or operating funds. Does not count: Capital costs alone.

**Maternal and newborn provisions**: Arrangements that address pregnancy, postpartum and newborn needs: accommodation at work, antenatal care access, facility readiness. Counts when: The arrangement provides something for pregnant or postpartum people or newborns. Does not count: Naming pregnant people as at risk.

Who and where (10)

**Pregnant and postpartum people**: People who are pregnant or have recently given birth.

**Newborns and children**: Babies, children and young people, at home, in school or in care.

**Older people**: People in later life, including those living alone.

**Disabled people**: People with physical, sensory, intellectual or psychosocial disabilities.

**Informal, contract and piece-rate workers**: People paid by the day, by the piece or without a formal contract.

**Home-based workers**: People who earn from work done in their own home.

**Tenants**: People renting their home, who may not be able to change the building.

**Migrant workers**: People working away from where they are from, within or across borders.

**Paid and unpaid caregivers**: People who care for children, older or disabled people, paid or unpaid.

**People facing gender-related exclusion**: Women, transgender and gender-diverse people where gender shapes access, safety or control.

How far it is shown (7)

**Explicitly documented**: The record or document states the provision as part of the arrangement, with a quoted passage.

**Partly documented or general mention**: The issue is named, but without a concrete action, a responsible body or resources.

**Not found in reviewed material**: The material reviewed was read for this and did not contain it. Says nothing about practice or other documents.

**Not assessed**: Nobody has reviewed this yet. Not a negative finding.

**Document inaccessible or unreadable**: The document could not be opened or its text could not be read reliably.

**Reported implemented**: The implementer or a news source reports delivery; not independently evaluated.

**Outcome evaluated**: An independent evaluation reports effects; see its scope and limits.

Add the equity and accommodation check to your heat plan, and plan for service continuity. [Plan a response, step 6](https://beattheheat.asia/plan/#plan-step-6) · [When a service closes](https://beattheheat.asia/care-through-heat/#when-a-service-closes)

### Researchers

Each flagship page ends with what is known, tested and uncertain, and the next useful question. [Documented provisions in heat plans](https://beattheheat.asia/resources/heat-action-plans/india/#provisions) · [Evidence map](https://beattheheat.asia/evidence-map/)

### Funders

Ask of any proposal: what practical change it supports, which institution delivers it, what recurring spending it needs, what evidence it will generate, and who among the intended users shapes the decision. [Health and equity reach in tracked funding](https://beattheheat.asia/heat-economy/money/#equity-reach)

## Questions worth studying

- Do paid breaks improve uptake of heat protection without reducing earnings?
- Do housing improvements support recovery as well as reduce indoor temperature?
- Do service-continuity arrangements reduce care burdens without transferring unpaid work?
- Does participation change budgets and operating rules?
- Which groups remain excluded after nominal coverage expands?
