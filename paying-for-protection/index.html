Paying for heat protection: who pays, how money arrives, and whether it lasts

Protection only works if someone pays for it, the money arrives in time, and it is still there next season. Start from what needs paying for, not from a product.

A way to compare routes, not a claim that every route exists in every country.

Three triggers, not one

  • Warning trigger: Prompts an official heat advisory or warning. Usually the weather service, often with health authorities.
  • Action trigger: Switches on agreed measures: outreach, rest breaks, cooling spaces open. The heat plan’s lead office and each department.
  • Payout trigger: Decides whether a specific insurance contract pays. Set in the contract; the insurer or a calculation agent confirms it.

The three need not match. Protective action should never wait for an insurance payout.

Why can someone lose income to heat and get no payout?

  • Wrong place. The contract measures heat at a weather station or grid point, but the workplace or home is hotter.
  • Wrong measure. The index counts temperature, but humidity, heavy work or hot nights caused the harm.
  • Wrong amount. It pays, but a fixed amount that covers only part of what was lost.

If the contract does not pay, what support is still there? Clear explanation prevents disappointment when a contract works as designed. It cannot fix a contract that leaves real losses uncovered. Global Asia Insurance Partnership, Scaling Parametric Insurance in Asia, 2026, pp. 48–51

From trigger to protection

  1. Trigger confirmed
  2. Fund holder paid
  3. People receive support they can use
  4. Protective action becomes possible

When money goes first to a government, cooperative or microfinance institution, someone still has to decide who gets it. Count the date at every step, not only the day the insurer pays. Global Asia Insurance Partnership, Scaling Parametric Insurance in Asia, 2026, pp. 52–53

Will it survive the next season?

  • Who pays next year’s premium and running costs?
  • What happens when donor money ends?
  • Who keeps the knowledge when staff change?
  • How will you review seasons with a payout, and without one?
  • What evidence would make you change it, or stop it?

When heat reaches lenders

Heat does not stop at the household. When people earn less in a heatwave, loan repayments slow, and the strain travels up to microfinance institutions and banks. A 2026 commentary sets out how, and what could help. Watch the 1:34 narrated film (transcript: captions).

  • Heatwaves in 2025 reduced how much lenders collected in the June quarter across loan books that depend on field visits, with a similar squeeze projected for 2026 in northern and central India.
  • About 60% of India’s ₹3.81 trillion microfinance portfolio is in agriculture and allied activities, and eastern India, among the most climate-vulnerable regions, holds roughly a third of it.
  • The borrowers most exposed (microfinance clients, farming households, gold-loan borrowers and self-employed informal workers) are also the most financially vulnerable, because their income depends on outdoor work and moving around.
  • When collections slow, lenders face a cash crunch of their own and turn to costlier short-term funding; branch closures and fewer field visits in hot zones add to the strain.
  • The Reserve Bank of India now treats heatwaves as a prudential risk, and draft climate-risk disclosure directions ask banks to map and stress-test heat exposure, though compliance remains voluntary for some.
  • At least one large public-sector bank now counts heat among 13 key climate hazards and is building sector-by-sector vulnerability tools.

What could help, in combination:

  • Parametric insurance: Payouts that arrive automatically when heat crosses a set threshold, so informal workers and smallholders have a buffer against lost income. SEWA’s heat cover for women informal workers is the leading Indian example.
  • Blended finance: Concessional or patient capital that takes early risk so commercial money can follow, for example into near-farm storage that cuts heat spoilage and lets farmers avoid distress sales.
  • Green and resilience bonds: Large, long-term capital for resilient public buildings, water and urban infrastructure; Indonesia’s sovereign Green Sukuk has raised over US$10 billion since 2018 for climate-resilient projects.
  • Shared heat-risk data: Standard, location-specific heat-risk information, such as the RBI’s Climate Risk Information System, so lenders can assess exposure without each building their own.
  • Lending tied to heat outcomes: Loan terms linked to measurable results such as lower indoor temperatures, lower peak power demand or more cool-roof coverage, rather than broad ESG scores.
  • City-level financing platforms: Pooling many small projects, such as cool roofs and efficient cooling for public buildings, into portfolios that banks and investors can finance, as municipal rooftop-solar bonds have done in Surat and New Delhi.

The commentary’s central point: the financial system needs to move from financing vulnerability to financing resilience.

What has been tried

Heat income cover for women informal workers

Gujarat and north-western India · Extreme heat

  • Who paid: Premiums were paid largely by Climate Resilience for All, with members contributing; reported amounts differ by source and year.
  • Who received it: Members of the Self Employed Women’s Association (SEWA). Swiss Re reported more than 46,000 women paid after the May 2024 heatwaves.
  • What it made possible: Cash for lost working days, alongside early warnings and low-cost protection such as shade tarps and cooling boxes.
  • What it left out: The 2023 pilot’s threshold was set too high, so women got nothing on very hot days until it was lowered. In 2025 most payments were reported to take six to eight weeks.
  • Did it continue? Grew from about 21,000 women in the 2023 pilot to 50,000 by 2025. No independent evaluation found.
  • Lesson: Insurance worked as one part of a package. The threshold and the payment speed decide whether money helps when it is needed.

Sources: Global Asia Insurance Partnership, Physical risks on the insurance industry, 2026, p. 13 · Swiss Re: the structure behind a parametric solution for SEWA · NPR, 16 July 2025: on super hot days, this insurance plan pays out cash for lost wages

Government cover that switched to its own disaster data

Lao PDR · Floods and other disasters, not heat

  • Who paid: The Government of Lao PDR, through the Southeast Asia Disaster Risk Insurance Facility (SEADRIF).
  • Who received it: The government, for emergency response and budget planning.
  • What it made possible: The first policy paid on a modelled count of people affected, which kept disagreeing with the government’s own figures. The renewed 2025–2027 policy pays on the national disaster office’s reported impact data, up to US$8 million a year. A US$2 million payout was made in September 2025, six days after the figures were finalised.
  • What it left out: The report does not describe how the money then reached affected families.
  • Did it continue? Renewed for 2025–2027 after the redesign.
  • Lesson: Trust grows when the trigger uses data the government already relies on to run its response. A lesson about data, not heat evidence.

Sources: Global Asia Insurance Partnership, Scaling Parametric Insurance in Asia, 2026, p. 51 · SEADRIF, 2025: lessons from Laos

Pay for days too dangerous to work

Philippines, 24 coastal municipalities · Wind, waves and rain, not heat

  • Who paid: The Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources pays the premiums from its budget, as the policyholder.
  • Who received it: About 14,200 registered small-scale fishers, up to US$100 per policy cycle.
  • What it made possible: Income when weather makes fishing unsafe: it insures lost working days, not damaged boats.
  • What it left out: Thresholds are not public, and results have not yet been reported; it launched in November 2025.
  • Did it continue? A government-supported pilot.
  • Lesson: The same logic heat income cover needs: pay for days too dangerous to work, with a public agency as policyholder so people who would never buy cover are reached.

Sources: Global Asia Insurance Partnership, Scaling Parametric Insurance in Asia, 2026, p. 50 · WTW, November 2025: Willis and Rare launch insurance for fishers in the Philippines

What this evidence can and can’t tell you

These reports describe how insurance is regulated, designed and delivered in Asia. The parametric report did not assess consumer outcomes, pricing, contracts or how triggers performed, so it supports design questions, not proof that a heat product works.
