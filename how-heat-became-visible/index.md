# How heat became visible

When science, health, labour, finance and the UN began to notice extreme heat, and who was still missing each time. 70 events from the record, each graded by how well its sources support it.

- Grades describe the evidence for what happened. Why each event mattered is historical interpretation, not a measured effect.
- A claimed first is shown as claimed, with its source; no first use of an IPCC heat term is asserted.
- Absence from this timeline is not evidence of inaction. Country coverage is uneven.
- Research cut-off: 3 November 2026. [What this page could not verify](#limits).

## The story in eras

- Before 1988

### Heat was measurable, but institutionally scattered

Urban temperature differences had a long scientific history. This review reaches an 1833 observation through a later EPA history, rather than through the original publication.

Sources (1)

- [Radiative Effects of Pollutants on the Planetary Boundary Layer](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=91008J1A.TXT) US EPA, 1976 [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250415133032/https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=91008J1A.TXT)

- 1988-2001

### Climate assessment gave heat a recurring policy route

The IPCC's early impacts assessments connected heat with health. By TAR, the policy summary linked heatwaves with mortality, crops, electricity and unequal vulnerability. This was recognition, rather than proof of sustained public attention.

- 1995-2010

### Urban mortality made heat a public-health emergency

Chicago, Europe and Russia became reference cases for surveillance, warnings and organised prevention. The term silent describes a recognition problem: deaths can be dispersed, delayed or attributed to other conditions. It does not mean communities were unaware of heat.

- 2010-2017

### South Asian experience entered both practice and science

Ahmedabad connected a deadly event with a municipal plan. Karachi followed a major emergency with a management plan. India circulated national guidance. Humid-heat research placed the Indus and Ganges agricultural regions within debates about adaptation limits.

- 2011-2019

### Heat became a connected disaster and development risk

SREX connected extremes with vulnerability. GHHIN built a dedicated exchange network. The Lancet Countdown and ILO made heat exposure and lost work capacity recurring indicators. These metrics widened the conversation beyond emergency deaths.

- 2018-2022

### Warming levels, attribution and physiology sharpened the stakes

SR1.5 connected temperature goals with exposure differences. Observations and experiments complicated the 35°C wet-bulb shorthand. AR6 strengthened the assessment of observed hot extremes. A 2022 India-Pakistan attribution study reached international audiences through WMO.

- 2023-2024

### Institutions began naming protection functions

Examples include a heat coordination role in Dhaka North, operational workplace measures in Singapore, insurance coverage for SEWA members, and Bangladesh's clinical guidance. COP28 and the 2024 UN call gave health and extreme heat greater international visibility. Announcements, coverage and rules are different forms of progress.

## Turning points, and who was still missing

- 1995-2008

### Mortality emergencies became a prevention field

Chicago supplied a sharply visible event and a surveillance problem. Europe supplied another large emergency. Health agencies carried lessons into operational guidance as repeated events made prevention harder to treat as a one-off response.

**Who was still missing**Homebound and socially isolated older people were difficult to reach. Death certification could miss indirect effects. People with disabilities and informal workers were not consistently identifiable in the records used here. The evidence base was weighted towards well-documented northern cities.

- 2010-2017

### South Asian municipal experience became transferable practice

Ahmedabad's emergency was followed by a plan with a designated municipal health role, forecast links and public communication. Karachi's emergency led to clearer inter-agency responsibilities. Municipal bodies, health services, meteorological institutions and partners carried the work. The timing connected recent loss with the next hot season.

**Who was still missing**A plan's publication did not establish who could afford to stop work, reach cool space or obtain care. Small cities, informal settlements and mobile workers remain less visible in this source set. Evaluation and continuity cannot be inferred from launch dates.

- 2010-2021

### Humid heat changed the language of limits

Theory made heat plus humidity a physiological question. South Asian projections connected it to densely populated agricultural areas. Observations and laboratory experiments then narrowed the distance between future risk and real exposure. Research institutions carried this sequence through peer-reviewed evidence.

**Who was still missing**A theoretical threshold did not represent the safety of older people, children, pregnant workers, people with chronic illness or workers in direct sun. Young healthy adults in controlled experiments do not represent every person or workplace. Tropical night heat and recovery conditions need separate attention.

- 2018-2022

### Heat became a recurring health and labour account

Annual Countdown indicators, ILO projections and AR6 made exposure and work losses comparable across reporting cycles. Health researchers, labour institutions and the IPCC carried complementary evidence. This coincided with growing demand for development-relevant climate metrics.

**Who was still missing**Modelled work capacity is not identical to observed wages, household income or lost employment. Unpaid care, piece-rate work and the ability to take a break are not captured by one aggregate working-hours number. Women's exposure can remain hidden within household and informal activity.

- 2022-2024

### Named climate risk gained named institutional roles

Rapid attribution connected an India-Pakistan event with climate change while the emergency remained current. Dhaka North named a coordination role; Singapore specified workplace triggers; Bangladesh issued health guidance. COP28 and the UN call carried the issue into broader policy audiences.

**Who was still missing**A visible role or declaration does not demonstrate staffing, budgets, enforcement or coverage. Outdoor and informal workers, children outside formal care, and people with disabilities may need delivery channels beyond alerts. Asia-wide first claims remain bounded partner claims.

## What this page could not verify

17 open questions, from early IPCC wording to media attention

- **Full early IPCC heat audit.** Repeated access failures affected original FAR, AR4, SREX, SR1.5 and SROCC pages or PDFs. Official mirrors and summaries supported narrower entries. They did not establish every chapter, first term or confidence statement. The older special reports listed in C were identified through an opened bibliography, but their heat passages were not audited.
- **All working groups in every cycle.** Direct text was not obtained for every WGI, WGII, WGIII and synthesis contribution. In particular, FAR's original heat content, original AR4 heat passages, AR5 WGIII, and detailed AR6 WGII and Synthesis heat-health text remain unresolved. The table leaves these gaps visible.
- **First IPCC use of heatwave, humid heat or wet-bulb.** This requires a searchable full corpus and term-by-term checks, including spelling variants. No first-use date is claimed.
- **Early heat-health records beyond urban climatology.** The 1833 entry rests on an EPA historical account. The original was not read. Earlier military, occupational and medical heat records were not verified in primary texts.
- **2004 heatwave event-attribution milestone.** The original landmark European attribution paper could not be confirmed in accessible full text in this review. No date, risk ratio or first claim was entered. The verified 2022 WMO account is not presented as the beginning of attribution science.
- **Full mortality series for landmark events.** Final totals for Europe 2003, Russia 2010 and the combined 2015 South Asian emergencies were not reconciled across studies and official records. Those totals were omitted. Chicago's 465 figure is the opened CDC report's certified-death count and period, not a definitive final excess-mortality estimate.
- **Canadian aftermath of the 2021 northwestern heatwave.** The opened source supports US surveillance. A verified history of subsequent Canadian warning, building or health-policy changes was not completed.
- **Every country named in the scope.** Dedicated plan, programme or first-institution milestones were not verified for Maldives, Myanmar, Thailand, Viet Nam, Cambodia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines or Timor-Leste. Bhutan appears as a regional report host, rather than through a verified domestic heat-plan milestone. Sri Lanka appears through a regional workshop. These are research gaps, not claims of absence.
- **Independent firsts and institutional continuity.** The first-plan claim for Ahmedabad, first-in-Asia claim for Dhaka North's heat officer, and early insurance claims were not independently audited across all jurisdictions. Current staffing, budgets and continued operation were not established by the historical launch sources. No first national heat plan across Asia is asserted.
- **Plan effectiveness.** A primary evaluation of Ahmedabad's mortality effects was not opened and checked. Similar outcome evidence for other listed plans was not verified. Timeline launches are not treated as proven reductions in illness or deaths.
- **Heat-specific adaptation finance totals.** A consistent global or regional spending series separating heat from cooling, health, cities and wider adaptation was not verified. No estimate of a heat-finance gap is supplied.
- **Philanthropic heat-fund outcomes.** Announcements and specific support are visible in opened sources, but total disbursement, additionality and reach were not reconciled. A reported COP30 health-funding commitment was not entered because its underlying announcement could not be opened and confirmed.
- **Insurance outcomes.** The opened project record establishes first-year coverage, not verified claim-level payouts, basis risk, sustained affordability or health effects. Later payout figures were not confirmed in primary records.
- **Media and public attention.** No comparable, reproducible South and Southeast Asian media series, Google Trends extract or multilingual coverage study was opened and verified. A study of research publications could not be confirmed either. No media-attention peak is dated here.
- **UNDRR hazard definitions and all database revisions.** The opened loss-statistics account and CRED report establish recording limits and delays. A version-by-version audit of heat classification, event thresholds and retrospective revisions across EM-DAT, DesInventar and national systems was not completed. Certified heat deaths, excess deaths and modelled attributable mortality remain separate measures.
- **Complete WMO State of the Climate chronology.** The 2026 Asian release was read. The earliest global or regional report to give heat a headline role was not established.
- **AR7 beyond the verified review notice.** The Cities report review is a process event. Its draft findings were not treated as approved evidence. Other AR7 material through the cut-off was not fully audited. No future report or COP outcome is included as completed.
